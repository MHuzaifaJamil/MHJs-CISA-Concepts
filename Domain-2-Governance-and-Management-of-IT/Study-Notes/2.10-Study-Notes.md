## 2.10 Overview: why measure IT performance

### The basic idea
- Organizations spend heavily on IT and expect visible returns.
- Stakeholders need assurance that IT investments are aligned and well managed.
- Stakeholders include the board, executives, customers, regulators and employees.
- Example: a board asks whether a new ERP spend actually improved order processing.

### Statutory or contractual measurement
- Definition: a legal or contract duty to measure and report IT performance.
- Use: proves compliance with laws, outsourcing contracts and service agreements.
- Audit impact: auditors check that required reports exist, are accurate and are timely.
- Example: an outsourcing contract requires monthly uptime reports from the vendor.

### Outcome measures
- Definition: results-based measures showing what IT delivered to the business.
- Use: show business value, competitive advantage and how well IT performs.
- Audit impact: auditors confirm measures tie to business goals, not just technical activity.
- Defined performance metrics are part of appropriate measurement practice.

### Incentives and sharing results
- Definition: incentives are rewards, pay or recognition linked to performance measures.
- Use: motivates staff to pursue the outcomes the organization actually wants.
- Audit impact: badly chosen measures can drive gaming of results.
- Example: bonuses tied to ticket closure counts may encourage premature closures.
- Results and progress should be shared with employees, customers and stakeholders.
- Sharing builds trust and supports transparency.

### The monitoring process
- Definition: ongoing checking that projects and services follow set direction and policy.
- Use: ensures appropriate actions are taken when performance drifts.
- Monitoring includes defining KPIs, KRIs and KCIs.
- It also includes systematic, timely performance reporting.
- It also includes prompt action when deviations are found.
- Audit impact: reporting without action on deviations is a monitoring weakness.

### Metric baseline and threshold
- Definition: a baseline is the measured starting level of a metric.
- Definition: a threshold is the level that signals success or failure.
- Use: lets management judge achievements against goals.
- Use: also evaluates effectiveness and efficiency of business processes.
- Audit impact: a metric lacking a baseline or threshold cannot support conclusions.
- Example: help-desk resolution time baseline is 8 hours; threshold is 6 hours.
- Exam traps:
  - A metric is meaningful only when compared against a baseline or target.
  - Raw numbers with no threshold do not show success or failure.

## 2.10.1 Key Performance Indicators (KPI) 📓

### KPI basics
- Definition: a measure of how well a process enables a goal.
- Use: monitors whether teams and the enterprise are meeting targets.
- Audit impact: auditors use KPIs to judge whether processes perform as intended.
- It is a lead indicator of whether a goal will likely be reached.
- It indicates capabilities, practices and skills.
- Example: employee satisfaction with IT services.
- A high satisfaction score suggests IT is meeting business needs.
- ⚠ Check: manual calls KPIs lead indicators; outcome (KGI) measures are not covered here.

### Performance drivers
- Definition: another name for performance indicators, mainly used in BSCs.
- Use: explains why a KPI predicts the outcome rather than reports it.
- Example: IT service delivery is an IT goal but a business capability.

### KPIs and control criteria
- Definition: control criteria set the expected operating level of a control.
- Use: criteria are often based on KPIs showing a control works correctly.
- Audit impact: auditors compare actual KPI results with desired objectives.

### Changeover KPI examples
- Definition: a changeover KPI measures success of moving to a new system or change.
- Use: shows whether change control was managed with enough quality and testing.
- Examples of measures:
  - Percentage of errors.
  - Number of trouble reports.
  - Duration of a system outage.
  - Degree of customer satisfaction.
- Audit impact: poor changeover KPIs point to weak testing or change control.
- Exam traps:
  - KPIs measure process performance; they do not forecast threats.
  - Threat-warning indicators belong to KRIs, not KPIs.

## 2.10.2 Key Risk Indicators (KRI)

- Definition: a measure that warns of growing exposure to risk events.
- Use: gives early warning before harm affects business performance.
- Audit impact: auditors verify thresholds exist and alerts reach assigned monitors.
- Example: percentage increase in phishing attempts signals higher threat level.
- A KRI usually has a threshold above which alerts are sent.
- Alerts go to individuals assigned to monitor that risk.
- Exam traps:
  - A KRI is forward-looking exposure; a KCI is control effectiveness.
  - A KRI without an assigned owner gives no real warning.

## 2.10.3 Key Control Indicators (KCI)

- Definition: a measure of how well a control reduces risk.
- Use: tracks control effect on causes, consequences or likelihood of risk.
- Audit impact: auditors use KCIs to judge control design and operating effectiveness.
- Example: percentage of IT assets compliant with security policies.
- This KCI shows how effectively security policies protect assets.
- Exam traps:
  - Compliance percentage of controls is a KCI, not a KPI.
  - Do not confuse KCI (control works) with KRI (risk rising).

### Quick comparison

| Indicator | Question answered | Example |
|---|---|---|
| KPI | Is the process enabling the goal? | Employee satisfaction with IT |
| KRI | Is risk exposure rising? | Phishing attempts up |
| KCI | Is the control reducing risk? | Assets compliant with policy |

## Developing metrics

### Four steps
- Definition: metric development is the method of building useful measures.
- Use: turns business objectives into scorable numbers.
- Step 1: identify critical data points needed for business objectives.
- Step 2: identify specific, quantifiable outputs of work from those processes.
- Step 3: establish targets against which results can be scored.
- Step 4: monitor performance and report to those who can adjust.
- Audit impact: auditors check each step exists, especially targets and reporting.

### Traits of an effective metric
- Consistently measured.
- Based on accepted best practices.
- Useful for internal and external comparison.
- Meaningful to IT customers and sponsors.
- Data gathered accurately.
- Expressed as a number, percentage or unit of measure.
- Contextually specific.
- Audit impact: inconsistent or inaccurate data makes any conclusion unreliable.

### Starting point 📓⭐
- Start by identifying processes critical to meeting customer requirements.
- Customer-driven critical processes come first, before choosing metrics.
- ⚠ Check: the manual's 2.10 text does not state this rule.
- It comes from your notes; the outline files it under 2.10.4.
- Exam traps:
  - Technical convenience or available tools are weaker starting points than customer needs.
  - Cost cutting is not the first driver of metric design.

## What the auditor checks metrics cover

- Long-term organizational objectives, such as technical, financial and operational goals.
- Short-term business and IT plan performance.
- Risk and regulatory compliance.
- Internal and external user satisfaction with service levels.
- IT processes, including solution and service delivery.
- Future-oriented activities, such as emerging technology and staff skill sets.
- Continuous monitoring of performance and capacity of IT resources.
- The measurement approach should be reviewed and updated with management feedback.
- Audit impact: gaps in coverage mean management could miss risks or poor value.
- Exam traps:
  - Metrics limited to financial results leave out users, processes and future readiness.
  - A one-time metric set that is never revised is a weakness.

## 2.10.4 Performance optimization

### Performance and optimization
- Definition: performance is how well a system works, including user satisfaction.
- Definition: optimization improves efficiency while keeping service at the highest level possible.
- Use: gain better service with minimal added infrastructure investment.
- Audit impact: auditors check improvements are measured, not assumed.
- Measures are not used to assign accountability or meet reporting duties.
- ⚠ Check: this tension with the incentives point is in the manual itself.
- Read it as: measures exist to drive improvement first.

### Effective governance
- Senior leadership sets goals aligned to approved business objectives.
- Process owners set metrics aligned to those goals.
- Each management layer monitors performance.
- Audit impact: missing any layer breaks the chain from strategy to measurement.
- Exam traps:
  - Goals come from senior leadership; process owners set the metrics.
  - Technical staff alone setting goals weakens business alignment.

### Critical Success Factors (CSF)
- Definition: vital elements whose failure can undermine a program.
- Use: focus management attention on what must go right.
- Manual CSFs for optimization: leadership support.
- Clear goals and objectives.
- Data-driven decision making, using surveys, interviews and process mapping.
- Employee involvement, such as improvement teams or brainstorming.
- Continuous improvement as an ongoing culture.
- Transparency: express goals and reports in common terms.
- ⚠ Check: manual states transparency separately, not as a listed CSF.
- Executive sponsor as top CSF for a security program 📓⭐.
- ⚠ Check: the manual 2.10.4 lists leadership support, not "executive sponsor" by name.
- ⚠ Check: the executive sponsor rule comes from your notes, not this section.
- Example: a security program lacking a funded sponsor stalls despite good tools.
- Exam traps:
  - Review boards, security units or process owners are secondary to sponsor support.
  - Sponsor authority and funding outweigh organizational structure.

### Methodologies and tools
- Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA): an iterative four-step improvement method.
- Use: controls and continuously improves processes and products.
- Plan: set objectives and processes; start small to test effects.
- Do: implement the plan and collect data.
- Check: compare actual results with targets and look for deviations.
- Act: request corrective action, analyze root causes and apply changes.
- If no improvement is needed, refine the PDCA scope in the next cycle.
- Audit impact: auditors look for evidence the Check and Act steps happen.
- PDCA with agile allows reassessing direction through sprints or iterations.
- Agile is iterative and incremental; waterfall gives a single pass.
- Exam traps:
  - Corrective action without root cause analysis is a weak Act step.
  - Data collection belongs to Do; comparison with targets belongs to Check.

### Other enterprise governance of IT (EGIT) frameworks
- COBIT: ISACA framework aligning IT with business and managing IT risk.
- COBIT also gives tools to assess and measure IT process performance.
- ISO/IEC 27000 series: best practices for information security programs.
- ISO/IEC 27001 is its well-known standard.
- ITIL: detailed framework for operational IT service management.
- ITIL was developed by the UK Office of Government Commerce.
- O-ISM3: a process-based information security management maturity model.
- ISO/IEC 38500: guiding principles for governing bodies on effective use of IT.
- ISO/IEC 20000: service management standard aligned with ITIL.
- ISO 31000: guidelines and common approach for risk management.
- Audit impact: auditors identify which framework the organization claims to follow.
- Exam traps:
  - Match framework to purpose: security 27000, service 20000, governance 38500, risk 31000.

## 2.10.5 Approaches and techniques

### Six Sigma
- Definition: a data-driven method to improve processes and reduce defects.
- Use: measurement-oriented process improvement that applies readily to IT.
- Defect: anything outside customer specifications.
- Lean Six Sigma also eliminates steps that add no value.
- Audit impact: auditors check defect data is accurate and tied to customer needs.
- Example: reducing failed deployments by measuring defects per release.

### Business agility and agile methodology
- Definition: a flexible way of working that delivers value in small increments.
- Use: adjusts delivery to changing needs and customer feedback.
- Audit impact: auditors verify control and documentation are not skipped for speed.
- Customer collaboration: customers stay involved throughout the project.
- Iterative development: short sprints each deliver a working increment.
- Self-organizing teams: cross-functional groups decide how to do the work.
- Continuous feedback: regular input from customers and stakeholders.
- Adaptability and flexibility: embrace change over rigid plans.
- Emphasis on simplicity: deliver the most essential features first.
- Frameworks: Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming (XP).
- Scrum uses time-boxed sprints and a product backlog.
- Scrum roles include product owner and scrum master.
- Example: a team releases a login feature in two weeks, then adds MFA.
- Exam traps:
  - Agile favors working increments and feedback, not no documentation or controls.
  - Waterfall is a single pass; agile revisits each aspect repeatedly.

### IT Balanced Scorecard (BSC) 📓⭐
- Definition: a method to assess IT beyond financial measures alone.
- Use: aligns IT with strategy while keeping perspectives in balance.
- Audit impact: auditors confirm scorecard measures link to business strategy.
- Objectives: a vehicle for management reporting to the board.
- Objectives: foster consensus among stakeholders on strategic aims.
- Objectives: show effectiveness and added value of IT.
- Objectives: communicate IT performance, risk and capabilities.
- It aids the IT strategy committee in business and IT alignment.
- Example: a scorecard shows uptime, user satisfaction, project value and training hours.

#### Four perspectives
- User orientation: the end user's view or evaluation of IT.
- Asks how users view IT ➕.
- Business contribution: the business value of IT investments.
- Asks how management views the organization ➕.
- ⚠ Check: manual says business contribution captures IT investment value; outline question unsupported.
- Operational excellence: the IT processes used to develop and deliver IT resources.
- Future orientation: human and technology resources IT needs for future delivery.
- Asks whether IT is positioned for future needs ➕.
- ⚠ Check: the "Asks" phrasings are not manual wording; use as memory aids.

#### Layers per perspective
- Each perspective has four layers: mission, strategies, measures, sources.
- Mission examples: become preferred supplier of information systems.
- Mission examples: deliver economic, effective and efficient IT services.
- Mission examples: obtain reasonable business contribution from IT investments.
- Mission examples: develop opportunities for future challenges.
- Strategy examples: user partnerships and enhanced service levels.
- Strategy examples: control IT expenses and provide business value.
- Strategy examples: new capabilities, staff training and R&D support.
- Measures: a balanced set of KPIs guides business-oriented IT decisions.
- Measures: KRIs act as early warning if objectives are not met.
- Measures: KCIs monitor effectiveness of the control environment.
- Sources: end-user personnel, the COO and process owners.
- Exam traps:
  - The BSC primary benefit is aligning IT objectives and metrics with strategy.
  - It does not calculate exact ROI, enforce regulation or replace financial reporting.

### IT portfolio management versus BSC
- Portfolio management: strength is agility in adjusting investments.
- Its feedback loop considers entire program investments.
- BSC: operationalizes the strategic plan using vision and strategy.
- Its feedback loop relates to strategic planning.
- BSC goal is not IT value or operations budget control.
- Audit impact: auditors choose the tool that fits the question being asked.
- Exam traps:
  - Adjusting investments quickly points to portfolio management, not BSC.
  - Linking investment decisions to strategy points to BSC.

### Additional performance measurement approaches
- Benchmarking: compare performance with peers and competitors to learn best practices.
- Use: reveals gaps in quality, efficiency and other metrics.
- Business process reengineering (BPR): radical redesign for dramatic improvement.
- BPR often uses IT to automate and streamline processes.
- Example: a fully automated supply chain improving visibility and inventory levels.
- Audit impact: BPR can remove old controls; auditors check new ones replace them.
- Root cause analysis: finding the origin of events or failures.
- Use: lets controls address what actually caused deficiencies.
- Audit impact: fixing symptoms only means problems recur.
- Life cycle cost-benefit analysis: compares costs with outcomes for strategic IT direction.
- Life cycle (LC): stages in the existence of an investment.
- Life cycle cost (LCC): estimated costs of maintenance, updates, failure and interoperability.
- Benefit analysis (BA): user and business operational costs or benefits from systems.
- Exam traps:
  - BPR is radical redesign; incremental tuning is continuous improvement, not BPR.
  - Benchmarking compares externally; root cause analysis explains internal failures.

## Takeaway

- KPI shows process performance, KRI warns of risk, KCI proves control effect.
- Every metric needs a baseline, a threshold and a reporting owner.
- Start metric design from customer-critical processes; executive sponsor leads security programs.
- PDCA loops: plan, do, check, act on root causes.
- BSC aligns IT to strategy; portfolio management adjusts investments.
- Verify claims against the manual; several outline items are only memory aids.
