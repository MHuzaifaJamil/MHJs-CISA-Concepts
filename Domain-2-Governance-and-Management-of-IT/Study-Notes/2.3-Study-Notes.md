## 2.3 Overview: the four document types

### Basics
- **Policy**
  - Definition: high-level statement of what management wants and expects.
  - Use: sets direction and tone for the whole enterprise.
  - Audit impact: the benchmark against which compliance is judged.
- **Standard**
  - Definition: mandatory rule or specification that measures policy compliance.
  - Use: sets boundaries and baselines for procedures and guidelines.
  - Audit impact: auditor tests whether systems and processes meet it.
- **Procedure**
  - Definition: documented step-by-step instructions to achieve a policy objective.
  - Use: tells staff exactly how to perform a task.
  - Audit impact: auditor identifies embedded controls and tests whether they operate.
- **Guideline**
  - Definition: advisory help for carrying out a procedure.
  - Use: gives examples, background, dependencies and tools.
  - Audit impact: not mandatory, so deviation alone is not a violation.
- Policies and standards are tools of governance and management respectively.
- Procedures and guidelines are the responsibility of operations.
- Terms are used differently across organizations; adopt the standard-setting bodies' meanings.
- Order of authority: policy, then standard, then procedure, then guideline.
- Each layer builds on the one above it.

### Policy hierarchy example (access control)
- Example: policy says information resources must be controlled against unauthorized access.
- Example: standard adopts least privilege, giving only what the job needs.
- Example: procedure gives accounting staff accounts payable access by job title and role.
- Example: guideline says managers review job changes; no reply in five days revokes access.
- ⚠ Check: outline says "role-based AP access" is the procedure; manual agrees (figure 2.8).
- ⚠ Check: five-day auto-revoke is shown as a guideline, though it acts mandatory.
- Exam traps
  - A mandatory, enforceable rule is a standard or policy, never a guideline.
  - Step-by-step "how" content is a procedure, not a policy.
  - Governance-level direction is policy; operational detail belongs lower in the hierarchy.

## 2.3.1 Policies

### Core idea
- **Policy** (see overview): management's high-level intent, expectations and direction.
- Information security policy is approved and reviewed by senior management 📓⭐.
- Audit impact: lack of senior management review is a governance-level concern.
- **Governance**
  - Definition: top-level direction and oversight of IT by leadership.
  - Use: policies are its "constitution", fixing rules everything else follows.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks policies align with strategic objectives.

### Characteristics of a good policy
- High-level statement of management intent, expectations and direction.
- Acts as the constitution of governance.
- Clearly aligned with the enterprise's strategic objectives.
- Mature enterprises keep high-level policies static for long periods.
- Divisions and departments write lower-level policies for their operations.
- Lower-level policies must stay consistent with high-level policies.
- States who approved it and its review date.
- Updated for new technology, risk and control changes, and regulation.
- Updated for significant changes in business processes.
- Supports business objectives and is reinforced by IS controls.
- Audit impact: auditor checks approver and review date to judge currency.
- Audit impact: auditor pays attention to processes using emerging technology.
- Exam traps
  - A policy hindering business objectives must be reported for improvement.
  - Static high-level policy is normal; stale policy ignoring change is a finding.

### Development and success factors
- **Bottom-up development** 📓⭐
  - Definition: policies built from operational staff input and risk analysis.
  - Use: policies reflect real risks and daily practice.
  - Audit impact: better fit with actual risk than top-down mandates.
- ⚠ Check: bottom-up development is absent from this manual section; from my notes.
- **Employee comprehension** 📓⭐
  - Definition: staff understand the policy's framework and intent.
  - Use: understanding drives actual compliance behavior.
  - Audit impact: a good policy not understood by staff still fails.
- ⚠ Check: comprehension as key success factor is not stated in this section.
- Manual does stress awareness for ISP sign-off and for procedures.
- **Missing policies** 📓
  - Definition: absence of a required policy over a whole area.
  - Use: reveals uncontrolled, enterprise-wide exposure.
  - Audit impact: systemic risk, not a single isolated failure.
- Example: no data backup policy leaves every backup practice unmanaged.
- ⚠ Check: missing policy versus technical gap ranking depends on question wording.
- **Employee policy manual** 📓
  - Definition: document setting behavior standards for staff.
  - Use: gives legal grounds for disciplinary action.
  - Audit impact: confirm it exists, is signed and is enforced.
- ⚠ Check: employee policy manual is not in this section; from my notes.
- Exam traps
  - After confirming policy quality, check staff understanding next; update frequency is secondary.
  - Management approval alone does not make a policy effective.
  - Bottom-up advantage is risk-based relevance, not coverage or top-management review.

### Audit use of policies
- Policies fall within audit scope; the auditor tests compliance with them.
- Auditor may rely on another assurance function's compliance testing.
- Reliance is allowed only if that work meets the audit department's standards.
- IS controls should flow from enterprise policies.
- Policies serve as the benchmark for evaluating compliance.
- Report any policy that hinders achieving business objectives.
- **Outsourced service provider (OSP)**
  - Definition: external party running IT or business functions for the enterprise.
  - Use: its work must still meet the enterprise's policy requirements.
  - Audit impact: check policy applicability, OSP compliance and policy conflicts.
- Exam traps
  - Relying on others' testing needs quality equal to audit standards, not blind trust.
  - A policy conflict with a provider's own policy is itself an audit concern.

### Information security policy (ISP)
- **Information security policy**
  - Definition: rules and statements protecting information and related technology.
  - Use: foundation of security infrastructure; guides behavior and tool choices.
  - Audit impact: the auditor's reference framework for IS audit assignments.
- Must balance level of control against level of productivity.
- Control cost must never exceed the expected benefit.
- Enterprise culture shapes how the policy is designed and implemented.
- Senior management must approve the ISP.
- Document it and communicate it to employees, service providers and partners.
- Adequacy and appropriateness of the ISP can itself be audited.
- ISO/IEC 27001 and the 27002 guidelines are content benchmarks.
- ⚠ Check: manual says "27001 or equivalent"; ISO 27001 is not mandatory ➕.
- Many enterprises model their ISP on major ISO/IEC 27001 subsections.
- Exam traps
  - A control costing more than its benefit is wrong, even if it adds security.
  - Maximum security is not the goal; balance with productivity is.

#### Elements of an ISP document
- Definition of information security for the enterprise.
- Scope and objectives of the policy document.
- Statement of management intent and expectations.
- Framework for control objectives, risk assessment and risk management structure.
- Compliance with legislative, regulatory and contractual requirements.
- Education, training and awareness requirements.
- Business continuity management and disaster recovery plans.
- Consequences of policy violations.
- General and specific responsibilities, including incident reporting guidance.
- References to supplemental documents such as detailed policies, standards and procedures.
- Communicate in a form accessible and understandable to each reader.
- ISP may be part of a general policy document.
- Keep detailed internal version; share summary version externally.
- Summary avoids disclosing sensitive enterprise information.
- Require sign-off at hire and regularly afterward, for example annually.
- Audit impact: sign-off evidence shows awareness; annual re-sign handles policy changes.
- Exam traps
  - Distributing the full internal ISP to third parties risks disclosure.
  - One-time sign-off at hire is weaker than periodic sign-off.

#### Policy set concerns
- Enterprises may document security as a set of policies.
- **High-level security policy**
  - Definition: top statement including confidentiality, integrity and availability.
  - Use: anchors all lower-level security policies.
  - Audit impact: check all three CIA properties are addressed.
- **Data classification policy**
  - Definition: describes classifications, control levels and user responsibilities.
  - Use: matches protection effort to data sensitivity.
  - Audit impact: confirm ownership is assigned for each classification.
- **Acceptable use policy**
  - Definition: states permitted use of all information resources.
  - Use: covers hardware, software, networks and internet.
  - Audit impact: basis for disciplining misuse.
- **End-user computing policy**
  - Definition: sets parameters for desktops, mobile tools and personal devices.
  - Use: governs personal devices used for enterprise business.
  - Audit impact: check unmanaged devices are covered.
- **Access control policies**
  - Definition: method for defining and granting access to IT resources.
  - Use: covers network, application and remote access.
  - Audit impact: auditor tests access against the policy.
- **Incident response policy**
  - Definition: steps to take, limit exposure and notify during a breach.
  - Use: speeds a consistent response.
  - Audit impact: verify notification roles are defined.
- **Remote access (work-from-home) policy**
  - Definition: sets remote security expectations for networks and data handling.
  - Use: covers public networks, home networks and VPN protocols.
  - Audit impact: check VPN and data-handling rules are enforceable.
- Example: a remote-work policy requiring VPN on public Wi-Fi.

#### Review of the ISP
- ISP needs an owner with approved management responsibility.
- Owner develops, reviews and evaluates the policy.
- Review at planned intervals, at least annually.
- Also review on significant change to enterprise, operations or inherent risk.
- Purpose: confirm suitability, adequacy and effectiveness.
- Review should seek improvement opportunities as environment, law or technology changes.
- Defined review procedure includes schedule, update responsibility, approval and distribution.
- This review is performed by management, not the auditor.
- Audit impact: the auditor assesses whether management's review is performed.
- **Management review inputs**
  - Feedback from interested parties.
  - Results of independent reviews.
  - Status of preventive, detective and corrective actions.
  - Results of previous management reviews.
  - Process performance and ISP compliance.
  - Changes in environment, business, resources, legal or technical conditions.
  - Third-party, outsourcing or offshoring relationships.
  - Threat and vulnerability trends.
  - Reported security incidents.
  - Recommendations from authorities, governing bodies or regulators.
- **Management review outputs** (decisions and actions)
  - Better alignment of security with business objectives.
  - Improved approach to managing information security.
  - Improved risk assessment and risk management.
  - Improved control objectives and controls.
  - Improved allocation of resources and responsibilities.
- Keep review records with version numbers, revision dates and distribution dates.
- Obtain management approval of the revised policy.
- **Version control**
  - Definition: tracking which policy edition is current and when it changed.
  - Use: proves staff received the latest approved version.
  - Audit impact: missing version history weakens evidence of currency.
- Exam traps
  - Annual review is a minimum; major change triggers an earlier review.
  - Management performs the review; the auditor evaluates it.

#### Auditor policy review checklist
- Basis or framework on which the policy was defined.
- Appropriateness and completeness.
- Contents of the policies.
- Exceptions and why, for example password rules unsuited to legacy applications.
- Policy approval process.
- Policy implementation process.
- Effectiveness of implementation.
- Awareness and training.
- Periodic review and update process.
- Audit impact: undocumented exceptions are a finding; documented ones show area and reason.

## 2.3.2 Standards (mandatory specifications)

- **Standard**
  - Definition: a mandatory requirement, code of practice or specification.
  - Use: criteria to judge whether procedures, processes or systems meet policy.
  - Audit impact: auditors measure compliance against it.
- Manual says standards are approved by a recognized external standards organization.
- ⚠ Check: enterprises also adopt internal standards; manual's formal definition stresses external origin.
- Policies are the constitution; standards are the laws measuring compliance.
- Example: policy demands access control; standard mandates least privilege.
- Standards govern how procedures and guidelines are created.
- They set boundaries within which procedures operate.
- They set security baselines and acceptable risk appetite, as directed by management.
- **Risk appetite**
  - Definition: amount of risk management is willing to accept.
  - Use: limits what procedures may allow.
  - Audit impact: control gaps beyond appetite must be reported.
- Boundaries cover allowable limits on processes, people and technologies.
- Strong standards matter in fast-moving environments, aiding trust and reliability.
- Update standards to reflect the latest thinking and technology.
- **Professional standards**
  - Definition: standards issued by bodies such as ISACA and the IIA.
  - Use: guide how professionals perform and comply.
  - Audit impact: ISACA standards are mandatory for ISACA members 📓.
- ⚠ Check: ISACA standards being mandatory comes from my notes, not here.
- Related guidelines and techniques help apply professional standards.
- Exam traps
  - Standards are mandatory; ISACA guidelines are advisory, a classic distractor 📓⭐.
  - Standards are not optional best practice; guidelines are.

## 2.3.3 Procedures (step-by-step)

- **Procedure** (see overview): documented, defined steps achieving specific policy objectives.
- Derived from the parent policy, reflecting its spirit or intent.
- Must operate within the boundaries set by standards.
- Written clearly and concisely for those executing the steps.
- Document business and aligned IT processes plus embedded controls.
- **Process owner**
  - Definition: person accountable for a business or IT process.
  - Use: formulates procedures as an effective translation of policy.
  - Audit impact: the auditor's contact for procedures and control design.
- Procedures are more dynamic than parent policies.
- They must reflect changes in business, supporting IT systems and compliance.
- Frequent review and updates keep them relevant.
- Auditor reviews procedures to identify controls and evaluate design.
- Auditor then tests operation of controls over business and IT processes.
- Embedded controls must meet control objectives while staying efficient and practical.
- Practice differing from documented procedure causes inconsistency, especially with new hires.
- Missing documentation makes controls hard for management and auditors to assess.
- Awareness is a critical aspect; an unknown procedure is ineffective.
- Use reliable deployment methods and automation to retain and distribute procedures.
- Procedures follow change management for update, approval and distribution.
- Change management is less formal than for policies.
- **Embedding procedures in systems**
  - Definition: building procedure steps into application workflows.
  - Use: reduces deviation from expected steps.
  - Audit impact: easier to test; automated enforcement strengthens control.
- Example: a system blocking payment release without a second approver.
- Exam traps
  - Where practice differs from procedure, the finding is inconsistent execution risk.
  - Missing procedures hinder control identification, so report even if outcomes look fine.
  - Procedures change more often than policies; frequent change is not itself a defect.

## 2.3.4 Guidelines (advisory) 📓

- **Guideline** (see overview): advisory content supporting execution of procedures.
- Created by process owners to give individuals more detail.
- Clarify policies and standards.
- Describe dependencies.
- Offer suggestions and examples.
- Include narrative explaining the procedures.
- Provide background information and usable tools.
- Guidelines have uses beyond security governance; here they are viewed in that context.
- Audit impact: advisory, so auditors note them but test against policy and standards.
- ⚠ Check: manual never says "advisory"; that wording is from my notes 📓.
- ⚠ Check: ISACA professional guidelines are a separate use of the term.
- Exam traps
  - A document that is optional and explanatory is a guideline.
  - Do not report non-adherence to a guideline as a policy violation.

## Takeaway

- Policy sets intent, standard sets mandatory limits, procedure gives steps, guideline advises.
- Senior management approves the ISP; management reviews it at least annually.
- Policies stay stable; procedures change often and need awareness and change control.
- Auditors use policies as benchmarks and report policies that hinder business objectives.
- Missing policy is systemic risk; staff must understand policies for them to work.
- Several 📓 items are not in this manual section; verify before relying on them.
