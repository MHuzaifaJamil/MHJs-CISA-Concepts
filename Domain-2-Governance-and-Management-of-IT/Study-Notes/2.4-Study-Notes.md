## 2.4.1 What Enterprise Architecture (EA) Is

### Basics
- Definition: EA is a structured record of an organization's IT assets.
- Use: It helps managers understand, run and plan IT spending.
- Audit impact: It gives the auditor a reference point for judging IT.
- Definition: An IT asset is any system, data or network the business relies on.
- Example: A bank maps core banking, databases, servers and links in one model.

### Why it matters
- Modern IT landscapes are complex, so transparency is needed.
- Enterprises must see how new investment affects the existing IT environment.
- Management also needs to judge returns or losses on that investment.
- Use: EA links investment decisions to expected business value.
- Audit impact: Poor EA means unclear ownership, duplicated systems and wasted spend.

### EA aligns IT and business goals 📓
- Definition: Alignment means IT supports what the business is trying to achieve.
- Use: EA shows which IT assets serve which business goals.
- Audit impact: Auditor checks systems against the EA to confirm alignment.
- ⚠ Check: Manual says auditors verify alignment with EA; "aligns goals" is outline wording.
- Exam traps
  - Tool to determine IT-to-business goal alignment: EA, not performance metrics alone.
  - A balanced scorecard measures performance; it does not map assets to goals.
  - Distractor logic: choose the option that structurally documents IT against business.

## 2.4.2 Auditor's Use of EA

- Auditor treats the enterprise's chosen EA as a main information source.
- Auditor does not impose a personal framework over the chosen one.
- Auditor confirms systems under review align with that EA.
- Audit impact: Misalignment signals shadow systems, weak governance or uncontrolled change.
- Example: A new payroll tool appears that the EA never listed; auditor raises it.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: start from the enterprise's EA, then test conformance to it.
  - Wrong: ignoring EA and relying only on interviews or vendor documents.
  - Wrong: auditor redesigning the architecture; auditors assess, management designs.

## 2.4.3 Zachman Framework

### Origin and idea
- Definition: Zachman is a matrix for classifying and developing enterprise architectures.
- Use: A starting point for many modern EA projects.
- Audit impact: Gaps in the matrix show undocumented parts of the enterprise.
- First published by John Zachman in the late 1980s.
- Core idea: building IT systems resembles constructing a building.
- Construction moves from abstract to physical using blueprints, floor plans, wiring diagrams.
- IT equivalent: diagrams, flowcharts, data or class models, and finally code.
- Artifacts show systems at progressively greater levels of detail.
- Different participants join at different project stages.
- Example: Executives view the scope row; engineers work from the technology row.

### The matrix
- Columns describe what is asked: data, function, network, people, process, strategy.
- Manual labels: Functional (Application), Network (Technology), People (Organization), Process (Workflow).
- Rows describe perspective, from abstract to concrete.
- Row 1: Scope.
- Row 2: Enterprise model.
- Row 3: Systems model.
- Row 4: Technology model.
- Row 5: Detailed representation.
- Goal: complete every cell of the matrix.
- At the start, most enterprises struggle to fill every cell.
- The hardest cells are usually at the highest (most abstract) level.
- ⚠ Check: Manual's figure includes a Strategy column; outside versions differ. Use manual.
- Exam traps
  - Zachman is a classification matrix, not a security or process-governance standard.
  - Completeness is the goal; leaving cells blank is a gap, not acceptable design.

### Two ways to complete it
- Enterprises may approach the matrix from a technology or business-process view.

#### Technology perspective
- Definition: Starts from the technology choices an enterprise faces.
- Use: Guides decisions such as Java EE or .NET for application development.
- Use: Guides connecting internal and inter-organizational systems.
- Use: Guides exposing legacy and ERP applications to the web without a rewrite.
- Use: Guides insourcing versus outsourcing and when to use virtualization or cloud.
- Audit impact: Auditor checks technology choices follow the documented architecture.

#### Business process perspective
- Definition: Starts from core value-adding and supporting processes.
- Use: Understand processes, their parts and the technology that supports them.
- Use: Redesign and replace parts progressively to gain business improvement.
- The business process model maps to the upper rows of Zachman.
- After mapping, the enterprise picks the best mix of technologies.
- Example: A retailer maps order-to-cash first, then chooses supporting systems.
- Exam traps
  - Business process view maps to upper rows; technology view addresses technology choices.
  - Process-first means technology follows process, not the reverse.

## 2.4.4 TOGAF

- Definition: TOGAF is The Open Group Architecture Framework.
- Use: High-level approach to design, plan, implement and govern enterprise IT architecture.
- Audit impact: Auditor can check whether architecture work follows the adopted method.
- Example: A firm adopts TOGAF so all projects follow one architecture process.
- Exam trap: TOGAF covers enterprise IT architecture generally, not security specifically.

## 2.4.5 SABSA

- Definition: SABSA is Sherwood Applied Business Security Architecture.
- Use: Focuses on information security architecture.
- Audit impact: Auditor checks security controls trace back to business requirements.
- ⚠ Check: Manual glossary calls it risk-driven; deeper layer details are unverified ➕.
- Exam trap: Security-architecture question points to SABSA; general EA points to Zachman or TOGAF.

## 2.4.6 Comparing the Frameworks

| Framework | Main focus | Memory hook |
|---|---|---|
| Zachman | Classifying EA in a matrix | Building blueprints, cells |
| TOGAF | Designing and governing IT architecture | Open Group, method |
| SABSA | Security architecture | Security, business-driven |

- Manual also names these as "other EA frameworks" beyond Zachman.
- ⚠ Check: Federal Enterprise Architecture appears in the manual index only; not covered here.

## Takeaway
- EA documents IT assets so investment and alignment can be judged.
- Auditors follow the enterprise's chosen EA and test systems against it.
- Zachman is a matrix: complete all cells, abstract to physical.
- Technology view guides choices; business process view maps to upper rows.
- TOGAF designs and governs IT architecture; SABSA targets security architecture.
