## 2.1 Opening principles

### Why laws matter to IT governance
- Definition: IT governance is how leaders direct and control IT toward business goals.
- Use: Governance must account for laws, regulations and standards that apply to the enterprise.
- Audit impact: Requirements depend on both geographic location and industry.
- Example: A bank in Germany faces banking rules plus EU privacy law.
- Audit impact: Ignoring an applicable law is a governance failure.
- The manual says enterprises may knowingly accept noncompliance risk and its penalties.
- ⚠ Check: Management may accept noncompliance; the auditor reports it, never approves it.

### Laws and the data life cycle
- Definition: Data life cycle is the path of data from creation to disposal.
- Laws touch six stages: receipt, processing, storage, transmission, distribution, destruction.
- Use: Map each stage to the legal rules that apply to it.
- Audit impact: Auditors test controls at every stage, including secure destruction.
- Example: A privacy law may require deleting customer records after a retention period.

### Laws evolve
- Laws and regulations change constantly.
- Audit impact: The IS auditor must stay current on which laws apply.
- Audit impact: Outdated legal knowledge produces incomplete audit scope and missed findings.

### Globally recognized compliance drivers
- Driver 1: Privacy and confidentiality of personal data.
- Driver 2: Intellectual property rights.
- Driver 3: Reliability of financial information produced by the enterprise.
- Use: These three drivers shape most regulatory policies and procedures.
- Definition: Regulatory driver means a legal force that requires specific policies and controls.
- Audit impact: Auditors map each driver to policies, procedures and tested controls.
- Example: Privacy maps to access controls; financial reliability maps to change controls.

### Specific legislation is not tested ⭐
- The manual states exam questions will not cover specific laws.
- The manual says the exam may ask how to audit for compliance.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: Know concepts and audit steps, not statute numbers.
  - Distractor: Choosing an answer because it names a famous law is usually wrong.
  - Distractor: Memorizing penalty amounts has no exam value.
- ⚠ Check: SOX details are context only, not tested content.

## 2.1.1 Impact of laws, regulations and standards on IS audit

### Statutory and regulatory requirements 📓
- Definition: Statutory requirements are duties written into law by a legislature.
- Definition: Regulatory requirements are rules issued by an authority under that law.
- Definition: Industry standards are agreed practices, often contractual rather than legal.
- Use: Audits are commonly performed to confirm compliance with statutory requirements.
- Audit impact: Legal requirements set audit scope and audit objectives.
- The manual names two concern areas for management and auditors.
- Area 1: Legal requirements that apply to the audit or IS audit itself.
- Area 2: Legal requirements placed on the auditee's systems, data and reporting.
- Example: A stock exchange rule dictates how a broker retains trade records.

### Steps to determine compliance with external requirements
- Step 1: Identify external requirements covering data, controls, storage, IT services and audits.
- Step 2: Document the applicable laws and regulations.
- Step 3: Check management considered them in plans, policies, standards and applications.
- Step 4: Review internal IT documents addressing adherence to industry laws.
- Step 5: Test adherence to the established procedures.
- Step 6: Confirm contracts with external IT providers reflect legal responsibilities.
- Audit impact: Step 6 matters because outsourcing does not transfer legal accountability.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: First identify and document which requirements apply.
  - Distractor: Testing controls before knowing the legal requirements is out of order.
  - Distractor: Relying on the vendor's assurance alone leaves contract coverage unverified.

### Terminology equivalence
- GEIT, EGIT, governance of IT and ITG mean the same.
- Audit impact: Different wording in a question does not signal a different concept.

### Multi-jurisdiction enterprises
- Definition: Multi-jurisdiction means operating under more than one country's laws.
- Use: Management must know the requirements of every jurisdiction it operates in.
- Audit impact: Audit scope must include each applicable jurisdiction's rules.
- Example: A retailer with stores in three countries follows three data-protection regimes.

### Extraterritorial reach of laws
- Definition: Extraterritorial means a law applies beyond its home borders.
- Some laws bind enterprises not headquartered in the issuing jurisdiction.
- Example: GDPR applies to enterprises handling data of individuals in the EU.
- Audit impact: Head-office location alone never decides which laws apply.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: Applicability follows where the data subjects or operations are.
  - Distractor: "Our servers are abroad, so the law does not apply" is wrong.
- ⚠ Check: Manual says "General Data Protection Act"; official name is Regulation.
- ⚠ Check: Outline says GDPR is absent; the manual does cite it.

### IIA considerations when auditing regulatory compliance
- Definition: IIA is the Institute of Internal Auditors, whose guidance the manual cites.
- Use: Seven elements describe an effective compliance program the auditor evaluates.
- 1. Standards and procedures: Established rules to reduce risk of criminal activity.
- 2. Senior responsibility: A named senior manager owns overall compliance.
- 3. Reliable staff: Background checks before granting access or authority roles.
- 4. Communication: Procedures conveyed through training or documentation.
- 5. Monitoring and auditing: Reasonable steps such as monitoring and reporting.
- 6. Consistent enforcement: Disciplinary action applied fairly across the enterprise.
- 7. Response and prevention: Report offenses to authorities and prevent repeats promptly.
- Audit impact: Missing any element is a finding in the compliance program.
- Example: Discipline applied only to junior staff breaks consistent enforcement.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: Compliance needs ownership, communication, monitoring and enforcement together.
  - Distractor: Written policy alone, without training or enforcement, is insufficient.
  - Distractor: Background checks apply before granting authority, not after an incident.

### UNCTAD Global Cyberlaw Tracker
- Definition: A United Nations mapping of national cyberlaw adoption.
- Use: Shows whether a country has passed, or has pending, relevant legislation.
- Topics tracked: e-transactions, consumer protection, data protection/privacy, cybercrime.
- Coverage: 194 UNCTAD member states, per the manual.
- Audit impact: A research aid for multi-country audits; it is not a legal opinion.

## 2.1.2 Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC)

### Why GRC exists
- Definition: GRC joins governance, risk management and compliance into one coordinated effort.
- Reason: Assurance processes cannot work well in silos.
- Audit impact: Siloed functions duplicate work and leave gaps between teams.

### The three components
- Governance: Managing the enterprise's policies, processes and decisions.
- Risk management: Identifying, assessing and treating potential risk.
- Compliance: Adhering to laws, regulations, standards and policies.

### OCEG definition
- Definition: OCEG is the group that coined the term GRC.
- Its view: GRC reliably achieves objectives, addresses uncertainty and acts with integrity.
- Its stated outcome is "Principled Performance".
- Use: GRC is often treated as one business activity with overlapping assurance work.

### Assurance functions inside GRC
- Governance, internal audit and compliance programs.
- Enterprise risk management (ERM) and operational risk management (ORM).
- Incident management and other related activities.
- Audit impact: Auditors can leverage other functions' work to reduce duplicated testing.

### Focus areas of GRC
- GRC can run anywhere but usually targets financial, IT and legal areas.
- Financial GRC: Proper financial processes and compliance, such as SOX in the US.
- IT GRC: Proper IT operation and policy compliance.
- Legal GRC: Overall regulatory compliance for the location and industry.
- Example: A bank uses one GRC tool to track SOX controls and IT policies.
- Overall goal: A holistic view of risk that could block objectives.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: GRC integrates; it does not replace independent audit.
  - Distractor: Choosing separate tools per department contradicts the silo-removal purpose.

## 2.1.3 Specific laws and standards (reference only)

### SOX, Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 📓
- Definition: A US law protecting investors from fraudulent financial reporting.
- Use: Requires internal controls over financial reporting at US public companies.
- Audit impact: Auditors assess IT general controls over financial systems.
- Audit impact: Senior management attests to adequacy of internal controls.
- Section 302: Officers certify the reports. 📓
- Section 404: Internal control assessment and reporting. 📓
- Example: A CFO signs a quarterly report after testing access controls on the ledger.
- Financial GRC example: SOX compliance tracking.
- ⚠ Check: Manual cites SOX but not sections 302 or 404.
- ⚠ Check: The ITGC link and management sign-off are not in manual section 2.1.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: SOX concerns reliability of financial information, not general privacy.
  - Distractor: Treating SOX as applying to private non-listed firms by default is wrong.

### Privacy and data laws
- GDPR: EU data protection law with global reach for EU individuals' data. ➕
- ⚠ Check: Consent and legitimate interest as legal bases are not in the manual. ➕
- HIPAA: US health information law; the manual only lists it as an audit-triggering law.
- ⚠ Check: HIPAA is in the manual list; the outline's ➕ mark can be removed.
- PCI DSS: Payment card industry security standard, not a law. ➕
- ⚠ Check: PCI DSS does not appear in manual section 2.1. ➕
- Audit impact: Know what each protects, not article or section details.

### Other laws listed in the manual
- US: GLBA, FERPA, COPPA, CIPA, FISMA 2002.
- Canada: PIPEDA.
- South Korea: PIPA.
- Japan: Financial Instruments and Exchange Act (FIEA) 2006.
- South Africa: POPI Act.
- UK: Ministry of Defence DEFCON 658 and the UK Data Protection Act.
- Saudi Arabia: Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL).
- Australia: Privacy Act 1988.
- Audit impact: These illustrate that laws are local; they are not exam content.
- ⚠ Check: Manual only lists these as laws that may require an audit.

### Basel Accords
- Definition: International banking supervision standards.
- Manual mentions Basel Accords as a regulatory driver for IT controls in section 2.2.
- ⚠ Check: Basel appears in manual section 2.2 EGIT, not in section 2.1.
- Audit impact: Banks may face IT control expectations from such standards. ➕

## Takeaway
- Laws depend on location and industry; auditors must keep their knowledge current.
- Specific statutes are not tested; the audit method for compliance is.
- First identify applicable requirements, then document, then test adherence.
- Extraterritorial laws like GDPR apply wherever covered individuals' data is handled.
- GRC unites governance, risk and compliance so assurance work is not siloed.
- IIA's seven program elements guide auditing of regulatory compliance.
