## 2.5 ERM overview (basics)

### Risk
- Definition: chance that a threat exploits a weakness and harms the business.
- Use: ranks what needs protection first.
- Audit impact: audit effort follows risk, so risk understanding drives audit planning.

### Enterprise risk management (ERM)
- Definition: process of finding threats and weaknesses, then choosing safeguards.
- Use: reduces risk to an acceptable level, called residual risk.
- Use: choice of safeguards depends on the value of the information resource.
- Audit impact: auditor judges whether the process is effective, not just present.
- ERM is a management function, not an independent audit function.
- ERM advises senior leaders on strategy to reach the acceptable risk level.
- Understanding risk appetite drives all risk management effort.
- Appetite shapes technology investment, asset protection and assurance level required.
- Example: a bank board decides how much fraud loss it will live with.
- Exam traps
  - Management owns and manages risk; the auditor verifies the process and its governance.
  - ERM is not internal audit, so answers calling it independent assurance are wrong.

### Risk management process words
- Process steps: identify, analyze, evaluate, treat, monitor, communicate.
- Use: gives one repeatable language across departments.
- Audit impact: a missing step, such as monitoring, is a reportable gap.

## Risk appetite and tolerance

### Risk appetite
- Definition: amount of risk leadership willingly takes to reach strategic goals.
- Use: sets the target level for all risk decisions.
- Audit impact: auditor compares actual risk and controls against stated appetite.
- It is a deliberate decision made by senior leadership.
- It is strategic and set at enterprise level.
- Example: a fast-moving technology firm accepts high appetite for innovation risk.

### Risk tolerance
- Definition: acceptable deviation from appetite before objectives are endangered.
- Use: gives managers a measurable limit for a project or department.
- Audit impact: breaches of tolerance should trigger escalation; check that they do.
- It is tactical and can be set per department or project.
- Example: the same firm sets low tolerance for balance sheet losses.
- Both inform decisions, such as whether to launch a new product.
- Launch risk is compared with appetite and tolerance; if too high, do not launch.
- Exam traps
  - Appetite equals strategic and enterprise-wide; tolerance equals tactical and local.
  - Tolerance is deviation around appetite, not a separate larger amount of risk.

## Risk responses

### The four responses
- Avoid
  - Definition: remove exposure by not doing the risky activity.
  - Example: moving a data center out of a flood zone. 📓
- Mitigate
  - Definition: lower probability or impact using controls.
  - Use: most common response; controls are defined, implemented and monitored.
- Share or transfer
  - Definition: pass impact to partners or third parties.
  - Use: insurance, contracts, partnerships.
  - Audit impact: transfer moves financial impact, not accountability for the risk.
- Accept
  - Definition: knowingly proceed despite possible impact.
  - Use: fits unavoidable risk or risk within tolerance.
  - Audit impact: needs formal monitoring so the business reacts if risk rises.
- Rejecting risk means ignoring it; it is a red flag for the auditor.
- Exam traps
  - Relocating to remove the hazard is avoidance, not transfer or reduction.
  - Ignoring risk is not acceptance; acceptance is informed, documented and monitored.
- ⚠ Check: the manual also lists "reduce" later; mitigate and reduce mean the same.

## Levels of IT risk management

### Operational level
- Concern: effectiveness of systems, control bypass, resource loss, noncompliance.
- Example: a key server outage stops customer service.

### Project level
- Concern: managing complexity so objectives are met.
- Example: an overrun ERP rollout misses its goals.

### Strategic level
- Concern: IT alignment to strategy, competitors, technological change.
- Example: a rival adopts cloud faster, shrinking market share.

### How the levels connect
- Levels belong to different owners, but must not work separately.
- A major system failure can become a strategic issue for top management.
- New project deliverables change the operational risk environment.
- Exam traps
  - Risk at one level can raise risk at another; isolated treatment is wrong.

## 2.5.1 Developing a risk management program

### Asset inventory and classification
- Definition (inventory): list of all assets the program must protect. 📓⭐
- Definition (classification): grading assets by value, criticality or sensitivity. 📓⭐
- Use: matches protection level to asset value. 📓⭐
- Use: prevents both over-protection and under-protection. 📓
- Audit impact: an incomplete inventory means unassessed, unprotected assets.
- ⚠ Check: manual 2.5.1 lists program purpose first; inventory is not named.
- ⚠ Check: your notes say inventory comes first; choose it when offered.
- Exam traps
  - When the question asks what to do before assessing risk, asset inventory leads.
  - Classification follows inventory; you cannot grade assets you have not listed.

### Step: establish purpose
- Purpose aligns with overall enterprise strategy.
- Programs mostly exist to advise management on risk to their objectives.
- Define purpose before planning, so effectiveness can be measured later.
- KPI
  - Definition: key performance indicator, measures how well a process performs.
- KRI
  - Definition: key risk indicator, early signal that risk is rising.
- Metrics evaluate whether the program works.
- Senior management and the board set tone and goals.

### Step: assign responsibility
- Name an individual or team to develop and implement the program.
- Risk management must be integrated at all enterprise levels.
- Operational staff and board members assist the risk management committee.
- Example: a risk committee with IT, finance and operations representatives.
- Exam traps
  - Senior management holds ultimate responsibility for risk; the risk team only coordinates. 📓

## 2.5.2 Risk management life cycle

### Overview
- Definition: repeatable process used to manage IT risk consistently.
- Use: ensures risk is handled the same way every time.
- Four steps: identification, assessment, response, monitoring.
- Audit impact: auditor assesses effectiveness of the whole cycle.

### Step 1: IT risk identification 📓
- Collect data to support identification, analysis and reporting.
- Goal: find assets needing protection because they are vulnerable.
- Threat
  - Definition: circumstance or event that can harm an information resource.
  - Harm: destruction, disclosure, modification or denial of service.
- Classification
  - Value-based classification prioritizes further investigation.
  - Criticality and sensitivity classification enables a standard protection model.
  - The most critical data let the business keep operating.
- Typical IT assets: information, hardware, software, documents, personnel, clients.
- Other assets: buildings, inventory, cash, goodwill, reputation.
- Example: customer database is classified critical and sensitive.

### Step 2: IT risk assessment
- Assess threats, vulnerabilities and likelihood of occurrence.
- Threat classes: errors, malicious attack, fraud, theft, equipment or software failure.
- Vulnerability
  - Definition: characteristic of a resource that a threat can exploit.
  - Examples: poor user knowledge, no security functions, untested technology, unprotected transmission.
  - Audit impact: inadequate controls create vulnerabilities; next assess threats and likelihood. 📓
- Risk needs both a vulnerability and a threat to exploit it.
- Human threat actors
  - Script kiddies, hacktivists, criminals, terrorists, nation-states.
  - Also riots, civil unrest and political instability.
- Environmental threats: floods, lightning, tornadoes, hurricanes, earthquakes, fires.
- Impact
  - Definition: result of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.
  - Factors: severity, duration, and velocity (speed of spread).
- Loss types
  - Direct money loss.
  - Breach of legislation.
  - Loss of reputation or goodwill.
  - Endangered staff or customers.
  - Breach of confidence.
  - Lost business opportunity.
  - Reduced efficiency.
  - Business interruption.
- Impact depends on enterprise nature.
- Example: lost customer data may bankrupt a small retailer.
- Example: lost patient records at a hospital can cost lives.
- Exam traps
  - Vulnerability is internal weakness; threat is the external or environmental event.
  - A vulnerability with no possible threat does not create realized risk.

### Combining elements into overall risk
- Basic formula: overall risk = probability x magnitude of impact.
- Extended formula: overall risk = probability x impact x velocity.
- Use: gives relative priority, not an exact loss figure.
- Worked example (score, assumes 1-5 scales ➕)
  - Threat A: probability 4, impact 3, velocity 2.
  - Step 1: basic score = 4 x 3 = 12.
  - Step 2: extended score = 12 x 2 = 24.
  - Threat B: probability 2, impact 5, velocity 4.
  - Step 3: basic score = 2 x 5 = 10.
  - Step 4: extended score = 10 x 4 = 40.
  - Result: on basic score A ranks higher (12 over 10).
  - Result: on extended score B ranks higher (40 over 24).
  - Verification: recomputed as 4 x (3 x 2) = 24 and 2 x (5 x 4) = 40; both match.
- ⚠ Check: manual gives the formulas only; the 1-5 scales are my illustration.
- Exam traps
  - Adding velocity can change ranking; do not assume the basic ranking stays.

### Step 3: Risk response and mitigation
- Avoid, reduce, transfer, accept. 📓
- Accepted risk needs formal documentation and executive approval. 📓⭐
- Auditor first verifies the acceptance documentation. 📓⭐
- Example: leadership cuts security spending below the assessed level.
- Best auditor advice: management formally documents acceptance of the resulting risk. 📓
- Exam traps
  - Auditor does not decide to accept risk; management does, in writing.
  - Deferring controls or hiding gaps are not the auditor's recommendation.

#### Controls
- Definition: countermeasures or safeguards, being people, processes or products.
- Use: offset either the impact or the probability of a risk.
- Audit impact: auditor judges design strength and likelihood of effectiveness.
- Control strength: design strength plus likelihood of effectiveness.
- Strongest controls are preventive, automated and formal.
- Reason: they stop events without human action and are tested.
- Weakest controls are informal and manual.
- Reason: informal, manual controls are applied inconsistently.
- Hybrid (IT dependent) control
  - Definition: mix of manual and automated steps.
  - Use: chosen when full automation is not achievable.
- Control matrix
  - Rates preventive, detective and corrective controls by strength.
- Exam traps
  - Among controls, preventive plus automated plus formal beats detective manual ones.

#### Residual risk
- Definition: risk remaining after controls are applied.
- Use: compared with appetite to decide next action. 📓
- Audit impact: residual above appetite means weak or missing controls.
- Risk above appetite: add stricter or secondary controls.
- Risk below appetite: look for excessive control and removable cost.
- Final acceptance considers:
  - Organizational policy.
  - Risk appetite.
  - Risk identification and measurement.
  - Uncertainty in the assessment approach.
  - Cost and effectiveness of implementation.
  - Cost of control versus benefit.
- Control selection uses cost-benefit analysis.
- Reduction methods: terminate, lower probability, lower impact, transfer by insurance.
- Exam traps
  - Residual risk is what remains after controls, not risk before controls.
  - Over-control below appetite is also a finding, because it wastes money.

### Step 4: Risk and control monitoring and reporting
- Monitor risk performance and changes in the environment.
- Significant change triggers reassessment and control changes.
- Covers risk assessment, mitigation and reevaluation.
- Process is ongoing and iterative, never finished.
- Goal: cost-effective balance between controls and adapting to threats.
- Audit impact: stale assessments after major change are a finding.
- Exam traps
  - A one-time assessment is wrong because IT risk is dynamic.

### Auditor understanding of IT business risk
- Auditor focuses on confidentiality, integrity and availability risk of critical information.
- Auditor often assesses effectiveness of the enterprise's risk management process.
- Understand accepted industry and international risk management processes.
- Understand business purpose, nature and operating environment.
- Understand dependence on technology to reach goals.
- Understand business risk of using IT.
- Get an overview of business processes and IT impact.
- Assessment starts with business objectives, then critical assets and systems.
- Process is adaptive; most effort goes to most sensitive or critical assets.
- Exam traps
  - Start with objectives and critical assets, not with technical controls.

## 2.5.3 Risk analysis methods

### Qualitative
- Definition: ranks risk with words such as high, medium, low.
- Use: quick ranking from checklists, interviews and subjective ratings.
- Simplest and most frequently used method.
- Weakness: lacks the rigor expected in accounting and management.
- Weakness: gathering and analysing text can be time-consuming.
- Audit impact: results are subjective; check consistency between raters.
- Example: workshop rates phishing likelihood as "high".

### Semiquantitative
- Definition: descriptive ranks mapped onto a numeric scale.
- Use: reduces subjectivity when full quantification is impossible.
- Typical mapping: high 5, medium 3, low 1.
- Total score is the sum of weights across factors.
- Common in surveys scored 1 to 5.
- Worked example (aggregate weights)
  - Factors: phishing high, outage medium, theft low.
  - Step 1: weights are 5, 3 and 1.
  - Step 2: total = 5 + 3 + 1 = 9.
  - Step 3: maximum possible = 5 x 3 = 15.
  - Step 4: share of maximum = 9 / 15 = 60 percent.
  - Verification: 9 / 15 reduces to 3 / 5, which equals 0.6; matches 60 percent.
- Audit impact: numbers look precise but still rest on subjective ratings.
- Exam traps
  - Numeric scores on opinions are still semiquantitative, not quantitative.

### Quantitative
- Definition: uses numbers, mainly money, for likelihood and impact.
- Use: gives measurable results to justify control spending.
- Data sources: historical records, experience, industry practice, statistics, testing.
- Generally performed during the BIA.
- Main problem: valuing information assets, since people value assets differently.
- Technology asset value includes replacement cost and value of data processed.
- Audit impact: check that asset values and frequencies have defensible sources.

#### SLE, ARO, ALE ➕
- ⚠ Check: section 2.5 never defines SLE or ARO; kept unverified.
- SLE (single loss expectancy) ➕
  - Definition: expected money loss from one occurrence of an event.
  - Formula: SLE = asset value x exposure factor.
- Exposure factor ➕
  - Definition: share of asset value lost in one event.
- ARO (annual rate of occurrence) ➕
  - Definition: expected number of events per year.
- ALE (annual loss expectancy) ➕
  - Definition: expected money loss per year.
  - Formula: ALE = SLE x ARO.
  - Use: compare yearly loss with yearly control cost.
- Worked example: ALE calculation ➕
  - Asset value: 500,000. Exposure factor: 40 percent. ARO: 0.5 (once in two years).
  - Step 1: SLE = 500,000 x 0.40 = 200,000.
  - Step 2: ALE = 200,000 x 0.5 = 100,000 per year.
  - Verification: alternative method 500,000 x (0.40 x 0.5) = 500,000 x 0.20 = 100,000; matches.
- Worked example: cost-benefit of a control ➕
  - Control cuts ARO from 0.5 to 0.1; control costs 30,000 per year.
  - Step 1: new ALE = 200,000 x 0.1 = 20,000.
  - Step 2: reduction in ALE = 100,000 - 20,000 = 80,000.
  - Step 3: net benefit = 80,000 - 30,000 = 50,000 per year.
  - Step 4: benefit-to-cost ratio = 80,000 / 30,000 = about 2.67.
  - Verification: total cost with control = 20,000 + 30,000 = 50,000; saving 100,000 - 50,000 = 50,000; matches.
  - Decision: buy the control; the cost is below the benefit.
- Manual's own loss example (section 4.12.1)
  - Disruption chance 0.1 percent over five years; impact US$10 million.
  - Step 1: 0.1 percent = 0.001.
  - Step 2: 10,000,000 x 0.001 = 10,000 maximum reasonable spend over five years.
  - Step 3: per year = 10,000 / 5 = 2,000.
  - Verification: 1 in 1,000 of 10 million is 10 million / 1,000 = 10,000; matches.
- ⚠ Check: manual calls the five-year figure ALE, but it is not annual.
- Exam traps
  - Never spend more on a control than the loss it prevents.
  - ALE uses annual frequency; per-event loss alone is only SLE.
  - Quantitative methods fail mainly on asset valuation, not on arithmetic.

#### SOX scoping by transaction value
- Definition: SOX is a law requiring accurate financial reporting and controls. 📓
- Use: in-scope applications are chosen by volume or value of transactions.
- Example: the point-of-sale system handling 98 percent of revenue is in scope.
- The other system, with 2 percent, may stay out of scope.
- Audit impact: scope follows financial significance, saving documentation and testing effort.
- Exam traps
  - Scope by transaction value, not by system age, size or technology.

## Takeaway
- ERM advises; management owns risk; auditor verifies acceptance is documented and approved.
- Appetite is strategic; tolerance is tactical deviation; residual risk is compared to appetite.
- Avoid, mitigate, transfer, accept; ignoring risk is a red flag.
- Strongest controls are preventive, automated, formal; weakest are manual and informal.
- Risk equals probability x impact; ALE equals SLE x ARO.
- Monitoring is continuous; scope and effort follow value and criticality.
