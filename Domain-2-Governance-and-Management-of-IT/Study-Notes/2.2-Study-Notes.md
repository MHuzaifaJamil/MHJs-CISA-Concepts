## Opening: Corporate Governance Basics

### Corporate governance
- Definition: the system by which an enterprise is directed and controlled.
- Use: sets ethics, decision-making and overall practices across the enterprise.
- Audit impact: auditors judge whether direction and control actually operate as designed.
- The board of directors is responsible for governing the enterprise.
- Example: a board approves strategy, then reviews management reports on results.

### Relationships and aims
- Definition: governance is a set of relationships among management, board, shareholders and stakeholders.
- Use: it provides the structure for setting objectives and monitoring performance.
- Audit impact: unclear relationships weaken accountability and make findings hard to assign.
- Aims: trust, transparency and accountability.
- Long-term goals: investment, financial stability and business integrity.

### Government use of governance frameworks
- Governments use governance frameworks to reduce inaccurate financial reporting.
- Many regulations require senior management to sign off on internal control adequacy.
- Reports often include an assessment of internal controls.
- Audit impact: auditors test the evidence behind management's control sign-off.
- Example: a CFO certifies controls over financial reporting each year.

### COBIT 2019 figure 2.1: components of a governance system
- Definition: figure 2.1 shows the building blocks that make up a governance system.
- Use: it reminds you governance is more than processes alone.
- Audit impact: auditors check all components, including culture and people, not only documents.
- The seven components are listed under 2.2.1 COBIT 2019.

## 2.2.1 Enterprise Governance of Information and Technology (EGIT)

### Concept
- Definition: EGIT is governance applied to how IT is used in the enterprise.
- Use: it lets all stakeholders influence IT decisions.
- Audit impact: auditors assess whether governance exists, works and is evidenced.
- IT governance: evaluate stakeholder needs, then set direction. 📓
- Stakeholder involvement drives effective governance. 📓
- Stakeholders include the board, management, internal customers and finance.
- The board is ultimately responsible for IT governance. 📓⭐
- The manual says EGIT is the responsibility of the board and executive management.
- Senior management, the CIO and the steering committee support the board. 📓
- Board looks to management to implement systems and IT controls.
- EGIT is stewardship of IT resources for all stakeholders.
- Stewardship means protecting resources entrusted to you by others.
- Example: shareholders expect IT spending to deliver promised returns.

### Purpose of EGIT
- IT aligns with and supports enterprise objectives.
- Promised benefits are realized.
- IT enables the enterprise by exploiting opportunities and maximizing benefits.
- IT resources are used responsibly.
- IT-related risk is managed appropriately.

### Two core issues
- Issue one: IT delivers value to the business.
- Value delivery is driven by strategic alignment of IT with the business.
- Issue two: IT risk is managed.
- Risk management is driven by embedding accountability into the enterprise.
- Definition: strategic alignment means IT goals and investments serve business goals.
- Strategic alignment is a critical success factor.
- Key element: business-IT alignment leads to business value.
- IT moved from enabler of strategy to integral part of strategy.
- Audit impact: misalignment is a governance finding, even when IT runs smoothly.
- Exam traps
  - Accountability for IT value and risk sits with the board, not IT staff.
  - Technical excellence without alignment does not equal good governance.

### EGIT overall processes
- IT resource management: keep an updated inventory of all IT resources.
- Resource management also addresses the risk management process.
- Performance measurement: confirm resources perform and deliver value.
- Deviations from performance indicators can signal risk early.
- Compliance management: meet legal, regulatory and contractual requirements.
- Audit impact: auditors test inventory completeness, metric quality and compliance evidence.

### Governance vs management (COBIT view)
- Governance evaluates needs, sets direction and monitors performance and compliance.
- Management plans, builds, runs and monitors within that direction.
- The two disciplines differ in activities, structures and purposes.
- EGIT is one domain of enterprise governance.
- Example: board sets risk appetite; IT managers pick controls inside it.
- Exam traps
  - Setting direction is governance; executing and operating are management.
  - Choose "board or governing body" for direction, "management" for execution.

### COBIT 2019 📓

- Use: ensures IT aligns with business, enables benefits, uses resources well, manages risk.
- Audit impact: auditors use it as a benchmark for evaluating governance maturity.
- Goals cascade: stakeholder needs flow down into specific IT objectives. 📓

- Design factors tailor the governance system to enterprise context. ➕

### Other governance frameworks
- ⚠ Check: the manual lists these near 2.10 (performance optimization), not in 2.2.1.
- ISO/IEC 38500: principles for governing bodies on effective, efficient, acceptable IT use. ➕
- Definition: ISO/IEC 27000 series is a set of information security program practices.
- Audit impact: ISO/IEC 27001 is a common benchmark for security program audits.
- ITIL: detailed guidance for operational IT service management and value delivery.
- Use of ITIL: run services consistently and deliver business value.
- O-ISM3: process-based maturity model for information security management.
- ISO/IEC 20000: service management specification aligned with ITIL.
- ISO 31000:2018: guidelines and common approach for enterprise risk management.
- Exam traps
  - ISO/IEC 38500 targets governing bodies, not day-to-day technical staff.
  - ITIL and ISO/IEC 20000 cover service operations, not board oversight.

## 2.2.2 Good Practices for EGIT

### Core good practices
- Strategic alignment ensures effective, valuable governance. 📓⭐
- Documented accountability ensures proper execution. 📓
- Measure effectiveness through stakeholder involvement. 📓
- Value delivery, risk management, resource optimization, performance measurement. ➕
- Audit impact: auditors ask who is accountable, and where that is written.
- Exam traps
  - Best proof of governance effectiveness is stakeholder involvement, not tool purchases.
  - Budget, documentation and technology are enablers, not the main driver.
  - Best single step to improve alignment is top management involvement.

### Factors driving EGIT importance
- Boards demand better return from IT investment.
- IT expenditure keeps rising.
- Regulatory control requirements apply to privacy and financial reporting.
- Examples named: SOX, Basel Accords, GDPR and sector rules.
- Basics: SOX covers US financial reporting; GDPR covers EU personal data.
- Basel Accords are banking-sector standards. ➕
- Selecting providers and managing outsourcing and acquisition, such as cloud.
- Adopting control frameworks and good practices reduces risk and adds value.
- Standardized approaches optimize cost versus custom-built ones.
- Frameworks are maturing and gaining acceptance.
- Benchmarking compares performance against standards and peers.

### Evaluate, direct, monitor processes
- These processes run end-to-end inside governance.
- They focus on conformance and performance.
- They focus on the system of internal controls.
- They focus on compliance with external requirements.
- Definition: conformance means following rules; performance means achieving results.
- Audit impact: auditors need evidence for both conformance and performance.

## 2.2.3 Audit's Role in EGIT

### Why audit matters
- Audit helps implement EGIT successfully.
- Audit gives leading-practice recommendations to senior management.
- Audit monitors compliance with EGIT initiatives.
- Monitoring EGIT metrics needs an independent and balanced view.
- EGIT reporting is at the highest enterprise level.
- EGIT audits may cross divisional, functional or departmental lines.
- Example: auditing IT investment spending across several business units.

### Audit charter and terms of reference
- Definition: the audit charter states audit's authority to move freely in the enterprise.
- Use: it permits EGIT engagements with more detailed boundaries.
- Audit impact: no charter means no clear right to access or report.
- The audit committee approves the charter.
- Terms of reference should state the scope, covering areas and issues.
- Terms should name the reporting line to the highest level.
- Terms should grant access to information, including third-party providers.
- Auditor status and skills must suit the audit's nature.
- If insufficient, management should hire an independent third party.
- Exam traps
  - Charter approval comes from the audit committee, not IT management.
  - Insufficient auditor skill leads to outside help, not proceeding anyway.

### Aspects the IS auditor assesses
- Alignment of enterprise governance and EGIT.
- Alignment of IT with mission, vision, values, objectives and strategies.
- Achievement of performance objectives, meaning effectiveness and efficiency.
- Legal, environmental, information quality, fiduciary, security and privacy requirements.
- The control environment.
- Inherent risk in the IS environment.
- IT investment and expenditure.

### Three Lines Model
- Definition: the IIA model splitting risk roles into three lines under a governing body.
- Use: clarifies who owns, oversees and assures controls.
- Audit impact: auditors sit in line three and must stay independent.
- First line: enterprise management owns processes, controls and metrics.
- First line identifies weaknesses through controls, metrics and reviews.
- Second line: risk management independently evaluates known and emerging risk.
- Second line partners with the first to document and mitigate risk.
- Second line may advise but not overrule management decisions.
- Third line: independent audit tests and gives assurance.
- Auditors set their own audit programs and scope.
- Third line has a separate reporting line to the audit committee.
- Governing body roles: integrity, leadership and transparency.
- Example: finance runs a control, risk monitors it, audit tests both.
- IS audit assures the enterprise acts on risks blocking its objectives.
- ⚠ Check: the manual still calls them "lines of defense" in some wording.
- Exam traps
  - Independence belongs to the third line; second-line advice is not assurance.
  - Control ownership sits with management, never with auditors.

## 2.2.4 Information Security Governance

### Effective information security governance
- Definition: a subset of corporate governance directing security activities.
- Use: ensures security risk is managed and resources used responsibly.
- Audit impact: auditors check security objectives trace to business objectives.
- Security must support business activities to be of value.
- Value drivers: confidentiality, integrity, availability, continuity, asset protection.
- Why significant: global networking and rapid technological change.
- Why significant: IT dependence, sophisticated threats and an extended enterprise.
- Executive sponsorship gives authority and funding. 📓⭐
- Exam traps
  - Lack of executive sponsorship is the usual root cause of weak security programs.
  - Security governance is led from the top, not from IT operations.

### Governance framework elements
- A security strategy linked to business objectives.
- Governing policies covering strategy, controls and regulation.
- Standards for each policy, so procedures comply.
- A security organization free of conflicts of interest.
- Institutionalized monitoring processes for compliance and feedback.
- Audit impact: a missing element is a framework gap to report.

### Accountability and policy approval
- Board and CEO are accountable for information security governance.
- The CEO answers to the board and acts through executive management.
- Policy approvers come from varied operations and staff functions.
- Varied approvers reduce bias toward one priority, technology overhead or security.
- Board-level approvers may include CEO, COO, CFO, CRO, CIO, CTO, HR, audit, legal.
- Policy approval should rest on consensus where possible.
- Exam traps
  - Policy approval belongs to senior management, not the security team alone.

### Information security program
- Definition: activities giving assurance that assets get suitable protection.
- Protection is commensurate with asset value or compromise risk.
- Information is protected wherever created, processed, stored and disposed.
- Scope extends to outsourcing, cloud, onshoring and offshoring.
- Audit impact: auditors confirm third-party and cloud data fall inside the program.

### Outcomes of effective governance
- Strategic alignment.
- Risk management.
- Compliance.
- Value delivery.

### Enablers
- Performance measurement: report on security processes against SMART objectives.
- SMART means specific, measurable, attainable, realistic and timely.
- Metrics are defined, agreed and aligned with strategy.
- Measurement exposes shortcomings and tracks fixes.
- Independent assurance comes from external assessments and audits.
- Resource management: use security knowledge and infrastructure efficiently.
- Capture knowledge and document security processes and practices.
- Security architecture defines efficient infrastructure use.
- Process integration: avoid siloed security activities.
- Silos with separate reporting make integration hard.

### What executives strive for
- High-quality information for decisions.
- Business value from IT-enabled investments.
- Operational excellence through reliable, efficient technology.
- IT risk kept at an acceptable level.
- Optimized IT cost.
- Compliance with laws, regulations, contracts and policies.

## 2.2.5 Information Systems Strategy

- IT plans must align with the business plan. 📓⭐
- IT strategy must support business objectives. 📓
- Top management involvement improves alignment. 📓⭐
- Lack of top management support leads to misalignment. 📓
- Boards cannot leave IS strategy only to functional management.
- Reason: operations now depend almost entirely on information systems.
- Threats include resource abuse, cybercrime, fraud, errors and omissions.
- IS strategic processes give reasonable assurance existing and emerging goals are met.
- They also support competitive advantage.
- Audit impact: auditors check top management shaped IT strategy.
- Example: a retailer's board reviews cloud strategy before approving spend.
- Exam traps
  - Best way to improve alignment is senior involvement, not more IT staff.
  - Delegating strategy wholly to IT management is the classic governance failure.

## 2.2.6 Strategic Planning

### Concept
- Definition: long-term direction for using IT to improve business processes. 📓
- Use: identifies cost-effective IT solutions and acquires needed resources.
- Audit impact: auditors verify plans exist, align and get updated.
- IT needs both long- and short-term plans. 📓⭐
- Strategic plans are long-term; tactical are shorter; operational are day-to-day. 📓
- Approved suppliers support the long-term strategy. 📓
- Software testing methodology is operational. 📓
- ⚠ Check: your note says tactical; source rationale says operational or project-level. 📓
- Business plan compatibility review is the key alignment test. 📓⭐
- In a short-range plan review, link business objectives to IS goals. 📓
- IT Balanced Scorecard maps IT metrics to business objectives. 📓⭐
- Enterprise architecture also supports alignment. 📓
- ⚠ Check: the manual covers the balanced scorecard in 2.10 and architecture in 2.4.
- Example: a three-year cloud roadmap versus this quarter's migration project plan.
- Exam traps
  - A strategic plan answer concerns long-term direction, not testing or project tasks.
  - Short-range plans are judged on tying business objectives to IS goals.
  - A strategic planning methodology is reviewed at strategy level, not tactical level.

### Strategic planning practice
- Planning is under top management responsibility.
- It identifies cost-effective IT solutions and action plans.
- Key players: IT management, IT steering committee and strategy committee.
- Traditional cycle: every three to five years.
- Agile strategy: continuous planning with shorter horizons.
- Volatile environments need more frequent reviews.
- Monitor customer, technology, regulatory and geopolitical shifts.
- Balance agility against stability and long-term vision.

### Requirements for new and revised systems
- Consider strategic intentions, objectives, initiatives and needed IT capabilities.
- Review the existing portfolio for functional fit, cost and risk.
- Assess IT capacity: infrastructure and support processes.
- Support processes include project management, development, maintenance, security administration, help desk.
- Consider ROI on existing IT and legacy decommissioning.
- Balance maintenance cost of existing systems against new initiative cost.

### Audit considerations
- Check a strategic planning process or framework exists.
- Check how operational, tactical and development plans feed IT strategy.
- Check plan contents, updating, communication and monitoring.
- Check how the CIO or senior IT management shapes business strategy.
- Lack of IT involvement in business strategy risks misalignment.
- Exam traps
  - Biggest risk of weak top management support is IT strategy misalignment.

## 2.2.7 Business Intelligence

### Concept
- Definition: tools and data giving management insight for planning decisions.
- Use: supports strategic planning with organized data.
- Audit impact: auditors test data quality, since bad data drives bad decisions.
- Data warehouse and data mart are covered under the EDFA layers below.
- ➕ Data lake: raw storage pool; the manual's 2.2.7 does not cover it.
- ➕ ETL: extract, transform, load; the manual describes this as staging.
- Definition: OLAP is online analytical processing, a multidimensional data cube.
- Use of OLAP: fast analysis of precalculated values.

### BI benefits
- Identify trends and patterns.
- Understand customer behavior.
- Assess performance and set realistic goals.
- Make better decisions.

### Typical BI measurement areas
- Process cost, efficiency and quality.
- Customer satisfaction.
- Customer profitability and its predictors.
- Staff and business unit KPI achievement.
- Risk management: unusual transactions and incident or loss statistics.

### Drivers of BI interest
- Size and complexity of modern enterprises.
- Pursuit of competitive advantage through ERP, cloud and supply chain.
- Legal requirements to report across all accounts and suspicious patterns.
- Example: a bank must report all accounts and transactions per customer.

### Data architecture
- A complete data architecture has two components.
- Component one: enterprise data flow architecture (EDFA).
- Component two: logical data architecture.
- Logical architecture is built in stages, by business domain.
- Factors: transaction types, participating entities and business hierarchies.
- Seek the most granular (atomic) data, since storage is cheap.
- Reason: summarizing loses attributes needed for later analysis.

### EDFA layers
- Presentation/desktop access: spreadsheets, queries, BSCs and dashboards.
  - Power users build their own queries and reports.
  - Ad hoc queries ➕
  - ⚠ Check: the manual says direct querying, not "ad hoc" queries.
- Data source: operational, external and nonoperational data.
- Core data warehouse: large relational store of normalized data for reporting.
- Data mart: subset for one business unit, relational or OLAP cube.
- Data staging and quality: copy, transform, quality-check, load reliable data only.
- Staging must handle changes like reused account numbers.
- Data access: connects storage to sources and hides source organization.
- Data preparation: assembles data for marts and precalculates OLAP values.
- Data mining explores large data volumes for patterns and trends.
- Indexing layer: appears in figure 2.3 but is not described. ➕
- Metadata repository: data about data, with business context.
- It documents transformation and validation rules.
- Warehouse management: schedules build and maintenance, and administers security.
- Application messaging: carries data and control messages between layers.
- Internet/intranet: browser interface and TCP/IP networking.
- Audit impact: auditors test staging quality controls and warehouse access security.
- Exam traps
  - Only reliable data should reach the warehouse; quality checks occur at staging.
  - Metadata describes data, not business results.

### Data analysis models
- Context diagrams show major processes and external parties.
- Activity or swim-lane diagrams break down business processes.
- Entity relationship diagrams show data entities and relations.
- Involve business operatives to avoid copying suboptimal existing data configurations.

## 2.2.8 Organizational Structure

### Concept
- Definition: how an enterprise arranges decision-making entities and roles.
- Use: shows who decides, who reports, and who is accountable.
- Audit impact: auditors compare actual structure with documented structure.
- Organizational structure is a key governance component.
- Structures vary by size, industry and location.

### IT governing committees
- Definition: groups that guide IT decisions at board or management level.
- Use: keep IT aligned and give formal oversight.
- Audit impact: auditors review committee charters, minutes and reports.
- There should be a clear split between strategy and steering levels.
- The IT steering committee operates at management level.
- ⚠ Check: the manual calls steering committees executive-level; "management" is outline wording.
- Steering decides overall IT spending and cost allocation.
- Steering aligns and approves enterprise IT architecture.
- Steering approves project plans, budgets, priorities and milestones.
- Steering acquires and assigns resources.
- Steering monitors projects for value, time and budget.
- Steering watches resource and priority conflicts between divisions, IT and projects.
- Steering recommends changes to strategic plans.
- Steering oversees day-to-day IT service delivery and projects.
- Steering keeps projects meeting requirements, reevaluating the business case.
- Steering communicates strategic goals to project teams.
- Steering contributes strongly to management IT governance duties.
- Steering focus: implementation.
- Steering members: sponsoring executive, business executives, CIO, advisors.
- Advisors include IT, audit, legal and finance.
- The IT strategy committee operates at board level.
- Strategy advises the board on alignment, resources, costs, risk and return.
- Strategy advises on IT relevance, major project progress and IT risk exposure.
- Strategy members: board members and non-board specialists.
- Strategy focus: current and future strategic IT issues.
- Board delegates it to give input and prepare strategy approval.
- Strategy contains IT risk and optimizes IT costs, including external sourcing.
- Strategy directs management on IT strategy and drives board strategy.
- Other committees: IT executive, IT governance, IT investment, IT management.
- Auditor must identify whichever group performs these functions, whatever its name.
- Exam traps
  - Strategy committee advises the board; steering committee implements and monitors.
  - Board-level direction versus management-level delivery separates the two.
  - Committee names vary; judge by function, not title.

### Steering committee detail
- A board member who understands IT risk ideally chairs it.
- This is highly desirable but not common practice.
- Its charter defines duties; members hold decision authority for their areas.
- It is a general review board for major IS projects.
- It stays out of routine operations.
- It reviews long- and short-range IT plans.
- It approves major hardware and software acquisitions within board limits.
- It approves and monitors projects, budgets, standards and procedures.
- It approves sourcing strategies: insourcing, outsourcing and offshoring.
- It reviews resource adequacy and allocation.
- It decides centralization versus decentralization.
- It supports the enterprise security program.
- It reports to the board on IS activities.
- Audit impact: auditors confirm approved terms of reference exist.

### Roles and responsibilities of senior management and boards
- Governance needs the board or senior management approving policy.
- It also needs monitoring and review of metrics, reports and trends.
- Board holds ultimate IT governance responsibility. 📓
- Senior management reviews the information security policy. 📓
- Senior business management owns the risk acceptance level. 📓
- Audit impact: auditors verify documented, senior-approved risk acceptance.
- Exam traps
  - Risk acceptance belongs to business management, not IT, security or audit.

#### Board of directors
- Know information assets and their criticality.
- Receive high-level risk assessment and BIA results.
- Approve key assets to protect, supporting a due care standard.
- Definition: due care is acting as a prudent party would.
- Tone at the top: senior management must follow the rules it sets.
- Define, communicate and enforce penalties from the board down.
- The board is accountable and liable for the enterprise.
- Accountable means ensuring legal, ethical conduct and effective resource use.

#### Senior management
- Provide leadership and ongoing support for security governance.
- Integrate security with business process owners.
- Cost-effectiveness depends on how well security supports business objectives.

#### Information security standards committee (ISSC)
- Definition: cross-functional group deliberating security controls and standards.
- Use: builds consensus on priorities and trade-offs.
- Audit impact: auditor presence ensures audit trails and logs exist.
- Members: C-level, IT, application owners, business owners, operations, HR, audit, legal.
- It deliberates control suitability and secure OS and database configuration.
- Legal advises on liability and conflicts with law.
- Membership is not prescriptive and may be adapted.

#### Chief information security officer (CISO)
- Definition: senior role driving the security program.
- Use: ensures policies, standards and procedures are implementable and auditable.
- Audit impact: auditors check the function exists, even without the title.
- The function may be done by the CIO, CTO, CFO or CEO.
- Legal responsibility ultimately rests with senior management and the board.
- The position is increasingly elevated to senior management.
- Failing to recognize this leaves management unaware of liability.

### Matrix of outcomes and responsibilities
- ⚠ Check: the manual's matrix is figure 2.5; RACI appears elsewhere in 2.2.8. ➕
- Definition: RACI shows who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed. ➕
- Outcomes: alignment, risk management, value delivery, resources, performance, process assurance.
- Levels: board, executive management, steering committee, CISO, audit executives.
- The matrix is informational and not specifically tested.
- Roles exist even when titles differ.

### IT organizational structure and responsibilities
- Definition: how IT departments arrange functions and reporting lines.
- Use: aligns IT structure with business requirements. 📓⭐
- Audit impact: org charts reveal roles, reporting lines and SoD. 📓⭐
- IT is headed by an IT manager, director or, in large firms, a CIO.
- Functions: applications, data, technical support, user support, operations, risk management.
- Roles: CIO, CTO, CISO, DBA, security administrator, network administrator and others. ➕
- Roles also include programmer, QA, librarian and help desk. ➕
- The CISA exam does not test specific job responsibilities.
- Universal roles, like owners and executives, may be tested in access questions.
- Exam traps
  - Use the org chart to find reporting lines and incompatible duties.
  - Job duties vary; answer by principle, not by title.

### Data ownership
- Definition: classifying data and assigning duty for confidentiality, completeness and accuracy.
- Use: creates accountability by naming employees responsible for protection.
- Audit impact: auditors check ownership is assigned and owners know it.
- Auditors sample job descriptions against the security policy.
- Auditors review data classification for appropriateness.
- Owners are responsible across the data life cycle, origination to disposal.
- Example: a finance director owns payroll data and approves who can view it.

#### Data owners
- They are managers and directors who use data to run the business.
- They identify and classify data by risk.
- They authorize access.
- They update access rules when personnel change.
- They regularly review access rules.
- They decide protection mechanisms for owned data.

#### Data custodians
- Definition: staff who store and safeguard data for owners.
- Examples: systems analysts and computer operators.
- Custodians do not decide who gets access.

#### Data users
- They are internal and external communities.
- Owners authorize their access.
- The security administrator restricts and monitors that access.
- Users watch for unauthorized people and follow guidelines.

#### Documented authorizations
- Data access is authorized in writing.
- Auditors sample authorizations for proper written authority.
- Where owners exist, only owners give written authority.

#### Security administrator
- Provides physical and logical security for programs, data and equipment.
- Physical security may be handled by someone else.
- Operates under the information security policy.
- Exam traps
  - Owner authorizes access; custodian stores; administrator implements and monitors.
  - Data owners are business managers, not IT staff.

#### New IT users
- Definition: users assigned IT resources sign a statement of obligations.
- Read and follow security policies.
- Keep IDs and passwords secret.
- Create quality passwords per policy.
- Lock screens when idle.
- Report suspected violations.
- Maintain physical security.
- Conform to laws and regulations.
- Use IT resources only for authorized business.

### IT roles and responsibilities
- The org chart defines hierarchy and authority.
- Job descriptions, RACI charts and swimlane diagrams clarify roles.
- Auditors observe whether formal descriptions match actual practice.
- Audit impact: mismatch between paper and practice signals control weakness.
- Systems development manager: manages programmers and analysts.
- Project management: plans and executes IS projects.
- Projects may report to a project management office.
- Project managers report progress to the steering committee.
- Help desk (service desk): answers user technical questions and problems.
- Record problems reported, solved and escalated, then analyze them.
- Help desk acquires hardware and software for end users.
- Help desk assists and trains end users.
- Help desk monitors technical developments.
- Help desk finds production problem sources and starts corrective action.
- Help desk initiates efficiency changes.
- Audit impact: help desk logs reveal recurring failures and user support quality.
- End user: operates business application services.
- End-user support manager: liaison between IT and end users.
- Data management: owns data architecture and treats data as a corporate asset.
- QA manager: facilitates quality activities across IT.

#### Information security management
- It is separate from IT, headed by a CISO.
- The CISO may report to the CIO or by dotted line.
- Conflict: the CIO values cost and continuity.
- The CISO values protection, even at higher cost.
- Exam traps
  - Best independence: security outside IT, not reporting to the operations head.

#### Infrastructure operations and maintenance
- The operations manager runs data center staff.
- Staff include operators, librarians, schedulers and data control.
- The control group collects, converts and controls input.
- The control group balances and distributes output.
- It works in a separate area for authorized staff only.
- Reason: it handles sensitive data.

#### Media management
- It records, issues, receives and safeguards removable media files.
- Tools track inventory, movement, version control and configuration.
- Audit impact: auditors check media inventories and issue records.

#### Data entry
- Entry may be batch or online.
- User departments often enter data directly.
- Controls make data validated, accurate, complete and authorized.

#### Supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA)
- Definition: centralized monitoring and control of large industrial sites.
- Use: oversee plants and power facilities spread over wide areas.
- Audit impact: internet connectivity exposes critical infrastructure to cyberattack.
- Site control is automated by RTUs or PLCs.
- Operators use an HMI to supervise or override controls.
- Data feeds a history log for trending and analysis.
- Example: oil rig systems measure extraction, temperature and water flow.

#### Systems administration
- It maintains multiuser systems across LAN, WLAN, WAN, PAN, SAN, intranets, extranets, mainframes.
- It adds workstations and peripherals.
- It sets up user accounts.
- It installs systemwide software.
- It runs anti-virus procedures.
- It allocates storage.
- Mainframe shops may call it systems programmer.

#### Security administration
- It begins with management commitment and written policy.
- Ideally a full-time employee, reporting to the infrastructure director.
- Small firms may not afford a full-time administrator.
- It maintains access rules.
- It controls issuing of user IDs and passwords.
- It monitors violations and takes corrective action.
- It reviews security policy periodically.
- It runs the security awareness program.
- It tests the security architecture.
- It works with compliance, risk and audit.

#### Database administration (DBA)
- Definition: custodian role that defines and maintains database structures.
- Use: protects shared data and keeps databases performing.
- Audit impact: DBA can bypass controls, so oversight is essential.
- Specifies and changes the physical data definition.
- Selects and tests optimization tools.
- Educates programmers on database structures.
- Implements definition, access, update and concurrency controls.
- Monitors usage, collects statistics and tunes performance.
- Defines backup and recovery procedures.
- DBA can override controls and access all data.
- Prohibiting DBA access to production data is usually impractical.
- Compensating controls: SoD, management approval, supervisor log review, detective tool controls.
- Exam traps
  - Answer is compensating control, not removing DBA access entirely.

#### Systems analyst
- Designs systems from user needs.
- Writes requirements, functional specifications and high-level designs.
- Usually involved in the early SDLC phase.

#### Security architect
- Evaluates security technologies.
- Designs network, access control and identity management security.
- Sets security policies and requirements.
- Deliverables differ from the systems analyst's program specifications.
- Works with compliance, risk and audit.

#### System security engineer (ISO/IEC 21827)
- Covers project life cycles through decommissioning.
- Covers whole enterprises.
- Interacts with other disciplines.
- Interacts with other enterprises for acquisition, certification and evaluation.

#### Applications development and maintenance
- Staff work only in a test environment.
- Another group moves changes to production.
- Audit impact: developers with production access break SoD.

#### Infrastructure development and maintenance
- Staff maintain systems software and the OS.
- Broad access requires tamper-resistant electronic logs.
- Access is limited to libraries of software they maintain.
- Domain admin and superuser accounts need tight control and monitoring.

### Vendor and outsourcer management
- Dedicated staff manage multiple vendors and outsourcers.
- They act as prime contact within IT.
- They direct the outsourcer and escalate issues.
- They monitor and report service levels.
- They review contract changes and obtain IT approval.

### Network management
- Dispersed IPFs use LANs, WANs and wireless networks.
- Administrators manage routers, switches, firewalls, segmentation, performance and remote access.
- They report to the IPF director or end-user manager.
- A dotted line to the IPF director is advisable.
- They ensure links work, backups occur and purchases are authorized.
- The LAN administrator should have no application programming duties.

### Separation of duties within IT
- Definition: splitting critical tasks so no one person controls everything.
- Use: prevents unauthorized actions and delays in spotting errors. 📓⭐
- Audit impact: reviewing access control policy checks incompatible duties. 📓⭐
- Duties to segregate: custody of assets, authorization, recording transactions.
- Without SoD: asset misappropriation.
- Without SoD: misstated financials and inaccurate documentation.
- Without SoD: undetected improper fund use or data change.
- Without SoD: undetected unauthorized program or data changes.
- Auditor documents job relationships and assesses SoD adequacy.
- SoD limits access to production data, programs, documentation and utilities.
- The SoD control matrix is a guideline, not an industry standard.
- It identifies conflicts so you can ask about compensating controls.
- Automated tools test access against an SoD matrix.
- Predefined matrices must be tailored to the enterprise.
- Remote logging keeps privileged users from reaching their own logs.
- Example: DBA activity logs go to a server reviewed by someone else.
- Compensating controls consider enterprise size and structure.
- Log developer access to production and review it independently. 📓⭐
- Exam traps
  - Combined roles require compensating controls; they are not automatically banned.
  - Best compensating control pairs logging with independent review.
  - Access control policy review confirms separation of conflicting duties.

### SoD control mechanisms
- Transaction authorization sits with the user department.
- Management and audit check periodically for unauthorized entries.
- Asset ownership is assigned in writing to the data owner.
- Access to data uses physical, system and application security layers.
- Decisions rest on policy, SoD and least privilege.
- Definition: least privilege means granting only access needed for the job.
- Sensitivity levels: top secret, secret, confidential, unclassified.
- Labels show sensitivity of stored documents.
- Controls are mandatory or discretionary, based on policy.
- Authorization forms are formal, management-approved access requests.
- Signature authorization logs support large or remote sites.
- Privileges are reviewed periodically.
- User authorization tables define who can update, modify, delete or view data.
- Levels: system, transaction and field.
- Secure tables with passwords or encryption.
- A control log is reviewed and exceptions investigated.
- Audit impact: auditors compare requests to signature logs and authorization tables.

### Compensating controls for lack of SoD (detail)
- Audit trails show who, when, type, fields and files updated.
- Audit trails let reviewers recreate the transaction flow.
- Reconciliation is done by user department or data control group.
- Exception reporting needs evidence of supervisor handling, like initials.
- Transaction logs are manual or automated.
- Supervisory reviews use observation, inquiry or remote means.
- Independent reviews matter most in small organizations.
- Evaluate system-generated reports before relying on them as controls.
- Small IT teams of four or five rely heavily on compensating controls.

## 2.2.9 Auditing the IT Governance Structure and Implementation

### Reviewing documentation
- Review IT strategies, plans and budgets.
- Review security policy documentation.
- Review organization and functional charts.
- Review job descriptions.
- Review IT steering committee reports.
- Review system development and program change procedures.
- Review operations procedures.
- Review HR manuals.
- Review QA procedures.
- Check documents were created as management authorized.
- Check documents are current and up to date.
- Unclear roles: recommend defining accountability. 📓⭐
- Governance effectiveness is measured by stakeholder involvement. 📓
- Exam traps
  - Undocumented roles call for documenting and assigning accountability first.
  - Effectiveness is gauged through stakeholder involvement, not committee count.

### Indicators of potential problems
- Excessive costs.
- Budget overruns.
- Late projects.
- High staff turnover.
- Inexperienced staff.
- Frequent hardware or software errors.
- Excessive backlog of user requests.
- Slow computer response time.
- Aborted or suspended development projects.
- Unsupported or unauthorized purchases.
- Frequent upgrades.
- Extensive exception reports.
- Exception reports not followed up.
- Lack of succession plans.
- Reliance on one or two key personnel.
- Lack of adequate training.
- Audit impact: these are warning signs, so expand testing when found.

## Takeaway

- The board is ultimately responsible; management and committees support it.
- Strategic alignment and top management involvement drive effective IT governance.
- Strategy committee advises the board; steering committee oversees delivery.
- SoD gaps need compensating controls: logging plus independent review.
- Data owners authorize; custodians store; administrators implement and monitor.
- Auditors stay independent as the third line and test governance evidence.
