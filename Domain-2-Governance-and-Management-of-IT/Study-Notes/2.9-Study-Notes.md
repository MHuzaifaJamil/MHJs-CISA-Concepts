## 2.9 Introduction: Why vendor risk matters

### Basic terms

- **Vendor (third party)**
  - Definition: An outside company that supplies products or services to your enterprise.
  - Use: Supplies software, hardware, cloud, staff, or managed services.
  - Audit impact: Its weaknesses become your risk exposure.
- **Third-party risk**
  - Definition: Risk that arises because an outside party touches your systems or data.
  - Use: Drives vendor screening, contract terms, and ongoing oversight.
  - Audit impact: Auditors test whether it is identified, owned, and monitored.
- **Vendor access**
  - Definition: The network, system, or data permissions given to a vendor.
  - Use: Lets vendors deliver support, maintenance, or hosted services.
  - Audit impact: Excess or lingering access is a common breach path.

### Core ideas

- More reliance on vendors raises the chance of incidents and breaches.
- Many major recent breaches began through vendor access to enterprise networks.
- The enterprise must actively manage third-party risk before customers or operations suffer.
- Example: A maintenance vendor's stolen remote login lets attackers enter the corporate network.

### Best practices for managing vendor risk

- Run a risk assessment on every potential vendor before awarding a contract.
- Put right-to-audit clauses in all vendor contracts.
- Give vendors minimum access only (least privilege).
- Keep an accurate inventory of vendors and their access levels.
- Remove vendor access promptly when the relationship ends.
- Monitor and certify all vendors on a regular schedule.
- Audit impact: Each practice maps to a testable control with evidence.
- Example: Comparing vendor inventory to active accounts reveals a terminated vendor still enabled.

**Exam traps**

- Best first step is assessing risk before contract award, not after go-live.
- "Trust the vendor's reputation" is wrong; verification and audit rights are required.
- Least privilege applies to vendors too, not only employees.
- Distractor: one-time certification; the manual says certify and monitor regularly.

## 2.9.1 Sourcing practices

### What sourcing means

- **Sourcing**
  - Definition: Choosing how the enterprise obtains the IT functions it needs.
  - Use: Decides which functions run in-house and which go to others.
  - Audit impact: Poor sourcing decisions create cost, control, and continuity risk.
- Strategy is decided per IT function, not for the whole department at once.
- Evaluate IT objectives and the current staffing model first.

### Sourcing questions

- Is this a core function of the enterprise?
- Does it hold critical knowledge, processes, or staff that cannot be replicated elsewhere?
- Can another party do it at same or lower price?
- Same or higher quality, with no added risk, is also required.
- Does the enterprise have experience managing third parties or offshore sites?
- Do contracts or regulation restrict offshore locations or foreign nationals?
- Example: A bank keeps credit-scoring logic in-house because it differentiates the business.

### Approval

- **IT steering committee**
  - Definition: Senior group that guides and prioritizes IT decisions.
  - Use: Reviews and approves the sourcing decision once agreed.
  - Audit impact: Missing approval signals a governance gap.

### Outsourcing process steps

- Define the IT function to be outsourced.
- Describe service levels required and minimum metrics.
- Know desired knowledge, skills, and quality of the provider.
- Know current in-house cost to compare with bids.
- Conduct due diligence reviews of potential providers.
- Confirm architectural needs for contractual or regulatory requirements.
- Communicate the decision and monitoring requirements to internal stakeholders.
- Same process applies when outsourcing offshore.
- Audit impact: Missing in-house cost baseline makes bid comparison unreliable.

### Delivery models

- **Insourced**
  - Definition: Enterprise staff fully perform the function.
  - Use: Keeps control of core or sensitive work.
  - Audit impact: Highest control, but staff skill gaps may exist.
- **Outsourced**
  - Definition: Vendor staff fully perform the function.
  - Use: Buys expertise or cost savings.
  - Audit impact: Control shifts to contract and monitoring.
- **Hybrid**
  - Definition: Mix of enterprise and vendor staff.
  - Use: Includes joint ventures and supplemental staff.
  - Audit impact: Unclear role boundaries cause accountability gaps.

### Location models

- **Onsite**
  - Definition: Staff work in the enterprise IT department.
  - Use: Direct supervision and easy access control.
  - Audit impact: Lowest distance and oversight risk.
- **Offsite (nearshore)**
  - Definition: Staff work remotely in the same area.
  - Use: Similar time zone and legal setting at lower cost.
  - Audit impact: Remote access controls need testing.
- **Offshore**
  - Definition: Staff work in a different geographic region.
  - Use: Uses time zones and lower labor rates.
  - Audit impact: Adds legal, tax, continuity, and cross-border data risk.

### What the auditor must understand

- Scope of vendor services: COTS products, outsourced services, cloud, managed services.
- **COTS (commercial off-the-shelf)**
  - Definition: Ready-made products bought rather than built.
  - Use: Fast, standard capability.
  - Audit impact: Limited code access raises need for escrow and support terms.
- **SLA (service level agreement)**
  - Definition: Contract part setting service levels and support.
  - Use: Defines operational and technical support commitments.
  - Audit impact: Auditor checks SLAs address operational and support needs.
- Also consider supplier financial viability and licensing scalability.
- **Software escrow**
  - Definition: Source code held by a neutral third party.
  - Use: Released to customer if vendor fails or stops support.
  - Audit impact: Protects continuity; auditor confirms the agreement exists.
  - ⚠ Check: Release triggers and verification testing are common practice, not in manual ➕.
- Example: Small vendor goes bankrupt; escrowed source lets the client maintain the application.

### RFP, vendor selection, contract basics

- **RFP (request for proposal)**
  - Definition: Document asking vendors to bid against stated requirements.
  - Use: Sets requirements specifications used to pick a vendor.
  - Audit impact: Security and other controls must be specified in it.
- Auditors are not legal or contract auditors, yet must grasp these essentials.
- Vendor selection must ensure a reliable, professional vendor is chosen.
- Contract needs an escrow agreement where appropriate.
- Contract must address the right to audit.
- Contract sets expectations for assurance testing by a trusted third party.
- Example: Annual SOC report from an external auditor for a cloud provider.
- ⚠ Check: Outline gives "Vendor selection essentials" no detail; manual says only reliable, professional.

**Exam traps**

- Auditor role is understanding requirements, not drafting legal contracts.
- Cheapest bid is not the answer; quality, risk, and viability matter equally.
- Outsourcing a function does not remove the need for in-house cost baseline.
- Steering committee approves sourcing; the auditor does not decide it.
- Nearshore is same area, offshore is different region; do not swap them.

## 2.9.2 Outsourcing practices and strategies

### Core principle

- **Outsourcing**
  - Definition: Handing control of IT functions to an external party by contract.
  - Use: Provider supplies the resources and expertise for the agreed service.
  - Audit impact: Control framework must be revisited after outsourcing.
- Service delivery transfers; accountability stays with client management. 📓⭐
- Transparency and ownership of decisions stay within the client.
- Ultimate responsibility for outsourced information stays with the enterprise.
- Outsourcing is a strategic decision, not merely a procurement decision.
- Enterprise reconfigures its value chain: retain core, outsource noncore.
- Example: A retailer outsources payroll processing but remains liable for payroll errors.

### Reasons for outsourcing

- Focus on core activities.
- Pressure on profit margins.
- Competition demanding cost savings and faster time-to-market.
- Flexibility in organization, structure, and market size.

### Advantages, disadvantages, risk (figure 2.13)

- Possible advantages:
  - Economies of scale from reusable component software.
  - Vendor staff devote more time and focus to a project.
  - Vendors have wider experience with problems and techniques.
  - Specifications and contracts are likely better than in-house alone.
  - Scope creep is less likely because vendors resist diversions.
- Possible disadvantages and business risk:
  - Costs exceeding customer expectations.
  - Loss of internal IT experience.
  - Loss of control over IT.
  - Vendor failure.
  - Limited product access.
  - Deficient legal and regulatory compliance.
  - Contract terms not being met.
  - Lack of contractor loyalty to the customer.
  - Disgruntled customers or employees.
  - Uncompetitive service costs.
  - Obsolete vendor IT systems.
  - Anticipated benefits not received.
  - Reputational damage from project failures.
  - Lengthy, expensive litigation.
  - Loss or leakage of information or processes.
- Risk reduction options:
  - Measurable, shared goals and rewards under the partnership.
  - Software escrow to keep software maintainable.
  - Multiple suppliers, or withholding work as an incentive.
  - Periodic competitive reviews and benchmarking.
  - Short-term contracts.
  - Cross-functional contract management team.
  - Contract provisions covering foreseeable contingencies.
- Auditor checks that management weighed these before choosing the strategy.

### Outsourcing contract provisions

- Service quality expectations, using ISO/IEC TR 33015, CMMI, ITIL, or ISO methods.
- Access control and security administration for both enterprise and vendor.
- Violation reporting and follow-up, ideally tied to SLAs.
- Owner notification and cooperation with investigations.
- Change and version control with testing in implementation and production.
- Clear network control responsibilities between the parties.
- Specific performance parameters, such as minimum transaction processing times.
- Capacity management criteria.
- Provisions for changing the contract.
- Defined dispute escalation and resolution process.
- **Indemnification**
  - Definition: Vendor compensates you for damage it causes.
  - Use: Shifts financial loss from provider-caused events.
  - Audit impact: Absent indemnity leaves the enterprise bearing losses.
- **NDA (nondisclosure agreement)**
  - Definition: Legal promise to keep shared information confidential.
  - Use: Protects both parties' sensitive information.
  - Audit impact: Confirm NDAs cover vendor staff and subcontractors.
- **Right to audit** ⭐
  - Definition: Contract clause letting you inspect the vendor's operations.
  - Use: Covers facilities, records, copies, personnel, and computerized files.
  - Audit impact: Without it, auditors cannot verify controls on contracted services.
  - Manual wants it straightforward and unambiguous.
- Business continuity and disaster recovery provisions, with testing.
- Confidentiality, integrity, and availability of enterprise data maintained; data ownership explicit.
- Vendor must comply with all legal requirements, including those enacted later.
- Ownership of intellectual property developed for the customer stated.
- Intellectual property rights protected.
- Explicit warranty and maintenance periods.
- Software escrow provisions.
- Clear roles and responsibilities between the parties.
- Vendor follows enterprise policies unless pre-agreed otherwise.
- Vendor identifies all subcontractors; enterprise approves changes.
- Example: Vendor swaps in an unvetted offshore subcontractor without approval, breaching the contract.
- ⚠ Check: Manual itself repeats legal compliance and intellectual property; outline mirrors it.

### Contract must allow independent external security review 📓⭐

- Outsourcing contracts must permit independent external security review.
- Practical form: right-to-audit clause plus third-party assurance reports.
- ⚠ Check: Manual states right-to-audit and third-party assurance; "external review" wording is yours.

### Managing the relationship

- Contract describes means, methods, processes, structure, and quality control.
- Auditor regularly reviews contract and service levels for appropriateness.
- Auditor may review outsourcer procedures and quality program results.
- Quality programs named: ISO/IEC TS 33061, CMMI, ITIL, ISO methods.
- These programs require regular audits to certify standards are met.
- ⚠ Check: TR 33015 (contracts) and TS 33061 (review) both match the manual.
- Choose the supplier carefully; cultural and personnel compatibility matters.
- Decision weighs quality, continuity, control, competitive advantage, technical knowledge.
- Careful choice is vital when outsourcing is a long-term strategy.

### SLAs

- **SLA (service level agreement)**
  - Definition: Contract terms committing a vendor to measurable service levels.
  - Use: Sets service, uptime or downtime, and support commitments.
  - Audit impact: SLA metrics are how auditors monitor outsourced activity. 📓⭐
- Covers required service level and support options.
- Includes guaranteed system performance, uptime or downtime, and customer support.
- Software or hardware requirements are also stipulated.
- Penalty provisions and enforcement options apply for services not provided.
- May include incentives such as bonuses or gain-sharing.
- Above all, an SLA is an instrument of control.
- Example: SLA guarantees 99.9% uptime with credits for each missed hour.
- ⚠ Check: The 99.9% figure is illustrative only, not from manual.

### Legal and benchmarking

- Know cross-border legislation when the vendor is from another country.
- Legal team must review and approve all outsourcing.
- **Industry standards and benchmarking**
  - Definition: Common standards used to judge a vendor's service level.
  - Use: Compare similar arrangements; sources are user groups, publications, associations.
  - Audit impact: Gives an objective yardstick for vendor performance.
- Examples named: ISO 9001:2015 and CMMI.

### Globalization practices and strategies

- **Globalization**
  - Definition: Moving IT functions offsite or offshore, in-house or outsourced.
  - Use: Same drivers as outsourcing, without necessarily outsourcing.
  - Audit impact: Management must actively oversee remote or offshore sites.
- Legal, regulatory, and tax issues: unfamiliar rules in a new country.
- Continuity of operations: BCP and DRP may be poorly designed or untested.
- Personnel: needed personnel policy changes may be overlooked.
- Telecommunication: remote links may suffer outages or security exposures.
- Cross-border and cross-cultural: time zones, languages, and cultures create problems.
- Cross-border data flow may face legal rules, such as encryption in transit.
- Planned globalization and expansion: future plans may affect location choice.
- Example: Offshore call center lacks tested recovery plan and loses service during an outage.

### Outsourcing and third-party audit reports

- **Third-party audit report**
  - Definition: Independent report on a provider's controls.
  - Use: Gives assurance on confidentiality, integrity, and availability.
  - Audit impact: Lets auditors rely on provider controls without visiting.
- Some industries place such audits under regulatory oversight.
- **SSAE 18 and AICPA guide**
  - Definition: US attestation standard framing three SOC report options.
  - Use: Replaced SAS 70, which focused on financial reporting.
  - Audit impact: Auditor must know which report fits the risk.
- **ISAE 3402**
  - Definition: IAASB standard on assurance reports at service organizations.
  - Use: International counterpart for service organization controls reports.
  - Audit impact: Appears in the manual as IAASB guidance.
- **SOC 1**
  - Definition: Report on controls relevant to user entities' financial reporting.
  - Use: Relied on for financial statement audits.
  - Audit impact: Wrong choice when the concern is security or privacy.
- **SOC 2**
  - Definition: Report on security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, privacy.
  - Use: Includes compliance with the provider's privacy practices.
  - Audit impact: Best fit for security and operational assurance.
- **SOC 3**
  - Definition: Like SOC 2 without detail on control design and tests.
  - Use: Easier summary report for wider distribution.
  - Audit impact: Too thin for detailed reliance; use SOC 2 instead.
  - ⚠ Check: "Wider distribution" use is common practice, not in manual ➕.
- ➕ SOC 2 Type I tests design; Type II tests operation over time.
- Other reports: penetration tests and security assessments.
- Example: Cloud provider's annual SOC 2 report is reviewed before renewing a hosting contract.

### Auditor responsibilities for assurance reports

- Understand management assertions and how well they cover services provided.
- Validate assessors were independent, objective, and competent.
- Know the internal process for handling qualified opinions.
- **Qualified opinion**
  - Definition: Assessor reports exceptions or scope limits in the controls reviewed.
  - Use: Signals areas where assurance is weaker.
  - Audit impact: Requires follow-up and management action.
- **Bridge letter**
  - Definition: Provider letter covering the gap since the last report period.
  - Use: Used when the new annual report is late.
  - Audit impact: Bridges time, but is weaker than a full report.
  - ⚠ Check: Manual names bridge letters without detail; definition is common practice ➕.
- Obtain, review, and present report results to management for action.

**Exam traps**

- Accountability never transfers; the client remains responsible for outsourced processes.
- Best assurance for outsourced security: contractual right to external review. 📓⭐
- Provider claiming standards compliance is weaker than independent verification.
- Market reputation and claims of compliance do not replace audit rights.
- Auditor monitors outsourced activity via SLA metrics, not by running the service. 📓⭐
- Auditor's main role is not defining functions, costing, or due diligence.
- SOC 1 is financial reporting; SOC 2 is security-related; SOC 3 lacks detail.
- Vendor policy applies only if the enterprise agreed to it in advance.
- Subcontractor changes need enterprise approval; notification alone is insufficient.
- Short contracts and multiple suppliers reduce lock-in risk; they are not flaws.

## 2.9.3 Cloud governance

### Basics

- **Cloud governance**
  - Definition: Applying governance to services run by cloud providers.
  - Use: Keeps cloud services aligned with strategy, secure, and risk-managed.
  - Audit impact: Third-party setting makes alignment and security harder to verify.
- Strategic direction of business and IT is the primary focus.
- Technical cloud models are covered in Domain 5, not here.

### Governance activities needing cloud consideration

- Goal setting.
- Policy and standards development.
- Roles and responsibilities.
- Risk management.

### Business process changes

- Data processing, development, and information retrieval may change.
- Storage, archiving, and backup processes must be revisited.

### Key issues

- Business units can bypass IT and buy cloud services directly.
- **Shadow IT**
  - Definition: Technology used without IT department knowledge or approval.
  - Use: Business units get faster services, avoiding IT queues.
  - Audit impact: Unvetted cloud services create unmanaged data and risk.
  - ⚠ Check: The manual describes the bypass but does not use the term ➕.
- Policies must cover sourcing, managing, and discontinuing cloud services.
- Assign a designated relationship manager or service management team.
- Ensure the third party assigns compliance checking and enforcement duties.
- Provide enough technical skills and resources to monitor security requirements.
- Act on deficiencies by enforcing SLAs or reassessing vendor viability.
- Keep overall control and visibility of security for sensitive or critical information.
- Keep visibility into change management, vulnerability identification, and incident reporting.
- Reporting process, format, and structure must be clearly defined.
- Example: Marketing signs up for a cloud tool; IT never reviews its security.

### Considerations

- **Cloud risk management strategy**: formal plan for identifying and treating cloud risks.
- Implement strong cybersecurity controls.
- Manage resilience, outsourcing, vendor lock-in, and concentration risk.
- **Vendor lock-in**
  - Definition: Difficulty moving away from a provider once dependent.
  - Use: Planning exit options reduces dependence.
  - Audit impact: Check exit strategy and data portability terms.
  - ⚠ Check: Portability terms are common practice, not stated in manual ➕.
- **Concentration risk**
  - Definition: Too much dependence on a single provider.
  - Use: Spreading services limits single-point failure.
  - Audit impact: One provider outage can halt many business processes.
- Ensure staff have adequate skills to manage cloud risk.
- Ultimate responsibility for information remains with the enterprise.

**Exam traps**

- Moving data to the cloud never transfers responsibility for that data.
- Governance starts with strategy and alignment, not with technical configuration.
- Business-unit cloud purchases outside IT signal a policy gap, not agility.
- Provider failures call for enforcing SLAs or reassessing viability.

## 2.9.4 Governance in outsourcing

### Basics

- **Governance of outsourcing**
  - Definition: Responsibilities, roles, objectives, interfaces, and controls for third-party services.
  - Use: Anticipates change and manages performance, cost, and maintenance.
  - Audit impact: Weak governance leaves assumptions about scope unchecked.
- Client and provider work together on one consistent approach.
- Approach identifies information, relationships, controls, and exchanges among stakeholders.
- Main objective: continuity of service, profitability, added value for both parties.
- Contracts cannot define every detail; governance balances risk, demand, provision, cost.
- Many enterprises assume the proposal includes more than it actually does.

### Extended responsibilities of both parties

- Ensure contractual viability through continuous review and shared benefit.
- Include an explicit governance schedule in the contract.
- Manage the relationship through SLAs and OLAs.
- **OLA (operating level agreement)**
  - Definition: Agreement on service levels between internal support groups.
  - Use: Supports meeting the customer-facing SLA.
  - Audit impact: Check OLAs align with SLA commitments.
  - ⚠ Check: Manual names OLAs without defining them; definition is standard practice ➕.
- Identify and manage stakeholders, relationships, and expectations.
- Set clear roles for decisions, escalation, disputes, demand, and delivery.
- Allocate resources and spending in response to prioritized needs.
- Evaluate performance, cost, user satisfaction, and effectiveness continuously.
- Communicate with all stakeholders on an ongoing basis.
- Governance is pre-planned, built into the contract, and evolves with change.
- Example: Quarterly governance meeting reviews SLA misses and adjusts service priorities.

### Business alignment

- **Business alignment**
  - Definition: IT services closely reflect what business users need.
  - Use: Achieved by outsourcing activities that support noncore services.
  - Audit impact: Alignment is measured with user satisfaction, not only technical metrics.
- Alignment is driven by those who receive the service.
- SLAs and OLAs are measured by performance and user satisfaction.

### Right-to-audit understanding

- Auditor must understand how the contract permits auditing the provider.
- Auditor needs visibility into provider internal controls.
- Controls cover confidentiality, integrity, availability, and preventive, detective, corrective types.
- SLAs for problem management, including incident response, must be documented.
- These SLAs must be communicated to all affected parties.

**Exam traps**

- Governance is shared by client and provider, but accountability stays with client.
- Detailed contract wording cannot replace a governance process.
- Alignment is judged by service recipients, not by the vendor.
- Right to audit without visibility into controls gives weak assurance.

## 2.9.5 Capacity and growth planning

- **Capacity and growth planning**
  - Definition: Planning IT resources and staff for current and future demand.
  - Use: Keeps services and projects on schedule as the business grows.
  - Audit impact: Gaps lead to delays and missed service levels.
- Essential because of IT's strategic importance and constant technology change.
- Must reflect long- and short-range business plans.
- Must be part of the budgeting process.
- Capacity changes should reflect infrastructure changes and available staff numbers.
- Lack of qualified staff can delay critical projects.
- Lack of qualified staff can cause missed agreed service levels.
- That staffing gap may lead enterprises to outsource for growth.
- Example: Hiring shortage delays a rollout, so management contracts a vendor team.

**Exam traps**

- Capacity planning ties to business plans and budget, not only to hardware.
- Staff shortage is a capacity problem too, not just a technology one.

## 2.9.6 Third-party service delivery management

### Basics

- **Service delivery management system**
  - Definition: Process that keeps third-party service and security at agreed levels.
  - Use: Implements and maintains security and service per the agreements.
  - Audit impact: Every enterprise using third parties should have one.
- Enterprise checks agreement implementation.
- Enterprise monitors compliance with agreements.
- Enterprise manages changes so services still meet agreed requirements.

### Monitoring and review of third-party services

- Services, reports, and records are monitored and reviewed; regular audits occur.
- Goal: confirm security terms are met and incidents are handled properly.
- Monitor service performance levels against agreements.
- Review third-party service reports and hold regular progress meetings.
- Share security incident information and review it with the third party.
- Review third-party audit trails and records of events, problems, failures, and disruptions.
- Resolve and manage identified problems.
- Example: Monthly review reveals repeated outages, triggering a corrective action plan.

### Managing changes to third-party services

- Manage changes considering business criticality, and reassess risk.
- Enterprise-side changes:
  - Service enhancements.
  - New applications and systems.
  - Updated policies and procedures.
  - New controls to fix incidents and improve security.
  - IT security policy updates.
- Provider-side changes:
  - Network changes and enhancements.
  - New technologies.
  - New products or newer versions.
  - New development tools and environments.
  - Changes in physical location of service facilities.
  - Change of vendors or subcontractors.
- Audit impact: Unreviewed provider changes can reopen risks already assessed.
- Example: Provider moves data center to another country without notifying the client.

### Service improvement and user satisfaction

- SLAs set the baseline for outsourcer performance.
- Contracts can add improvement expectations with penalties and rewards.
- Examples: fewer help desk calls, fewer system errors, better availability.
- Users and IT agree on improvements to raise satisfaction and meet objectives.
- Monitor satisfaction by interviews and surveys at SLA-aligned intervals.

**Exam traps**

- Monitoring means reviewing reports, audit trails, and incident data, not trusting self-reports.
- Provider changes need risk reassessment, even when the provider calls them minor.
- SLA is the baseline; improvement targets go beyond it with rewards or penalties.
- Satisfaction surveys follow SLA periods, not ad hoc timing.

---

## Takeaway

- Accountability stays with the client, whatever service delivery is outsourced.
- Right to audit and independent review are the key contract controls.
- Monitor vendors using SLA metrics, reports, audit trails, and SOC reports.
- Match report to risk: SOC 1 financial, SOC 2 security, SOC 3 summary.
- Cloud, offshore, and subcontractor changes need reassessed risk and legal review.
- Least privilege, vendor inventory, and prompt access removal limit vendor breach exposure.
