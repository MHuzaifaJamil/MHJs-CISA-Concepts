## 2.8 IT Resource Management (Introduction)

### Limited resources and opportunity cost

- **Resource**: anything an enterprise spends to reach goals, mainly people and money.
  - Definition: the inputs (staff, budget, time) available to do work.
  - Use: management allocates them across competing projects and services.
  - Audit impact: auditor judges whether allocation gives the enterprise the greatest value.
- Resources are always limited, so every choice excludes other choices.
- **Opportunity cost**
  - Definition: the value lost by not pursuing the next-best use of resources.
  - Use: compares competing investments before committing money or staff.
  - Audit impact: ignoring it leads to funding low-value projects.
- Example: funding an ERP upgrade means no budget for a customer portal.
- The auditor must understand the enterprise's investment and allocation practices.

### ROI: financial and nonfinancial benefits

- **ROI (return on investment)**
  - Definition: what the enterprise gains compared with what it spends.
  - Use: justifies and ranks IT investments.
  - Audit impact: narrow ROI hides nonfinancial value and distorts project selection.
- Financial benefits: cost reductions and revenue increases affecting budget and finances.
- Nonfinancial benefits: better operations, strategic results, customer satisfaction, information quality, shorter cycle times.
- Modern leaders weigh both kinds, not only financial ones.
- Where feasible, convert nonfinancial benefits into monetary units for clearer analysis.
- Example: shorter order cycle time is valued as saved labor hours times hourly cost.

### What IT management does

- **IT management**
  - Definition: overseeing IT operations and resources day to day.
  - Use: plans, builds, implements, operates and monitors IT as governance directs.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks activities stay aligned with governance direction.
- Governance sets direction; management executes it.
- Management ensures IT keeps supporting enterprise objectives.

## 2.8.1 Value of IT

- **Value of IT**
  - Definition: how much benefit an IT project gives compared with its cost.
  - Use: selects which IT projects get funded.
  - Audit impact: value claims without alignment to strategy are weak.
- Value is the relationship between cost paid and benefit received.
- Larger benefit relative to cost means greater value.
- IT value depends on strategic alignment 📓⭐
- Example: a cheap tool nobody needs has low value despite low cost.
- **IT portfolio management vs IT financial management**
  - Definition: portfolio management is directive and strategic; financial management tracks money.
  - Use: portfolio decides how much to invest, continue or divest.
  - Audit impact: auditor must not confuse budgeting controls with strategic selection.
- Exam traps
  - Cost alone never defines value; the benefit-to-cost relationship does.
  - Alignment with strategy beats technical sophistication as the value driver.
  - Financial management is not the strategic, directive discipline; portfolio management is.

## 2.8.2 Implementing IT portfolio management

### Purpose and basics

- **IT portfolio**
  - Definition: the full set of IT projects, assets and services the enterprise holds.
  - Use: managed together, like an investment portfolio.
  - Audit impact: missing portfolio view means unprioritized, duplicated or misaligned projects.
- **Investment portfolio analysis** 📓⭐
  - Definition: comparing projects by risk and expected reward.
  - Use: supports prioritizing IT projects.
  - Audit impact: strongest tool for choosing between competing projects.
- It balances IT project risk against financial reward 📓
- It determines whether the enterprise pursues the best IT projects.
- Example: ranking twenty requested projects by risk, cost, benefit and strategic fit.

### Criteria and first step

- Criteria may be financial, strategic or tactical, and can be classified and evaluated.
- Criteria must be comprehensive yet able to change with strategy.
- First practical step: standardize terminology to reduce misunderstandings.

### Initial tasks

- Ensure management commitment and agreed-on targets.
- Plan the portfolio model in line with the management process.
- Specify portfolio inclusion criteria.
- Describe roles, tasks and decisions of those involved.
- Organize tools, support and instructions.

### Implementation methods

- Analyze and assess the risk profile.
- Diversify projects, infrastructure and technologies.
- Align and continuously realign with business objectives.
- Pursue continuous improvement.
- **Diversification**
  - Definition: spreading investments across different projects and technologies.
  - Use: limits exposure to one failure.
  - Audit impact: heavy concentration in one vendor or technology raises risk.

### Mandatory projects and business cases

- Many projects are discretionary; others are mandatory.
- Mandatory examples: regulatory compliance and reducing technical debt.
- **Technical debt**
  - Definition: future rework cost created by quick or outdated technical choices.
  - Use: justifies remediation projects.
  - Audit impact: unmanaged debt raises failure and security risk.
- A documented business case is required even for mandatory projects.
- Completed programs stay in the portfolio; status changes.
- Results are evaluated against the original plans.
- Exam traps
  - Prioritizing new projects points to investment portfolio analysis, not CSA or risk assessment.
  - CSA and audits assess controls; they do not rank project value.
  - Mandatory does not mean exempt from a business case.
  - Do not delete completed programs; closing loses benefit-realization evidence.

## 2.8.3 IT management practices

- IT began as a service or support function for production departments.
- In modern enterprises IT is a critical function integrated throughout operations.
- **IT management practices**
  - Definition: how policies and procedures for IT activities are carried out.
  - Use: turn policy into consistent daily operation.
  - Audit impact: auditor must see that a well-managed IT department supports objectives.
- Auditors review policies and procedures affecting the IT function.
- Review covers HR management, change management, financial management, information security management.
- ⚠ Check: manual also lists onsite, offsite, offshore staffing; outline omits it.
- Onsite: staff work in the IT department.
- Offsite (nearshore): staff work remotely in the same area.
- Offshore: staff work at a remote site in a different region.
- Exam traps
  - Treating IT as only a cost center misreads its integrated, strategic role.

## 2.8.4 Human resource management

### Basics

- **HR management**
  - Definition: policies for recruiting, hiring, training, promoting, measuring, disciplining, succession, retention and termination.
  - Use: builds quality IT staff.
  - Audit impact: weak HR practices degrade staff quality and IT performance.
- ⚠ Check: manual says HR content is untested on CISA, citing subjectivity.
- Still, the manual says a CISA should know mandatory leave.
- Auditors still need HR awareness for control assessments.

### Recruiting and hiring

- Hiring aims to choose qualified candidates and comply with legal recruitment rules.
- **Background check**
  - Definition: verifying a candidate's criminal, educational, financial and professional history.
  - Use: screens unsuitable or dishonest candidates.
  - Audit impact: skipped checks leave residual insider risk.
- **Confidentiality or nondisclosure agreement (NDA)** 📓
  - Definition: signed promise not to disclose sensitive information.
  - Use: protects proprietary data from unauthorized disclosure.
  - Audit impact: should be signed before access to internal information.
- It may require honoring a previous employer's security policies.
- It may forbid exploiting knowledge of a former employer's internal controls.
- **Employee bonding**
  - Definition: insurance-like protection against losses from theft, mistakes and neglect.
  - Use: compensates the enterprise for employee-caused losses.
  - Audit impact: not accepted worldwide; illegal in some countries.
- **Conflict of interest disclosure**
  - Definition: employee declares interests that could bias their work.
  - Use: lets management reassign or restrict duties.
  - Audit impact: absence enables undetected self-dealing.
- **Code of professional conduct or ethics**
  - Definition: written standards of acceptable behavior.
  - Use: sets expectations for all staff.
  - Audit impact: auditor verifies it is published and communicated.
- **Noncompete agreement**
  - Definition: employee agrees not to work for competitors for a period.
  - Use: protects business knowledge and customers.
  - Audit impact: enforceability varies by jurisdiction ➕
- Residual risk remains even with these controls.
  - Employee may be unsuitable for the role.
  - Reference checks may be skipped or fraudulent.
  - Temporary staff and contractors add uncontrolled risk.
  - Low confidentiality awareness can compromise security.
- Example: a contractor hired without screening gets admin access and leaks data.
- Exam traps
  - Controls reduce risk; they never eliminate it, so residual risk persists.

### Employee handbook

- **Employee handbook**
  - Definition: a document given at hire explaining rules and expectations.
  - Use: communicates policies consistently to all employees.
  - Audit impact: it is an information source, not a control.
- Contents: security policies, conduct, ethics code, expectations, benefits, vacation, overtime.
- Also covers outside employment, evaluations and emergency procedures.
- It states disciplinary actions for excessive absence, security breaches and noncompliance.
- A published code of conduct should specify responsibilities of all employees.
- Exam traps
  - Having a handbook proves nothing about compliance; enforcement and signed acknowledgment matter.
  - Never pick the handbook as the answer when a control is asked for.

### Training

- **Training**
  - Definition: teaching staff what they need to do jobs securely.
  - Use: raises effectiveness, efficiency and morale.
  - Audit impact: untrained users are a leading source of human error.
- New-hire orientation sets a baseline: cybersecurity, confidentiality, data management, compliance, conflict of interest.
- IT staff get specialized training on security and access controls.
- Ongoing training gives refreshers and updates for job functions and tools.
- Training is essential for IT staff because technology changes quickly.
- Training must be given when new hardware or software is implemented.
- Training should include management, project management and technical topics.
- **Cross-training**
  - Definition: training more than one person to do the same job.
  - Use: reduces dependence on one employee and backs succession.
  - Audit impact: assess risk of one person knowing the whole system.
- Example: two administrators trained on payroll batch jobs for continuity.
- Exam traps
  - Cross-training adds continuity but can concentrate knowledge; assess exposure first.

### Scheduling and time reporting

- **Time reporting**
  - Definition: recording hours spent on activities.
  - Use: monitors scheduling and staffing adequacy.
  - Audit impact: inaccurate entries undermine cost allocation and governance data.
- Proper scheduling improves use of computing resources.
- Time is one of the scarcest IT resources.
- Supports cost allocation, invoicing, chargebacks, KPI measurement and activity analysis.
- Example: comparing hours on application changes versus new development.

### Terms and conditions of employment

- Employees, contractors and third parties must sign them.
- They clarify expectations and consequences if requirements are violated.
- They must reflect the enterprise security policy.
- NDA must be signed before access to internal information.
- State legal responsibilities and rights, such as copyright and data protection.
- State duties for classifying information and managing assets.
- State duties for handling information from other enterprises.
- State the enterprise's duties for personal information it holds.
- Cover responsibilities outside premises and hours, including remote or hybrid work.
- State actions if security requirements are disregarded.
- Obligations may continue for a defined period after employment ends.

### Expectations during employment

- Staff apply security measures per established policies and procedures.
- Responsibilities are documented in approved job descriptions.
- All staff receive awareness, education and training.
- A formal disciplinary process handles security breaches.
- Clear guidance reduces the risk of human error.

### Employee performance management

- **Performance management**
  - Definition: setting goals and reviewing progress.
  - Use: aligns individual work with enterprise objectives.
  - Audit impact: pay and promotion should follow performance evidence.
- **SMART goals**
  - Specific: clear and understandable.
  - Measurable: verifiable and results-oriented.
  - Attainable: achievable yet sufficiently challenging.
  - Relevant: tied to department or enterprise mission.
  - Time-bound: has a schedule and milestones.
- Reviews also gauge employee aspirations and satisfaction.
- Pay, bonuses and promotions should be based on performance.

### Disciplinary actions

- **Performance improvement plan (PIP)**
  - Definition: structured, measurable plan to correct conduct or performance.
  - Use: common disciplinary step before harsher action.
  - Audit impact: documentation shows fair, consistent treatment.

### Promotion policies

- Policies should be fair, equitable, objective and understood by staff.
- They weigh business need, performance, education, experience and responsibility.
- Auditor confirms policies exist and are followed.
- Auditor can analyze historical promotion data for demographic bias.

### Required vacations and job rotation

- **Required (mandatory) vacation** 📓
  - Definition: employee must be away at least once a year.
  - Use: another person performs the job, exposing irregularities.
  - Audit impact: detective deterrent control against internal fraud.
- Fraud may be discovered only if no collusion covers discrepancies.
- **Job rotation**
  - Definition: staff periodically swap tasks.
  - Use: reduces fraud and over-dependence on key staff.
  - Audit impact: spreads knowledge of procedures, controls and technologies.
- Example: a payables clerk on forced leave reveals fictitious vendor payments.
- Exam traps
  - Mandatory leave is the answer for exposing internal fraud, not more training.
  - It fails against collusion, so it does not guarantee detection.

### Retention and succession

- Retention uses compensation, benefits, mentorship and development.
- **Succession plan**
  - Definition: plan for who covers critical roles when staff leave.
  - Use: ensures continuity via redundancy or trained replacements.
  - Audit impact: no plan creates key-person dependency.

### Termination policies

- Policies must be written and cover voluntary and involuntary cases.
- Adverse terminations need documented escort procedures.
- Return devices, access keys, ID cards and badges.
- Remove or block company information on personal devices.
- Revoke logins, including application-specific credentials, not just network IDs.
- Notify appropriate staff and security personnel.
- Final pay routines remove the person from payroll.
- Conduct a termination interview to learn about management and retention gaps.
- Role changes or transfers may require revoking and reissuing access.
- Example: a dismissed analyst keeps database access because only network ID was disabled.
- Exam traps
  - Prompt, complete access removal outranks the exit interview in importance.
  - Disabling only the network account leaves application access open.

## 2.8.5 Enterprise change management

- **Enterprise change management**
  - Definition: a defined, documented process to apply technology improvements.
  - Use: introduces infrastructure and application changes with all impacted levels involved.
  - Audit impact: poor involvement causes user resistance or ignored changes.
- ⚠ Check: this is organizational change, not IT system change control.
- IT leads or facilitates the change as focal point.
- IT tracks technology trends that can improve business processes.
- Obtain senior management commitment first.
- Then gain support from each functional area and its management.
- Build a communication process telling users the impact and benefits.
- Include a way to collect user feedback and involvement.
- Feedback is gathered throughout: requirements validation, user acceptance testing, training.
- Example: a new HR system launch with town halls, pilot users and training.
- Exam traps
  - Sequence: senior management commitment, then functional areas, then end-user communication.
  - Rolling out without user involvement is the usual failure cause.

## 2.8.6 Financial management practices

### Basics

- Technology costs are a major part of enterprise budgets.
- IT resources are costly to acquire, develop and maintain.
- Short useful life forces regular replacement costs.

### Cost allocation and chargeback

- **Chargeback (user-pays)**
  - Definition: charging IT costs to the departments using the services.
  - Use: gives users a market-like price for IPF services.
  - Audit impact: improves monitoring of IS expenses and resource use.
- Charges use a standard formula covering staff time, computer time, other costs.
- Board sets the chargeback policy.
- CFO, user management and IS management implement it jointly.
- IPF means information processing facility.
- Example: marketing is billed for its share of server hours each month.
- Exam traps
  - The board establishes the policy; IT alone does not decide it.

### IS budgets

- **IS budget**
  - Definition: planned allocation of funds to IS activities.
  - Use: facilitates forecasting, monitoring and analysis.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks actual spending against the approved budget.
- Budget should link to short-term projects and long-term IT objectives.

### Software expenses versus capitalization

- **Operating expense (opex)**
  - Definition: cost expensed in the period it occurs.
  - Use: recurring costs like subscriptions.
  - Audit impact: misclassifying can misstate profit.
- **Capital expense (capex)**
  - Definition: cost recorded as an asset and spread over its life.
  - Use: long-lived purchases like owned software.
  - Audit impact: improper capitalization can inflate assets and earnings.
- Classification is a major financial element in IT.
- **IAS 38**
  - Definition: international standard on intangible assets, including software and websites.
  - Use: sets six criteria before development costs may be capitalized.
  - Audit impact: auditor needs to understand how development costs are tracked.
- One criterion is showing probable future economic benefits.
- Interpretations vary, so under IFRS get guidance from chartered accountants.
- ⚠ Check: the six criteria are not listed in the manual; not stated here.
- Software bought outright and installed on-premises is generally capital expense.
- Cloud subscription software is usually operating expense.
- Fraudulent manipulation of classification can cause material financial misstatement.
- Auditors need not know every accounting rule.
- They ensure expenses are tracked properly against an approved budget.
- Exam traps
  - "Generally" and "usually" signal tendencies, not absolute accounting rules.
  - Auditor confirms tracking and budget; accountants decide treatment.

## 2.8.7 Information security management

- **Information security management**
  - Definition: the function protecting information and processing resources.
  - Use: guards against unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, destruction.
  - Audit impact: auditor assesses whether it achieves confidentiality, integrity and availability.
- Goal: maintain confidentiality, integrity and availability (CIA).
- It also sustains customer trust and business continuity.

### Key responsibilities

- **Risk assessment and management**
  - Definition: identifying and evaluating threats, vulnerabilities and impacts.
  - Use: selects risk treatment strategies.
  - Audit impact: should be regular, not one-time.
- **Security policy development**
  - Definition: writing policies, standards, guidelines and procedures.
  - Use: sets rules for staff, contractors and stakeholders.
  - Audit impact: policies must exist, be enforced and be understood.
- **Incident response and management**
  - Definition: procedures to handle and coordinate responses to security incidents.
  - Use: investigates breaches, limits impact, prevents recurrence.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks procedures are defined and tested.
- **Security awareness and training**
  - Definition: programs teaching staff security practices.
  - Use: builds a security-conscious culture.
  - Audit impact: weak awareness raises human-error incidents.
- **Security architecture and design**
  - Definition: designing secure systems, networks and infrastructure.
  - Use: implements firewalls, intrusion detection, encryption and access controls effectively.
  - Audit impact: gaps here create systemic weaknesses.
- **Vulnerability management**
  - Definition: finding and fixing weaknesses before attackers exploit them.
  - Use: scans, patches, updates and penetration tests.
  - Audit impact: needs a process for prompt remediation.
- **BCP and DRP**
  - Definition: plans to keep operating and recover after disruption.
  - Use: BIA and scenarios set key recovery metrics.
  - Audit impact: metrics must match business tolerance.
- **BIA (business impact analysis)**
  - Definition: assessing how outages affect the business over time.
  - Use: sets recovery priorities and metrics.
  - Audit impact: wrong BIA means wrong recovery investment.
- **RTO (recovery time objective)** 📓
  - Definition: maximum acceptable downtime before operations must resume.
  - Use: sizes recovery strategy and site choice.
  - Audit impact: shorter RTO requires costlier recovery solutions.
- **RPO (recovery point objective)** 📓
  - Definition: maximum tolerable data loss, expressed in time units.
  - Use: drives backup and replication frequency.
  - Audit impact: backup interval longer than RPO breaches objective.
- Example: RPO of one hour needs backups or replication at least hourly.
- **Identity and access management**
  - Definition: managing accounts, access rights and privileges.
  - Use: strong authentication, least privilege and activity monitoring.
  - Audit impact: excess privilege enables fraud and breaches.
- **Compliance and regulatory requirements**
  - Definition: meeting laws, regulations and industry standards on information security.
  - Use: audits, controls and documentation prove compliance.
  - Audit impact: gaps create legal exposure.
- **Security incident monitoring and analysis**
  - Definition: watching security logs and alerts from many sources.
  - Use: detects incidents early.
  - Audit impact: unmonitored logs delay detection.
- ⚠ Check: manual text here omits SIEM; label is an outline addition ➕
- Exam traps
  - RTO measures restoration time; RPO measures data loss; never swap them.
  - RPO decides backup frequency; RTO decides recovery speed.
  - Information security management leads protection, but business owners still own risk acceptance.

## Takeaway

- Value equals benefit versus cost, and depends on strategic alignment.
- Investment portfolio analysis best supports project prioritization by weighing risk against reward.
- Handbooks inform; mandatory leave, rotation and fast access revocation actually control.
- Change management needs senior commitment first, then functional areas, then users.
- Chargeback policy comes from the board; auditors verify spending against approved budgets.
- RTO is downtime tolerated; RPO is data loss tolerated.
