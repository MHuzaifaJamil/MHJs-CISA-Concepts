## 2.7 Opening principles

### Data governance basics
- Definition: Data governance is the set of rules deciding who manages data and how.
- Use: It treats data as an asset that needs ownership and consistent handling.
- Audit impact: Auditors evaluate whether the governance program exists and works.
- Audit impact: Auditors also check classification practices align with the governance program.
- ⚠ Check: Manual 2.7 never defines data governance; this is my summary ➕.

### Information asset inventory
- Definition: An inventory is a detailed list of the enterprise's information assets.
- Use: It is the first step toward classifying assets and sizing protection. 📓
- Audit impact: You cannot protect or classify assets that nobody has listed.
- Example: A bank lists databases, file shares, tapes and paper records first.
- Review and update the inventory regularly, since assets change.
- Review the classification itself for appropriateness, not only the list.
- Audit impact: A stale inventory means some assets carry the wrong protection.

### Information classification
- Definition: Classification assigns each asset a level of sensitivity and criticality. 📓
- Use: Each level carries specific security rules, so access controls match the level. 📓
- Audit impact: Classification gives one consistent view of security needs enterprise-wide.
- Secondary benefit: it enables cost-benefit analysis of controls.
- Definition: Cost-benefit analysis compares the cost of a control with the risk it reduces.
- Audit impact: Linking protection to business objectives avoids overspending on unneeded protection. 📓
- Audit impact: Classification also prevents under-protection of valuable assets. 📓
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: classification ensures assets receive suitable protection for their value.
  - Wrong: "same baseline security for all assets"; that ignores asset value.
  - Wrong: "only critical assets get protected"; lower levels still need controls.
  - Wrong: "protects all IT assets equally"; classification differentiates, it does not equalize.

### Ownership and simplicity
- Definition: The information owner is the person accountable for the information.
- Use: The owner decides the classification, following the classification and handling policy.
- Audit impact: IT or security staff assigning classification themselves is a finding.
- End-user managers and security administrators use classifications in risk assessment.
- Their use decides who can access what, and how much.
- Keep schemes simple; three to five sensitivity levels is typical.
- Scheme size should reflect enterprise size and nature.
- Audit impact: Overly complex schemes become impractical and users stop applying them.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: the owner classifies; custodians and administrators implement controls.
  - Wrong: security administrator classifies; they enforce but do not own the data.

### What classification defines
- Importance of the information asset.
- Asset owner.
- Process for granting access.
- Approver of access rights and access levels.
- Extent and depth of security controls.
- Audit impact: A classification policy missing these items has a design gap.

### Requirements and separation of environments
- Consider legal, regulatory, contractual and internal requirements.
- These cover privacy, confidentiality, integrity and availability of information.
- Definition: Production data is live data used to run the business.
- Definition: Test data is data used in development and testing environments.
- Use: Classification identifies who may access production versus test data and programs.
- Audit impact: Developers should not have access to production data or programs.
- Example: A programmer testing a payroll change uses masked test records, not live payroll.

### Labeling and misuse
- Definition: Labeling marks documents and media with their classification level.
- Use: Labels tell users the handling rules for each item.
- Audit impact: Unlabeled media suggest potential misuse of information. ⭐
- Users may disclose confidential data because they never knew the rules.
- Definition: Social engineering manipulates people into revealing information or access.
- Audit impact: Social engineering exploits user misunderstanding of handling rules.
- Uniform treatment by level is easier than writing rules per information type.
- Audit impact: Missing labels plus missing user instruction make policies hard to follow.

### Classification examples
- Public: freely open to anyone; may be reused and redistributed.
- Example: Public data includes marketing material and press releases.
- Internal: limited to employees expressly granted access.
- Example: Business plans, organization charts and internal reports.
- Confidential: must be kept private; exposure harms the enterprise.
- Example: Unpublished financials, customer lists, payment card data, contracts.
- Confidential data may fall under laws or standards such as HIPAA and PCI DSS.
- Restricted: highly sensitive; exposure can bring legal fines or criminal charges.
- Example: Proprietary information, research, and data protected by state or federal rules.
- ⚠ Check: Outline lists only legal fines for Restricted; the manual adds criminal charges.
- ⚠ Check: Level names are examples; manual says only three to five is typical.
- Purpose of inventory and classification: guide handling of critical data assets.
- IT governance teams prioritize protection to support objectives and meet regulations.

## 2.7.1 Data inventory and classification

### Data inventory for privacy
- Definition: A privacy data inventory lists all personal data the enterprise holds.
- Use: It is the most important resource for conducting a PIA. ⭐
- Definition: A privacy impact assessment (PIA) checks that personal data is properly safeguarded.
- Definition: A PIA also covers use, sharing, individual access and destruction.
- Audit impact: A complete inventory lets the PIA confirm controls and find vulnerabilities.
- Reason: The master inventory covers all sensitive data.
- Inventory includes types of personal information collected.
- It also holds details needed to show privacy compliance.
- Scope must be enterprise-wide and current.
- Audit impact: A partial or outdated inventory makes PIA results unreliable.

### Metadata and joint ownership
- Definition: A metadata repository stores descriptions of data, such as source, type and owner.
- Use: Inventory content and scope are largely consistent with the metadata repository.
- Data governance implements and manages the metadata repository.
- Privacy and data governance manage the privacy inventory jointly.
- Audit impact: Check that both functions share responsibility, with no gap between them.
- Consider unintentional capture, use and storage of personal data.
- Example: Free-text comment fields that hold customer health details nobody planned to collect.
- Audit impact: Unplanned personal data is missing from the inventory and escapes controls.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: the inventory comes before the PIA and classification.
  - Wrong: start with controls or policies; you cannot scope them without the inventory.

## 2.7.2 Legal purpose, consent and legitimate interest

### Overview
- Definition: Personal information is data about an identifiable individual, the data subject.
- Definition: A data controller decides why and how personal data is processed.
- ⚠ Check: Manual uses "controller" undefined; this definition is standard usage ➕.
- These three concepts cover how data is obtained, used, shared, retained and accessed.
- They also cover making data available to the data subjects.
- GDPR lists more processing bases than these three; these are the most common.
- Definition: GDPR is the European Union's general data protection regulation of 2016.
- Audit impact: Many systems must be engineered to conform to these legal terms.
- Privacy engineers need to understand these terms for that reason.

### Legal purpose
- Definition: Legal purpose is the lawful reason for collecting personal information.
- Use: The controller states purpose in the privacy notice or similar communication.
- The purpose must comply with law and rely on a permissible legal basis.
- Align later use with the stated purpose and consents obtained.
- This is called use limitation.
- Consult data protection authorities on legitimate purpose when necessary.
- Member states may add more specific requirements beyond the EU minimum.
- Audit impact: Know the rules of every country where data is collected or processed.
- Legal-purpose rules, especially consent sufficiency, may differ between regulations.
- Example: Order-update emails cannot be reused for marketing without consent.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: new uses need a lawful basis or fresh consent.
  - Wrong: original consent covers any later use; use limitation says otherwise.

### Consent
- Definition: Consent is the individual's agreement to how their data is processed.
- Definition: Implicit consent is inferred from actions; explicit consent is clearly stated.
- Use: Obtain whichever type the applicable regulation requires.
- Obtain consent before collection begins.
- Obtain consent before using data beyond its original purpose.
- Obtain consent before transfer to third parties or other jurisdictions.
- Audit impact: Consent obtained after collection or transfer is a compliance failure.
- When sending consents electronically, include a cover sheet noting privileged content.
- ⚠ Check: Manual calls the cover sheet good practice, not law.
- Definition: A privacy engineer builds privacy requirements into systems and services.
- Privacy engineers record consents and withdrawals of consent.
- They must also document denials, since individuals may give no paperwork.
- Systems must support decisions on use or nonuse of personal data.
- Systems must also support legal use limitation.
- GDPR and HIPAA both specify consent requirements.
- Definition: HIPAA is a United States health-information privacy law.
- Audit impact: Auditors test that consent records, withdrawals and denials are retained.
- A signed consent form alone may not prove an adequate consent process.
- ⚠ Check: The consent-form points above come from the manual's privacy-notice section, not 2.7.2.
- Example: A clinic logs each patient's opt-in, later withdrawal, and refusals in its system.

### Legitimate interest
- Definition: Legitimate interest is a lawful basis for processing data without consent.
- Use: Applies when individuals' rights do not override the enterprise's, given reasonable expectations.
- Reasonable expectation depends on the relationship with the enterprise.
- Typical relationships: client, customer, patient, employee.
- Example: Using employee data for annual statements or country tax filings.
- Example: Public health statistics, such as infection counts per region.
- Example: Forensic analysis to find the source of a hacking incident.
- Individuals' interests can win if they did not expect the further processing.
- Assess the purpose, necessity and balance elements.
- Definition: A legitimate interest assessment weighs enterprise need against individual rights.
- Involve legal counsel, the information security officer and the privacy officer.
- GDPR is the predominant regulation requiring this assessment.
- Other local, state or national laws may also allow it.
- Audit impact: Check a documented assessment exists before processing without consent.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: legitimate interest is assessed case by case, never assumed.
  - Wrong: it removes the need for any analysis; balance must still be tested.
  - Wrong: it always favors the enterprise; unexpected processing favors the individual.

## 2.7.3 Data subject rights

### Rights overview
- Definition: Data subject rights are the powers individuals hold over their personal data.
- Use: Rights allow access, control and communication about their own data.
- Rights vary widely by law and are debated.
- Identify, document and understand the rights that apply to the data you process.
- Common examples are access and erasure under GDPR ➕.
- Audit impact: Auditors check that data subject requests are handled appropriately.
- Related checks: privacy terms in third-party contracts, retention and destruction policies.
- Further checks: cross-border privacy rules addressed, staff trained, and inventory maintained.

### NIST Privacy Framework
- Definition: The NIST Privacy Framework is a tool for building a privacy program.
- Use: It manages privacy through enterprise risk management.
- It has two functions supporting data subject rights: Control-P and Communicate-P.
- ⚠ Check: Framework has other functions; manual names only these two here.
- Control-P: manage data processing with enough granularity to handle privacy risk.
- Control-P considers both the enterprise's and individuals' viewpoints.
- Communicate-P: give individuals and enterprises reliable understanding and dialog about processing.
- Communicate-P recognizes that people must know how data is processed.

| Category | Function | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| CT.PO-P | Control-P | Policies, processes, procedures for data processing |
| CT.DM-P | Control-P | Data managed to protect privacy and enable principles |
| CT.DP-P | Control-P | Disassociated processing reduces linkage to individuals |
| CM.PO-P | Communicate-P | Policies for transparency of processing practices |
| CM.AW-P | Communicate-P | Reliable awareness of processing practices and risk |

- CT.PO-P also requires legal and regulatory requirements beyond internal policy.
- Principles named under CT.DM-P: individual participation, data quality, data minimization.
- Definition: Data minimization means collecting and keeping only the data needed.
- Definition: Disassociability means processing without tying data to identifiable individuals.
- Subcategories give privacy engineers specific guidance, found in the framework itself.
- Audit impact: Map each category to a policy, a control and evidence.
- Exam traps:
  - Control-P is about managing processing; Communicate-P is about transparency and dialog.
  - Do not swap CT (control) and CM (communicate) prefixes.

### Transborder data flow
- Definition: Transborder data flow is data transmission between two countries.
- Use: Covers email, invoices, payment advice sent by cable, links or satellite.
- Transmission choices consider cost and delay.
- Origin or destination country laws may apply.
- Concerns: legal compliance, protection, data security, integrity and privacy.
- Privacy laws on protection and access may differ or conflict between countries.
- Some countries regulate the encryption of data sent across borders.
- Definition: Encryption converts data into unreadable form for anyone without the key.
- Audit impact: Legal limits on encryption can weaken protection for exchanged data.
- Internet routing is set by routers and is not fixed.
- Traffic between two computers in one country may still cross a border.
- Example: An email between two offices in one country routes through a foreign network.
- Audit impact: Assume cross-border exposure even when both endpoints are domestic.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: consider laws of both origin and destination countries.
  - Wrong: only the sender's country law matters; the destination may also apply.

## 2.7.4 Data quality 📓

### Data quality review priority
- Definition: Data quality is how accurate, complete and reliable data is for its purpose.
- ⚠ Check: Manual has no 2.7.4; section ends after transborder flow.
- ⚠ Check: Manual mentions data quality only in analytics and NIST context.
- Data quality content comes from my notes, not manual 2.7 ➕.
- Use: Management asks auditors to focus data quality improvements where risk is highest.
- Regulator-reported data is reviewed first. 📓⭐
- Reason: Errors there create legal and regulatory risk.
- Example: Capital ratios filed with a banking regulator are checked before internal dashboards.
- Audit impact: Regulatory exposure ranks above convenience, volume or age of the data.
- Exam traps:
  - Correct logic: prioritize by legal and regulatory consequence of error.
  - Wrong: historical trend data; it carries no immediate reporting obligation.
  - Wrong: internal management reports; the impact is internal, not legal.
  - Wrong: external-source data; its origin does not raise legal exposure by itself.

## Takeaway
- Inventory first, then classify; owners decide levels, custodians apply controls.
- Classification matches protection to value: no over-protection, no under-protection.
- Unlabeled media signal misuse risk and social engineering exposure.
- Consent comes before collection, secondary use and third-party or cross-border transfer.
- Legitimate interest needs a documented balance test, not an assumption.
- Data quality audits start with data reported to regulators.
