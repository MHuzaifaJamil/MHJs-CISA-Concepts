## 2.6 Program overview (basics first)

### Personal data
- Definition: information that relates to an identifiable person.
- Use: decides which data falls under privacy laws and controls.
- Audit impact: auditor must confirm the organization's own definition matches applicable law.
- ⚠ Check: manual says the definition varies among regulations; no universal definition exists.
- Example: a customer name plus email address identifies one person.

### Data subject
- Definition: the person whom the personal data describes.
- Use: holds legal rights over their data, such as access and withdrawal.
- Audit impact: auditor tests that data subject requests are handled properly.

### Data controller and data processor
- Definition: the controller decides why data is used; the processor handles it.
- Use: both must comply with the parameters set in the privacy notice.
- Audit impact: contracts must bind processors to the same privacy commitments.
- Example: a retailer is controller; its outsourced payroll vendor is processor.

### Privacy governance program
- Definition: a managed program that identifies and controls all personal information.
- Use: ensures handling follows law, policy, procedures, guidelines and data subject rights.
- Audit impact: auditor evaluates whether the program covers all personal data, not samples.

### Life cycle protection
- Definition: controls applied from collection through use, storage, sharing and destruction.
- Use: protects privacy at every stage, including associated sensitive information.
- Audit impact: gaps at any stage, such as destruction, are reportable findings.

### Privacy management practice areas
- Privacy roles and responsibilities for data.
- Training and awareness communications and activities.
- Monitoring vendor and third-party management practices.
- Developing a privacy audit process.
- Implementing a privacy incident management capability.
- Audit impact: missing owners for any area signals weak governance.

### Exam traps (2.6 overview)
- Privacy program is governance-led, not an IT-only technical control project.
- Distractors that stress encryption alone ignore roles, notices and audit processes.

## Program requirements (figure 2.12)

### Internal requirements
- Definition: things the organization must set up inside itself.
- Assign privacy roles and responsibilities.
- Define and inventory personal information.
- Implement employee privacy policies, procedures and training.
- Establish vendor management programs.
- Manage privacy incidents.
- Perform privacy audits and assessments.
- Harmonize with organizational culture, or change it with management support.
- Account for capabilities and limits of information systems.
- Audit impact: a policy the systems cannot technically enforce is a gap.

### External requirements
- Definition: things imposed or expected from outside the organization.
- Identify and document applicable laws, regulations, standards and contractual obligations.
- Choose privacy principles and frameworks.
- Post a privacy notice reflecting actual practices and legal requirements.
- Maintain third-party oversight.
- Address data subject rights.
- Address customer, investor and industry requirements.
- Accommodate existing contractual relationships.
- Audit impact: notice content must match real practice, not aspiration.
- ⚠ Check: figure 2.12 is sourced from the CDPSE manual, not original CISA content.

### Exam traps (requirements)
- Legal and contractual requirements come first; the organization's preferences follow them.
- A published notice that differs from real practice creates legal exposure.
- Culture fit matters; a program ignoring culture fails in operation.

## Privacy principles and laws

### Privacy principles
- Definition: high-level rules guiding how personal data is treated.
- Use: apply privacy controls consistently across all business activities.
- Audit impact: auditor checks that a chosen framework is actually implemented.
- ISO/TS 17975:2022: principles and data requirements for consent on personal health information.
- OECD Privacy Principles.
- AICPA Generally Accepted Privacy Principles.
- ISACA Privacy Principles.
- ⚠ Check: manual calls these examples only; the list is not exhaustive.

### GDPR and Trans-Atlantic Data Privacy Framework (DPF)
- Definition: EU laws protecting EU individuals' data, including data stored in the US.
- Use: governs organizations that trade within the EU.
- Audit impact: cross-border transfers must follow these rules.
- ⚠ Check: manual lists the DPF as a "law"; it is a transfer framework. ➕
- ⚠ Check: specific GDPR articles, fines and deadlines are not in this section. ➕
- Example: a US cloud host storing EU customer records needs a lawful transfer basis.

### Exam traps (principles and laws)
- Laws are built on principles; the principle set chosen is a management decision.
- Applicable law depends on where data subjects live, not only where servers sit.

## 2.6.1 Privacy documentation

### Why documentation matters
- Definition: written evidence of how personal data is managed.
- Use: demonstrates practices, meets obligations, builds trust and shows due care.
- Audit impact: no documentation means no provable privacy program.
- Policies, notices and documents must align with laws, contracts and standards.
- Required or adopted industry standards must appear in documents and practice.
- Inward and outward documents are reviewed regularly, including websites and social media.
- Program breadth and depth must meet or exceed legal requirements.
- Regulators judge the enterprise against what it said it would do.

### Privacy notice
- Definition: outward-facing statement for data subjects and data protection authorities.
- Use: describes collection, use, retention, safeguarding and disclosure of personal data.
- Audit impact: it establishes legal accountability; controller and processor must comply with it.
- Notice explains what data is collected and why.
- Notice explains how data is used, processed and handled.
- Notice explains who receives data without the subject's knowledge.
- Notice explains retention period, impact on subjects, and destruction.
- Notice explains how to exercise rights and supports consent.
- Language must be easy to understand.
- Formats: web page, forms, brochures, facility documents, contracts, CCTV signs.
- Example: a hospital leaflet explaining record sharing and patient access rights.
- Keep the notice separate from terms of use, avoiding perceived duplicity.
- Allow enough time between notice and collection for subjects to decide.
- Too little time can make even a thorough notice insufficient.
- The internal version is called a privacy policy or privacy statement.
- ⚠ Check: manual says the same provisions are named differently by audience only.

### Consent form
- Definition: document recording a data subject's agreement to the privacy notice.
- Use: gives subjects a reference and records the interaction with the collector.
- Audit impact: explicit consent protects against later claims of misunderstanding.
- Forms: opt-in/opt-out button, tick box, signature box.
- Provisions needed: consent for existing data and renewal if noncompliant.
- Informed consent is an ongoing exchange, not just a signature.
- Ongoing exchange may include Q&A sessions, emails and presentations.
- Subjects can usually withdraw consent at any time.
- Withdrawal must be easy; difficulty raises enterprise risk.
- Consent capture is separate from telling subjects how to make inquiries.
- Example: a pre-ticked box is weaker evidence than an unticked box ticked by user.
- ⚠ Check: pre-ticked box example is my illustration, not stated in manual. ➕

### Personal information inventory
- Definition: documented list of personal data assets collected, derived, processed or stored.
- Use: raises awareness of what is at risk and supports protection measures.
- Audit impact: auditors verify it exists, is complete and is kept current.
- It is an internal document, not itself a regulatory requirement.
- No universal definition exists, so each enterprise decides what to record.
- Include data flow mappings whenever possible.
- Covers network, applications, systems, storage areas and physical forms.
- Can be a spreadsheet, database, document or asset tool.
- Keep inventory and data flow diagrams updated and current.
- Example: HR, customer and marketing data each listed with location and owner.

### Other documentation
- Activity logs: training attendance, failed identity verifications, access to personal records.
- Data protection legal requirements document: shared with privacy engineers for system design.
- Privacy risk assessment report: systematic review of collection, use, sharing, maintenance and destruction.
- Risk assessment also covers new technologies and risk beyond legal requirements.
- Privacy impact assessment (PIA) report: findings plus how risks will be mitigated.
- ⚠ Check: manual cites section 2.11.1 for PIA; classification sits in 2.7.
- Privacy governance reports: current compliance, improvements, incidents and program status.
- Governance reports demonstrate value, raise awareness, communicate successes and engage stakeholders.
- Training records: topic, attendees, date, delivery method, test results.
- Training records prove due care to auditors and show training effectiveness.
- Data incident register: facts, effects, remedial and preventive measures.
- Individual rights register: requests received, how and when, such as copies or rectification.
- Audit impact: each register is evidence the auditor samples against.

### Exam traps (documentation)
- Notice is outward-facing; policy is its internal counterpart.
- Signature alone does not prove informed consent; ongoing exchange does.
- Inventory is not legally mandated but is the foundation of every other control.
- Combining notice with terms of use is common but not best practice.
- Documents unreviewed or stale fail due-care tests, even if once accurate.

## 2.6.2 Audit process (privacy)

### Privacy audits
- Definition: audits, assessments, testing and compliance reviews of privacy practices.
- Use: confirms policies and practices comply with internal and external requirements.
- Audit impact: annual data protection audits are described as essential for compliance.
- Audits can reveal enterprise architecture failures against privacy-by-design principles.
- Such failures create business risk.
- Performing audits demonstrates due care.
- ⚠ Check: "annual" frequency is manual wording; law or contract may require otherwise. ➕

### ISACA Privacy Principle 9: Monitoring, Measuring and Reporting
- Definition: principle that privacy program effectiveness is consistently monitored and reported.
- Use: guides the audit and measurement framework below.
- Framework measures effectiveness of the privacy program.
- Framework measures compliance with policies, standards and legal requirements.
- Framework measures use and implementation of privacy tools.
- Framework tracks advances in privacy-enhancing technologies.
- Framework tracks changes in privacy laws.
- Framework tracks types and numbers of privacy breaches.
- Framework tracks privacy risk areas in the controller's digital ecosystem.
- Framework tracks third parties with access and their risk levels.
- Report compliance to key stakeholders.
- Integrate international privacy practices, then check implementation in audits.
- Set procedures for personal data use in investigations, monitoring and analytics.
- Procedures apply to internal or external auditors.
- Anonymize data where law bars monitoring raw personal data.
- Audit that the anonymization is effective and consistent enterprise-wide.
- Example: fraud analytics run on anonymized transactions where law restricts raw monitoring.

### IS auditor evaluation points
- Privacy requirements are included in third-party contracts.
- Retention and destruction policies are implemented.
- Cross-border regulations are addressed in data processes.
- Employees received training on privacy regulations.
- Data inventory is created and maintained.
- Data subject requests are handled appropriately.
- Audit impact: evidence for each point is contract, record, log or inventory.

### Exam traps (audit process)
- Best auditor evidence is implementation proof, not a policy that merely exists.
- Third-party access risk is a core audit area, not a vendor-only matter.
- Anonymization is a legal-driven answer when raw monitoring is prohibited.
- Auditing the anonymization process itself is also required.

## 2.6.3 Privacy-enhancing techniques ➕

- ⚠ Check: manual's section 2.6 has no 2.6.3; this subsection is my addition.
- ⚠ Check: manual mentions anonymization only inside 2.6.2; pseudonymisation is not found there.

### Pseudonymisation 📓
- Definition: replacing identifiers with an artificial token or code. 📓
- Use: keeps data usable for analytics while limiting exposure in a breach.
- Audit impact: a mapping table links tokens back to identities. 📓
- Mapping table must be strongly protected, or tokens are easily reversed. 📓
- Not a substitute for encryption and access controls. 📓
- Auditor verifies the mapping table is stored separately with restricted access.
- Example: patient names replaced by IDs; the ID-to-name table sits in a vault.

### Anonymisation
- Definition: altering data so individuals can no longer be identified.
- Use: applied when law prohibits monitoring raw personal data.
- Audit impact: auditor tests that anonymization is effective and consistently applied.
- Key difference: pseudonymised data can be re-linked; truly anonymised data cannot. ➕
- ⚠ Check: the re-link difference is general knowledge, not stated in the manual. ➕

### Exam traps (techniques)
- Pseudonymisation lowers risk but does not remove the need for encryption.
- Pseudonymised data is still personal data because re-identification remains possible. ➕
- Weak mapping-table protection is the typical finding.
- When law forbids raw monitoring, choose anonymization, then audit its effectiveness.

## Takeaway
- Privacy program equals roles, notices, inventory, training, vendors, incidents and audits.
- Notice creates legal accountability; practice must match what was promised.
- Consent is an ongoing process and must be easy to withdraw.
- Inventory with data flows is the internal foundation of all privacy controls.
- Audit privacy through third-party contracts, retention, cross-border rules, training and requests.
- Protect pseudonymisation mapping tables; anonymize when law bars raw data monitoring.
