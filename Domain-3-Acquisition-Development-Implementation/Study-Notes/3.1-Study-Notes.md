## 3.1.0 Governance foundations (section opening)

### Project governance
- Definition: rules, policies and controls that keep a project aligned with enterprise goals.
- Use: ensures strategic and tactical alignment with objectives and risk strategy.
- Audit impact: without governance, every aspect of a project may be compromised.
- Governance structure involves both the project organization and the functional line organization.
- All governance decisions are driven through the business case.
- Benefits achieved are reviewed periodically, not only at project end.
- Example: a steering committee halts a project when its business case stops holding.

### Portfolio and program links
- Portfolio and program structures show common objectives, risks and resource connections.
- Audit impact: auditors use these links to see where one project's failure spreads.

### Hard, soft and environmental factors
- Definition (hard factors): measurable items such as deliverables, quality, costs and deadlines.
- Definition (soft factors): team dynamics, conflict resolution, leadership, culture and communication.
- Definition (environmental factors): politics, power issues, stakeholder expectations, ethical and social issues.
- Use: good project management balances all three groups, not hard factors alone.
- Audit impact: projects often fail on soft or environmental factors, not technical ones.
- Example: on-budget project fails because the sponsor's department resists the new process.

### Third-party transformation teams
- Definition: specialist vendors hired to embed technology in a major transformation program.
- Use: they supply technical leadership, such as for ERP or IaaS solutions.
- Audit impact: confirm solution fits business needs, not a generic template.

### Grouped roles in small enterprises
- Small enterprises may combine or restructure project roles.
- Audit impact: auditor ensures segregation of duties (SoD) is not compromised.
- Example: one person both develops and approves code changes is a red flag.

### Scope of exam knowledge
- The CISA exam does not test any specific project management standard.
- Know structures, policies, procedures and related controls instead.
- Audit impact: auditor first learns which standard the enterprise actually uses.
- Exam traps:
  - Pick the answer about control principle, not a named methodology's terminology.
  - Distractors citing a specific standard's steps are usually irrelevant.

## 3.1.1 Project management practices

- Definition: applying knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to meet an objective.
- Use: meets defined user requirements, budget and deadline for an IS project.
- Project management is a business process in a project-oriented enterprise.
- It begins with the project charter and ends with project completion.
- Component processes: initiating, planning, executing, controlling, monitoring and closing.
- The project management process itself needs careful, explicit design.
- Apply business process engineering design issues to the project management process.
- Most IS projects divide into explicit phases, such as the SDLC.
- Audit impact: auditor checks that a defined process exists and is followed.
- ⚠ Check: the manual lists controlling and monitoring separately; some standards merge them.

## 3.1.2 Project management structure

- Core structure: steering committee, sponsor, project manager and project team.

### Organizational structure types
- Definition (functional-structured): work split by department; project manager has no formal authority.
- Use (functional): project manager can only advise peers on which activities to complete.
- Definition (project-structured): project manager holds formal authority over budget, schedule and team.
- Definition (matrix-structured): authority is shared between project manager and department heads.
- Audit impact: weaker manager authority means higher risk of delay and unresolved conflict.
- Example: in a functional structure, a manager cannot compel a department to deliver.
- Exam traps:
  - "No formal authority" points to functional structure.
  - "Shared authority" points to matrix; "full authority" points to project-structured.

### Initiation and governance points
- The IT steering committee prioritizes major project requests.
- The committee also identifies the project manager.
- The project manager need not be IT staff.
- The project manager needs complete operational control and appropriate resources.
- The IS auditor may join the team as control expert in an advisory role.
- In that role the auditor is not performing an audit.
- Heavy involvement may make the auditor ineligible to audit the application later.
- IS projects may start anywhere in the enterprise, including IT.
- Exam traps:
  - Auditor on team gives advice; independence concern is about later auditing the same system.
  - The sponsor or steering committee prioritizes projects, not the auditor.

## 3.1.3 Project management roles and responsibilities

### Project steering committee
- Definition: stakeholder group giving overall direction to the project.
- Use: represents major stakeholders and is ultimately responsible for deliverables, costs and schedules.
- Includes a senior representative from each significantly affected business area.
- The project manager is a member.
- Members are empowered to decide on designs affecting their departments.
- The project sponsor is a member and chairs.
- It reviews progress regularly and meets in emergencies when required.
- It takes corrective action on escalated issues.
- Audit impact: confirm minutes show regular reviews and decisions on escalations.

### Senior management
- Demonstrates commitment so the needed people are involved.
- Approves the necessary resources.

### Project sponsor
- Definition: senior manager of the primary business unit the system supports.
- Use: provides funding.
- Defines critical success factors and metrics together with the project manager.
- Assumes ownership of data and application.
- Exam traps:
  - Sponsor owns data and funds the project; the project manager does not.

### User management
- Assumes ownership of the project and resulting system.
- Allocates qualified representatives to the team.
- Participates in process redesign, requirements, test cases, acceptance testing and training.
- Reviews and approves deliverables.
- Audit impact: user sign-off evidence is key proof of acceptance.

### User project team
- Completes assigned tasks and works to local standards.
- Acts as subject matter experts for developers.
- Advises the project manager of plan deviations.

### Project manager
- Provides day-to-day management and leadership.
- Keeps activities in line with overall direction.
- Ensures appropriate departmental representation.
- Ensures adherence to local standards and stakeholder quality expectations.
- Resolves interdepartmental conflicts.
- Monitors and controls costs and timetable.
- Facilitates scope definition, manages the budget and controls through the schedule.
- Holds line responsibility for dedicated project personnel.

### Quality assurance (QA)
- Reviews results and deliverables within and at the end of each phase.
- Confirms compliance with requirements.
- Review points depend on the SDLC, system size and deviation impact.
- Measures staff adherence to the enterprise SDLC and advises on deviations.
- Proposes process improvements or additional control points.
- Audit impact: QA independent of developers supports SoD.

### Systems development management
- Provides technical support for hardware and software environments.
- Assures compatibility with the enterprise computing environment and IT direction.
- Assumes operating support and maintenance after installation.

### Systems development project team
- Completes tasks and involves users in development.
- Works to local standards.
- Advises the project manager of necessary plan deviations.

### Security officer (or security team)
- Ensures controls protect data per classification and security policy.
- Consults on security measures throughout the life cycle.
- Reviews security test plans and reports before implementation.
- Evaluates security documents for accreditation.
- Monitors security effectiveness during operation.
- Audit impact: early security involvement is cheaper than retrofitting controls.

### Information system security engineer
- Applies engineering principles to find and contain vulnerabilities.
- Designs per defense in breadth and security in depth.
- Ensures data privacy considerations uphold data subject rights.
- Exam traps:
  - Role questions: match the duty to the owner; ownership sits with sponsor and users.
  - IT operations do not own business data or the project outcome.

## 3.1.4 Project management techniques ⭐

- Techniques: Gantt chart, PERT, CPM, critical path and timebox.
- Use: quantitative and qualitative size estimating, scheduling, allocation and productivity measurement.
- Choice of technique depends on project size and complexity.

### Three intertwined elements
- Elements: deliverables, duration and budget.
- Demanding deliverables correlate with long duration and high budget.
- Budget = resources required x fees or costs.
- Resources are estimated at the start using size-estimation techniques.
- Resources are usually kept almost fixed to minimize direct and administration costs.

### Man-month dilemma
- Formula: R x D = TR, where TR (total resources) is constant.
- Few resources give a long project, near point LR.
- Many resources give a short project, near point LD.
- LR and LD are practical limits of the curve.
- Too long seems impossible; too many people at once is unmanageable.
- Rectangle area at any point is proportional to budget.
- Worked example: TR = 24 person-months.
  - Step 1: R = 4 gives D = 24 / 4 = 6 months.
  - Step 2: R = 8 gives D = 24 / 8 = 3 months.
  - Step 3: R = 12 gives D = 24 / 12 = 2 months.
- Verification: 4 x 6 = 24, 8 x 3 = 24, 12 x 2 = 24; all equal TR.
- ⚠ Check: the model is simplified; real teams add coordination overhead beyond the formula. ➕
- Exam traps:
  - Adding people does not always shorten schedule; manageability is the practical limit.

## 3.1.5 Portfolio / program management

- Definition (portfolio): all projects in the enterprise at a point in time.
- Definition (program): projects and tasks linked by strategies, objectives, budgets and schedules.
- Programs are more complex, longer, higher budget, higher risk and more strategic.
- Programs have a defined start and end date, like projects.
- Example (IS program): ERP implementation with infrastructure, BPR and training projects.
- Example (non-IS program): mergers and acquisitions affecting systems, structure and processes.

### Program management objectives
- Manage scope, financials, schedules, objectives and deliverables.
- Manage context, environment, communication, culture and organization.

### Program roles and governance
- Roles: program owner, program manager and program team.
- The program owner is distinct from the project owner.
- Meetings: program-owner meetings and program-team meetings.
- Program and project methodologies run in parallel, handled separately, not combined.
- A written assignment from the program sponsor formally starts a program.
- Programs are closely coupled; portfolio projects need not be.
- Structures: expert pools, PMO and project portfolio groups.
- Integrative tools: project management guidelines and standard project plans.

### Portfolio versus program example
- Individual activities, such as one ERP module build, are projects.
- Related activities under one larger goal form a program.
- All underway activities together form the portfolio.
- Exam traps:
  - Tightly linked projects are a program; unrelated projects only share the portfolio.

## 3.1.6 Project Management Office (PMO)

- Definition: permanent, adequately staffed unit owning project management processes.
- Use: maintains and develops procedures and standards.
- Objective: improve project and program management quality and secure success.
- The PMO governs processes, not project or program content.
- Audit impact: auditor distinguishes auditing content versus procedural aspects.
- Exam traps:
  - PMO checks how projects are run, not whether the solution is correct.

### Project portfolio management objectives
- Optimize portfolio results, not individual project results.
- Prioritize and schedule projects.
- Coordinate internal and external resources.
- Transfer knowledge across projects.

### Project portfolio database
- A portfolio database is mandatory for portfolio management.
- It holds owner, schedules, objectives, project type, status and cost.
- Typical reports: portfolio bar chart, profit-versus-risk matrix, progress graph.
- Audit impact: missing or stale database means weak portfolio prioritization.

## 3.1.7 Project benefits realization

- Definition: ensuring IT-enabled investments deliver promised, measurable business value.
- Use: capabilities arrive on time and within budget.
- IT assets must keep contributing to business value afterwards.
- Plan looks beyond project cycle to lifetime benefits and costs.
- Benefits rarely arrive exactly as planned, so strategies are checked and adjusted.

### Key elements
- Describe benefits management.
- Assign a measure and target.
- Establish a tracking and measuring regimen.
- Document assumptions.
- Establish key responsibilities for realization.
- Validate benefits predicted in the enterprise.
- Plan the benefit to be realized.

### Related points
- Benefits realization at project level has four phases (figure 3.4). ➕
- ⚠ Check: phases appear only in a figure; do not guess names.
- It is a compromise among cost, quality, delivery time, reliability and dependability.
- Auditor first understands how the enterprise defines value or ROI.
- Repeated failure to meet ROI objectives suggests weak SDLC and project management.
- It is continuous and needs business sponsorship. ➕
- Allow time for technical problems to settle before measuring benefits. ➕
- Worked example (ROI go/no-go): benefit 150,000, cost 100,000, cost of funds 8%.
  - Step 1: ROI = (benefit - cost) / cost = 50,000 / 100,000.
  - Step 2: ROI = 0.50, or 50%.
  - Step 3: 50% exceeds the 8% cost of funds, so go.
- Verification: 100,000 x 1.50 = 150,000, which confirms the 50% ROI.
- ⚠ Check: manual gives no ROI formula; this one is common practice. ➕
- Exam traps:
  - After go-live, auditor's primary focus is whether planned benefits are measured and reported.
  - Reviewing controls or change requests is secondary to benefits measurement.

## 3.1.8 Project initiation

- Information is gathered into terms of reference or a project charter.
- The charter states objective, stakeholders, project manager and sponsor.
- Approving the project initiation document (PID) or request document (PRD) authorizes start.
- Audit impact: no approved charter means no formal authority or accountability.

### Initiation methods
- One-on-one meetings give two-way communication between manager and team members.
- Kick-off meetings let the manager tell the team what must be done.
- Project start workshops build stakeholder buy-in and a common overview.
- Enterprises may combine methods.
- Document significant project events as project artifacts.

## 3.1.9 Project objectives

- Definition: action statements that support attainment of project goals.
- SMART: specific, measurable, attainable, realistic, timely.
- Audit impact: vague objectives make benefits and success impossible to verify.

### Breakdown structures
- Definition (OBS): hierarchy of the solution's components and their relationships.
- Use: helps with intangible results and avoids missing deliverables.
- Definition (WBS): hierarchy of tasks needed to build the OBS elements.
- Use: manageable work units, communications tool, cost and resource baseline.
- WBS is process-oriented and phased.
- WBS detail is the basis for negotiating detailed objectives.
- Figure 3.5 example: Services Online objective with WBS for Sales Application. ➕
- Example work packages: WP1 web page, WP2 sales interface. ➕
- Exam traps:
  - OBS lists what is built; WBS lists the work to build it.

### Work packages (WPs)
- Each WP has a distinct owner.
- Each WP lists main, additional and out-of-scope objectives.
- Specifications include dependencies and a performance evaluation method.
- The top WBS level is the final deliverable.
- Sub-deliverables contain WPs assigned to a unit.
- WPs should not exceed 10 days.
- WPs must be independent and not duplicated across the WBS.
- Task lists feed the project schedule.
- Schedule shows dates, percent complete, dependencies, names and stage boundaries.

## 3.1.10 Project planning

- Determine scope, agreed with stakeholders.
- Determine tasks, sequence, duration and priority.
- Determine IT and non-IT resources.
- Determine budget and source of funding.

### Cost estimation methods
- Definition (analogous): estimate from prior similar projects.
- Use (analogous): quickest, but least precise.
- Definition (parametric): uses statistical data such as hours and material costs.
- Use (parametric): more accurate than analogous.
- Definition (bottom-up): cost each activity in detail, then add everything.
- Use (bottom-up): most accurate, most time-consuming.
- Definition (actual costs): extrapolate from past costs of the same system.
- Exam traps:
  - Fast and rough means analogous; precise and slow means bottom-up.

### Software size estimation
- Definition: methods determining relative physical size of software.
- Use: guides resource allocation, time, cost and effort comparisons.
- Single-point estimates, such as source lines of code (SLOC), fail on complex systems.
- Multiple-point estimates were developed to overcome that limit.
- Modern technology uses diagrams, objects, spreadsheet cells, queries and GUI widgets.

### Function Point Analysis (FPA) ⭐
- Definition: multiple-point technique sizing software by user-visible functions.
- Use: estimating large business applications.
- It is an indirect measure of software size, unlike SLOC.
- Five counts: user inputs, outputs, inquiries, files, external interfaces.
- Each entry is rated simple, average or complex.
- Weights, inputs: 3 / 4 / 6.
- Weights, outputs: 4 / 5 / 7.
- Weights, inquiries: 3 / 4 / 6.
- Weights, files: 7 / 10 / 15.
- Weights, external interfaces: 5 / 7 / 10.
- Complexity adjustment covers reliability, criticality, complexity, reusability, changeability, portability.
- FP metrics: productivity = FP per person-month.
- FP metrics: quality = defects per FP; cost = dollars per FP.
- Works for business applications; weaker for OS, process control, communications.
- COCOMO suits those other software types.
- Exam does not test FPA calculation specifics.
- Audit impact: FPA gives auditors an objective basis to challenge estimates.
- Worked example, unadjusted count:
  - Step 1: 4 simple inputs x 3 = 12.
  - Step 2: 3 average outputs x 5 = 15.
  - Step 3: 2 complex inquiries x 6 = 12.
  - Step 4: 2 average files x 10 = 20.
  - Step 5: 1 simple interface x 5 = 5.
  - Step 6: total = 12+15+12+20+5 = 64.
- Worked example, adjustment and productivity:
  - Formula: adjusted FP = count x (0.65 + 0.01 x rating sum). ➕
  - Step 7: assume ratings sum to 42; multiplier = 0.65 + 0.42 = 1.07.
  - Step 8: adjusted FP = 64 x 1.07 = 68.48.
  - Step 9: with 8 person-months, productivity = 68.48 / 8 = 8.56 FP.
- Verification: row sum by column order (12+15=27, +12=39, +20=59, +5=64) gives 64.
- Verification: 64 x 1.07 = 64 + (64 x 0.07 = 4.48) = 68.48; 8.56 x 8 = 68.48.
- ⚠ Check: the manual gives no adjustment formula or 0.65/0.01 constants; treat as ➕.

### Cost budgets
- Personnel hours by type: analyst, programmer, clerical.
- Machine hours: computer time, duplication, office and communication equipment.
- External costs: third-party software, licences, contractors, training, certification, space.
- Budgeting totals human and machine effort; scheduling sequences the tasks.
- Two-step costing: sum effort per phase, then multiply by hourly rate.
- Revisit budget and schedule at milestones and analyze variances.
- Report variances to management on a timely basis.
- Worked example, two-step costing:
  - Design: analyst 100h x 80 = 8,000; programmer 200h x 60 = 12,000.
  - Build: programmer 400h x 60 = 24,000; clerical 50h x 30 = 1,500.
  - Build phase machine time: 100 h x 20 = 2,000.
  - Phase totals: design 20,000; build 27,500.
  - Project total = 20,000 + 27,500 = 47,500.
- Verification: by resource type: analyst 8,000; programmer 600 h x 60 = 36,000; clerical 1,500; machine 2,000; sum = 47,500.

### Software cost estimation
- Definition: cost result derived from software size estimation.
- Use: helps scope the project; automated tools exist for each phase.
- Cost drivers: source code language, execution time constraints.
- Cost drivers: main storage and data storage constraints.
- Cost drivers: computer access, target machine, security environment, staff experience.

### Scheduling and establishing the time frame
- Earliest start date follows logical sequence, with parallel tasks where possible.
- Latest expected finish considers hours, availability, holidays and recruitment time.

#### Gantt charts
- Show start and end of activities along a timeline.
- Show concurrent versus sequential activities.
- Show resource assignment and percent allocation.
- Compare early or late completion against the baseline.
- Track milestones, such as phase end or key deliverable.

#### Critical path method (CPM)
- Definition: the longest path through the project network.
- Use: gives the shortest possible completion time for the whole project.
- Critical activities have zero slack time.
- Noncritical activities have slack time.
- Slack = latest possible completion minus earliest possible completion.
- A forward and backward pass determine earliest completion times.
- Activities are branches; nodes join them in the network.
- Resource leveling smooths resource peaks and valleys, which are costly.
- Leveling uses heuristic methods, because few algorithmic methods exist.
- ⚠ Check: CPM says usually one critical path; PERT says only one.
- Exam traps:
  - Delay on a critical activity delays the project; delay within slack does not.
  - Critical path is longest, yet it gives the shortest project time.

#### Program Evaluation Review Technique (PERT)
- Definition: CPM-type technique using three duration estimates per activity.
- Estimates: optimistic, most likely, pessimistic.
- Formula: (optimistic + pessimistic + 4 x most likely) / 6.
- Assumes beta distribution, so probabilities and confidence levels are possible.
- Use: uncertain durations, such as pharmaceutical research or complex software.
- Events are milestones; activities are arrows.
- First identify all activities, events and their sequence.
- Critical path is unique; it can accelerate or delay the project.
- Worked example, activity estimates (O / M / P in weeks):
  - A: 2/5/14 gives (2 + 14 + 20) / 6 = 6.
  - B: 3/4/11 gives (3 + 11 + 16) / 6 = 5.
  - C: 4/6/8 gives (4 + 8 + 24) / 6 = 6.
  - D: 1/2/9 gives (1 + 9 + 8) / 6 = 3.
- Worked example, network and critical path:
  - Network: A then B then D; A then C then D.
  - Path A-B-D = 6 + 5 + 3 = 14 weeks.
  - Path A-C-D = 6 + 6 + 3 = 15 weeks.
  - Longest is A-C-D, so it is critical at 15 weeks.
- Worked example, forward and backward pass:
  - Forward: A ends 6; B 11; C 12; D starts 12, ends 15.
  - Backward: D latest finish 15; C latest finish 12; B latest finish 12.
  - B slack = latest minus earliest finish = 12 - 11 = 1.
  - Critical activities A, C, D have slack 0.
- Verification: path sums 14 and 15 match pass results; B slack also equals 15 - 14 = 1.
- Verification: recompute A: 6 x 6 = 36 = 2 + 14 + (4 x 5 = 20); matches.

#### Timebox management
- Definition: delivering software within a short, fixed time and preset resources.
- Use: supports prototyping and RAD-type approaches.
- It prevents cost overruns and delivery delays.
- Quality process is still needed.
- System test and user acceptance testing (UAT) are normally performed together.
- Audit impact: auditor checks that speed does not skip testing and quality review.
- Exam traps:
  - Timebox fixes time and resources; scope flexes, not the deadline.

## 3.1.11 Project execution

- The program manager with the PMO starts execution of planned tasks.
- Monitor production and quality metrics of internal teams, contractors and vendors.
- Key success factor: oversight of the integrated team from requirements to production transition.
- Audit impact: confirm vendor deliverables are measured, not trusted.

## 3.1.12 Project controlling and monitoring

- Controlling covers scope, resource usage and risk.
- New requirements are documented and allocated resources if approved.
- Stakeholder satisfaction needs accurate requirements, documentation, baselining and steering committee activity.
- Metrics are needed to monitor development effort.
- First step: identify resources such as skills, tools and facilities.

### Management of scope changes
- Scope is documented in the WBS as part of the baseline.
- A formal change request goes to the project manager.
- Only stakeholders may submit change requests.
- Archive all change requests in the project file.
- Project manager judges impact on scope, schedule and budget.
- Change advisory board evaluates for the sponsor and recommends.
- If accepted, the project manager updates the plan.
- Sponsor formally confirms the updated plan.
- Audit impact: unapproved changes ("scope creep") break baseline and cost control.
- Exam traps:
  - Developers never approve scope changes; sponsor confirms after board recommendation.

### Management of resource usage
- Resource usage is how the budget is being spent.
- Productivity is monitored too.
- Earned value analysis (EVA) compares metrics at regular intervals.
- EVA metrics: budget to date, actual spending to date.
- EVA metrics: estimate to complete, estimate at completion.
- Estimate to complete exceeding remaining plan means overrun.
- Worked example: one task, 3 days x 8 hours = 24 hours planned.
  - Step 1: after day 1, spent 8; remaining = 24 - 8 = 16.
  - Step 2: worker says 20 more hours are needed.
  - Step 3: 20 exceeds 16, so the task is overrunning.
  - Step 4: estimate at completion = 8 + 20 = 28 hours. ➕
  - Step 5: overrun = 28 - 24 = 4 hours.
- Verification: 4 / 24 = 16.7% over; also 20 - 16 = 4 hours, matching.
- ⚠ Check: manual shows only the 16-hour test; EAC formula is common practice. ➕

### Management of risk
- Definition: an uncertain event or condition affecting the project.
- Category 1 impacts business benefits; the sponsor mitigates.
- Category 2 impacts the project itself; the project manager mitigates.
- Exam traps:
  - Benefit-threatening risk belongs to sponsor; delivery risk belongs to project manager.

## 3.1.13 Project closing

- Hand the system to users or support staff.
- Assign outstanding issues.
- Sponsor must be satisfied the system is acceptable.
- Closure questions: who issues notice, when, team release, open actions and funding.
- Hand off documentation and duties; identify lessons learned.
- Post-project review is formal and happens at closing.
- Postimplementation review occurs later, after benefits and costs can be measured.
- Value metrics: total cost of ownership (TCO) and ROI.
- Audit impact: open issues without owners or funding signal weak closure.
- Exam traps:
  - Benefit measurement belongs in the postimplementation review, not at handover.

## 3.1.14 IS auditor's role in project management

- Take an active role in the life cycle where appropriate.
- Understand the system to identify vulnerabilities and control points.
- Advise the project team and senior management of deficiencies.
- Audit impact: early involvement is cheaper than finding control gaps after go-live.

### Typical tasks
- Meet key team members to learn components, objectives and user requirements.
- Discuss control selection to rank major risks and exposures.
- Discuss authoritative sources to identify mitigating controls.
- Evaluate controls and advise on design and implementation.
- Periodically review documentation and deliverables; confirm the methodology is followed.
- Review application audit trails for security, edit and processing controls.
- Identify and test production library security.
- Review test plans to verify requirements are covered.
- Analyze test results to evaluate the maintenance process.
- Review maintenance standards and test procedures are applied as described.
- Participate in postimplementation reviews.

### Change management system tracking
- Check history of work orders: date, programmer, changes, close date.
- Check history of programmer logons and logoffs.
- Check history of program deletions.
- Check adequacy of SoD and QA activities.
- Exam traps:
  - Auditor advises and tests; auditor does not make project decisions or own controls.

## Takeaway

- CISA tests structures, roles and controls, not any named standard.
- Sponsor funds and owns data; project manager controls schedule and budget.
- PMO governs process, never project content.
- Critical path is longest, has zero slack, and sets minimum duration.
- Bottom-up is most accurate; analogous is quickest; FPA sizes business applications.
- Post-implementation review measures benefits; auditor stays advisory to preserve later independence.
