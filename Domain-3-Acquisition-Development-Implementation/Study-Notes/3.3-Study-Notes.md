## 3.3.0 Methodology basics

- **Systems development methodology**
  - Definition: a structure an enterprise uses to plan and control development.
  - Use: organizes IS, software and new business application projects.
  - Audit impact: auditor judges whether the chosen method fits project requirements.
- Driver: systems grow complex and must deliver before the business changes.
- Auditor must know models to assess existence and effectiveness of development controls.
- The auditor also checks the method is used properly, not merely named.

## 3.3.1 Business application development

- **Life cycle**
  - Definition: defined phases covering build, deployment, maintenance and retirement.
  - Use: gives management control over building and operating applications.
  - Audit impact: each phase is an incremental foundation for the next.

### Application types

- **Organization-centric application**
  - Definition: collects, stores, archives and shares information on a need-to-know basis.
  - Use: sales data reaches accounts, administration and regulatory reporting.
  - Audit impact: usually built with the SDLC or detailed software engineering.
- **End-user-centric application**
  - Definition: gives different views of data to optimize performance.
  - Use: decision support systems (DSS) and geographic information systems (GIS).
  - Audit impact: mostly built with alternative approaches, so SDLC controls may be absent.

### Project triggers

- New opportunity tied to a new or existing business process.
- Problem with an existing business process.
- New opportunity enabled by technology.
- Problem with current technology.
- Need to align with partner or industry standard systems and interfaces.

### Key business drivers

- **Key business driver**
  - Definition: attribute of a business function driving its behavior toward strategic goals.
  - Use: translates enterprise objectives into requirements for all parties.
  - Audit impact: conflicting drivers found early prevent costly rework.
- Objectives should be SMART and expressed in scorecard form.
- Scorecards give objective evidence to measure value and prioritize requirements.
- Example: cost versus functionality is a typical conflicting driver pair.
- Projects start through defined procedures documenting need, solution and benefits.
- Internal and external factors affecting the problem must be identified.

## 3.3.2 SDLC models ⭐

- Manual lists three models: traditional waterfall, V-shaped and iterative.
- ⚠ Check: outline adds spiral ➕; manual does not name spiral here.
- Auditor links model choice to which controls are expected.

### Traditional waterfall

- **Waterfall model**
  - Definition: sequential model where each phase finishes before the next begins.
  - Use: oldest and most common model for business applications.
  - Audit impact: verification at each phase catches errors before final acceptance testing.
- Works best when requirements are stable and well defined.
- Determines system architecture relatively early.
- Useful for web applications needing screen prototypes.
- Advantage: template for placing methods in each phase.
- Problem: unanticipated events force iterations.
- Problem: users struggle to give explicit requirements up front.
- Problem: rejecting unwarranted requirements can cause project conflict.
- Problem: working system appears late, so users need patience.
- Problem: business change may alter requirements before delivery.
- Exam traps
  - Stable, well-defined requirements point to waterfall, not agile or prototyping.
  - Late working software is a waterfall weakness, not a strength.

### V-shaped (verification and validation) model

- **V-model**
  - Definition: links each development phase to a matching testing level.
  - Use: gives disciplined verification and validation throughout the life cycle.
  - Audit impact: strong assurance that requirements are met and security maintained.
- Unit test is most granular and validates detailed design.
- Integration test pairs with architectural design.
- System test relates to the architectural specification.
- UAT references the requirements.
- ⚠ Check: outline pairs integration with architecture; manual text says system test.
- Suits smaller projects.
- Problem: inflexible for complex or fast-changing projects.
- Problem: concurrent dependencies can delay the timeline.
- Problem: heavy documentation reduces development time.
- Exam traps
  - UAT maps to requirements, not to detailed design.
  - Unit testing maps to detailed design, not requirements.

### Iterative model

- **Iterative model**
  - Definition: cyclical process, each cycle runs requirements through testing.
  - Use: suits large projects delivering independent features periodically.
  - Audit impact: risk analysis is needed more often by a qualified resource.
- Each cycle incrementally improves the product.
- Problem: project management is more complex.
- Problem: overall completion date may be ambiguous.
- Exam traps
  - Ambiguous completion date signals iterative or agile, not waterfall.

## 3.3.3 SDLC phases ⭐

- Traditional SDLC has six phases, each with goals, activities and outcomes.
- Phases and deliverables are decided during early planning.
- Other interpretations use different phase counts and names.
- Maintenance work may need fewer phases or less detail.
- Purchased packages replace design and development with selection and configuration.
- Phase list: feasibility, requirements, selection or design, configuration or development, testing, review.

### Phase 1: Feasibility study

- **Feasibility study**
  - Definition: analysis of benefits and solutions for the identified problem.
  - Use: builds the business case justifying the next phase.
  - Audit impact: auditor reviews it to judge if buy-or-build decision was sound.
- Business case states strategic benefits: productivity gains or cost avoidance.
- It quantifies cost savings and estimates payback schedule or ROI.
- Intangibles count too: user readiness and process maturity.
- Benefits should be quantified wherever possible.
- Defines a time frame for implementation.
- Determines the optimum risk-based alternative, such as develop or acquire.
- Checks whether the existing system fixes it with little change (workaround).
- Checks whether a vendor product solves the problem.
- Estimates approximate cost to develop.
- Checks fit with business strategy.
- Output: comparative report on costs, benefits, risk, resources and organizational impact.
- Report recommends one alternative and a course of action.
- **Impact assessment**
  - Definition: study of future effects of a project on current projects and resources.
  - Use: lists advantages and limitations of a chosen course.
  - Audit impact: shows hidden resource conflicts before approval.
- Develop-or-acquire factors:
  - Date the system must be functional.
  - Cost to develop versus buy.
  - Staff availability, skills and hardware needed.
  - License type (yearly or perpetual) and maintenance cost.
  - Interfaces with systems that supply or use its data.
  - Strategic plan fit, risk appetite and regulatory compliance.
  - IT infrastructure compatibility, future change needs and domain expertise.
- Exam traps
  - Feasibility asks "should we, and build or buy", not "how to design".

### Phase 2: Requirements definition 📓⭐

- **Requirements definition**
  - Definition: specifying business requirements of the system chosen in feasibility.
  - Use: describes what system does, user interaction, conditions and information criteria.
  - Audit impact: best phase to build in security, privacy and audit trails.
- Includes nonfunctional requirements, for example access control.
- Many security and privacy weaknesses are fixed by early focus here.
- Activities:
  - Identify and consult stakeholders.
  - Identify data privacy and governance requirements.
  - Analyze requirements for conflicts and set priorities.
  - Identify system boundaries and environment interaction.
  - Identify security requirements.
  - Convert user requirements to system requirements, such as a UI prototype.
  - Record requirements in a structured format or management tool.
  - Resolve stakeholder conflicts and conflicts with available resources.
- Good requirements are complete, consistent, unambiguous, verifiable, modifiable, testable, traceable.
- Example: a requirements tool stores requirements in a shared multiuser database.
- Fixing requirement errors later costs far more, so reviews pay off.
- Outputs: preliminary design, project schedule and resource commitments.
- Management sponsorship and user involvement are needed to realize benefits.
- Auditor checks security engineering team involvement across the data life cycle.
- Auditor checks audit trails are defined.
- Auditor identifies regulatory, statutory and legal requirements.
- Exam traps
  - Auditor consulted at requirements phase to recommend controls and cost-effectiveness.
  - Design, testing or implementation are too late; changes cost more.
  - Consulting is advisory; auditor must not design or own the controls.

### Phase 3A: Software selection and acquisition

- **Software acquisition**
  - Definition: buying a vendor package instead of developing in-house.
  - Use: follows requirements definition when the decision is to acquire.
  - Audit impact: auditor reviews the decision, RFP and vendor selection.
- It is not a standard SDLC phase.
- Weigh ability, cost, risk and ownership against vendor dependence.
- Decision factors: cost gap, generic software availability and time gap.
- Users must be actively involved in package evaluation and selection.
- Prepare an RFP stating requirements to invite supplier bids.

### Phase 3B: Design

- **Design phase**
  - Definition: detailed blueprint built from preliminary design and requirements.
  - Use: sets baseline of system and subsystem specifications.
  - Audit impact: controls must be built into specifications and test plans.
- Includes program and database specifications and security considerations.
- Establishes a formal change control process against uncontrolled new requirements.
- Team defines software architecture, then decomposes it into modules and components.
- Several design iterations may be needed before coding detail is reached.
- Key activities:
  - System flowcharts and entity relationship models.
  - Structured top-down design techniques.
  - Input and output design: screens and reports.
  - Prototyping tool, if used, mostly supports screen design in an IDE.
  - Processing steps and computation rules.
  - Data file or database design.
  - Program specifications.
  - Data conversion plans.
  - Risk assessment of data flows and personal data sharing.
- Test plan levels:
  - Unit (program), subsystem (module), integration (system), interface.
  - Loading and initializing files, stress, security, backup and recovery.
- Detailed conversion plans reduce problems from incompatible data and unfamiliar staff.
- User involvement is limited once processes are documented.
- End users normally do not review detailed technical design.
- Developers must explain how architecture meets requirements and why key decisions were made.
- Hardware and software choices may carry cost and control implications.
- Key documents: system, subsystem, program and database specs, test plans, change control process.
- Design goes to developers after user approval and software baselining.

#### Software baselining

- **Software baseline (design freeze)**
  - Definition: cutoff point in design after which requirements are frozen.
  - Use: stops requirements entering development without formal review.
  - Audit impact: missing baseline signals scope creep risk.
- Design phase is the optimum point to baseline.
- Requirements are reviewed item by item for time and cost.
- Changes need formal approval based on cost-benefit impact analysis.
- Failure to baseline leads mainly to scope creep.
- Work products become configuration items with version numbers.
- Items include functional requirements, specifications and test plans.
- Configuration items come under formal change management control.
- Exam traps
  - Scope creep is controlled by baselining plus formal change control.

#### Risks of software development

- **Strategic risk**
  - Definition: goals weighted without considering enterprise strategy.
  - Audit impact: project may be misaligned with what matters most.
- **Business (benefit) risk**
  - Definition: system fails to meet user needs and expectations.
  - Audit impact: likely underused, unmaintained and soon obsolete.
- **Project (delivery) risk**
  - Definition: activities exceed financial limits, finish late or never.
  - Audit impact: exists at five levels.
- Project risk levels:
  - Within the project: wrong requirements or poor delivery management.
  - With suppliers: unclear communication causing late or deficient delivery.
  - Within the enterprise: stakeholders withhold inputs, or priorities and politics shift.
  - External environment: customers, competitors, regulators and economy.
  - Chosen technology: displaced or poorly supported in the market.
- Primary cause is lack of discipline or an inappropriate methodology.
- Low maturity means successes are not repeatable.
- Following an SDLC alone does not guarantee project success.

#### Auditor review of project management discipline

- Project meets cooperative goals and objectives.
- Planning includes estimates of resources, budget and time.
- Scope creep is controlled and a software baseline exists.
- Management tracks design and development activities.
- Senior management support is present.
- Periodic review and risk analysis occur in every phase.

#### Structured analysis, design and development techniques

- **Structured techniques**
  - Definition: graphic notations at falling abstraction levels until coding is possible.
  - Use: tied to the traditional SDLC approach.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks processes are defined, documented and followed.
- Early activities: system context diagrams, hierarchical data flow and control flow decomposition.
- Also control transformations, minispecifications and data dictionaries.
- Define external events, then one transformation data flow diagram (DFD) each.
- Later design adds flowcharts, inputs, outputs, computations and file specifications.
- Modular top-down representation lets programmers develop and test linearly.

#### Entity relationship diagrams (ERDs)

- **ERD**
  - Definition: diagram of system data and how the data relate.
  - Use: logical data model in requirements, physical model (schema) in design.
  - Audit impact: shows data the system must capture and manage.
- **Entity**: grouping of like data elements, drawn as a rectangle.
- **Attribute**: property or characteristic of an entity.
- **Primary key**: attribute or combination uniquely identifying each entity instance.
- Exam traps
  - Logical model belongs to requirements; physical model belongs to design.

#### IS auditor's role in project design

- Main focus: controls in specifications and test plans.
- Check continuous online auditing functions are built in, especially for ecommerce.
- Evaluate design process effectiveness: structured design, prototyping, test plans, baselining.
- Review system flowcharts for adherence to general design.
- Verify user management approved all changes.
- Review input, processing and output controls.
- Interview key users about understanding and input to screens and reports.
- Assess audit trails for traceability and accountability.
- Verify integrity of key calculations and processes.
- Verify system identifies and processes erroneous data correctly.
- Review QA results of programs developed.
- Verify corrections were made and audit trails or embedded audit modules (EAMs) were coded.
- Perform a risk assessment.

### Phase 4A: Configuration

- **Configuration (acquired systems)**
  - Definition: defining, tracking and controlling changes in an acquired system.
  - Use: tailor a package by setting control parameters rather than changing code.
  - Audit impact: direct code change breaks vendor support and weakens control.
- Packages switch functions on or off through parameter tables.
- Interface programs may be needed to connect to existing systems.
- ERP work: edit configuration tables plus some development for integration.
- On-premises versus cloud can limit or alter configuration options.
- Change management policy defines roles and responsibilities.
- Changes are classified and prioritized by business risk.
- Impact assessment precedes each change.
- Business process owners and IT authorize changes.
- Changes are tracked with status.
- Data changes occur under system control, not direct user intervention.

### Phase 4B: Development

- **Development phase**
  - Definition: programmers and analysts build the system from detailed design.
  - Use: work occurs in a test or development environment.
  - Audit impact: segregate environments and control access to code.
- Code and write program and system documents.
- Debug and test programs.
- Build data conversion programs.
- Create user transition procedures.
- Train selected users.
- Document vendor software modifications so future updates apply.
- Identify secure coding and configuration standards for security by design.
- Testing includes unit, system and several UAT iterations.

#### Programming methods and techniques

- **Coding standards**
  - Definition: rules for documentation, data declaration, statements and I/O.
  - Use: let code be read without referring to design specifications.
  - Audit impact: an essential control; minimizes setbacks from staff turnover.
- Standards also ease maintenance and modification.
- **Structured programming**
  - Definition: dividing programs into subsystems, modules, subroutines and units.
  - Use: easier to develop, understand and maintain.
  - Audit impact: eases tracing where changes apply.
- **Cohesion**: how single-purpose each software item is.
- **Coupling**: how independent an item is from others.
- High cohesion and low coupling reduce unintended consequences of changes.
- Exam traps
  - Good design means high cohesion and low coupling, never the reverse.

#### Online programming facilities (IDE)

- **Online programming facility**
  - Definition: interactive coding and compiling from a terminal or workstation.
  - Use: lowers cost, speeds response and expands programmer aids.
  - Audit impact: introduces version, integrity and overwrite risks.
- Risk: multiple program versions proliferate.
- Risk: unauthorized access and updating reduce program integrity.
- Risk: valid changes can be overwritten by other changes.
- Mitigation: access control software.
- Facility also enforces standards and structured programming.

#### Programming languages

- **Source and object code**
  - Definition: source is human-written; compiler translates it to machine object code.
  - Use: object code is what the computer executes.
  - Audit impact: decompilation can recover source from object code.
- High-level general purpose: COBOL and C.
- Object-oriented business languages: C++, Eiffel, Java.
- IDEs such as Visual Studio or JBuilder supply coding templates.
- Web scripting: HTML, JavaScript, CSS.
- Data-centric: R, Scala, IDL, SAS.
- Scripting: shell, Perl, Tcl, Python, VBScript.
- CGI scripts extend web servers with search interfaces and dynamic pages.
- High-level assembler targets embedded devices such as slot machines and vending machines.
- 4GLs combine a DBMS with nonprocedural report and screen generation.
- Decision support or expert systems: EXPRESS, Lisp, Prolog.
- **Low code/no code (LCNC)**
  - Definition: platforms letting non-developers build applications.
  - Audit impact: removing traditional developers has control implications to assess.

#### Program debugging

- **Debugging**
  - Definition: finding and fixing coding flaws and abends before production.
  - Use: bugs are found by running programs in the test environment.
  - Audit impact: unfixed abends reach production.
- **Abend**: unplanned program ending caused by programming errors.
- Compilers give some feedback but are not debugging tools.
- **Logic path monitor**: reports sequence of events, hinting at logic errors.
- **Memory dump**: snapshot of memory at failure, showing data and parameter inconsistencies.
- **Trace**: dumps at several stages showing counters and registers changing.
- **Output analyzer**: compares expected results with actual results.
- Exam traps
  - A compiler is not a debugging tool.

### Phase 5: Final testing and implementation

- **Final testing and implementation**
  - Definition: establishing and testing actual operation of the new system.
  - Use: final UAT and user sign-off.
  - Audit impact: last checkpoint before production exposure.
- May include certification and accreditation.
- Security testing includes penetration testing and controls validation.
- After full-system test, production turnover happens on a set migration date.
- Large or complex systems may need a separate phased project.
- Implementation plan is created in design and revised during development.
- Each step names owner, verification method and backout procedure.
- Commissioning tests prove end-to-end connectivity across interfaced platforms.
- Ensure test transactions do not remain in production files.
- For acquired software, user management coordinates implementation.
- Do not delegate whole implementation to the vendor.
- Reason: avoid unauthorized changes or malicious code from vendor staff.
- **Site acceptance testing**
  - Definition: full-system test in the actual operations environment.
  - Use: confirms production readiness against documented requirements.
- Exam traps
  - Vendor must not run implementation alone; user management stays accountable.
  - Backout procedure is required for every implementation step.

### Phase 6: Post-implementation review

- **Post-implementation review**
  - Definition: formal check after a new or heavily modified system goes live.
  - Use: verifies proper design, development and built-in controls.
  - Audit impact: tests whether promised benefits arrived.
- Assess adequacy: user requirements, business objectives and controls.
- Evaluate projected cost benefits or ROI against feasibility findings.
- Recommend fixes for deficiencies and plan their implementation.
- Assess the project process: methodologies, standards and project management techniques followed.
- Exam traps
  - Primary focus: are planned cost benefits measured and reported, per my notes. 📓
  - Reviewing control design and integrity also matters; read the stem for priority.

## 3.3.4 IS auditor's role in SDLC project management

- Analyze risk and exposures inherent in each SDLC phase.
- Confirm control mechanisms minimize risk cost-effectively.
- Never recommend controls costing more than the risk they reduce.
- Obtain documentation from each phase.
- Attend project team meetings and advise the team.
- Assess the team's ability to deliver key items on promised dates.

### Project management activities to review

- Oversight level by project committee or board.
- Risk management methods within the project.
- Issue management.
- Cost management.
- Planning and dependency management.
- Reporting to senior management.
- Change control.
- Stakeholder involvement.
- Sign-off by both systems development and user management.
- Exam traps
  - Minimum sign-off needs development and user management responsible for cost or use.

### Expected SDLC documentation

- Objectives for each phase.
- Key deliverables with personnel assigned direct responsibility.
- Schedule highlighting completion dates for key deliverables.
- Economic forecast per phase: resources and their cost.

## 3.3.5 Software development methods

- Method choice depends on enterprise policy, developer preference and technology.
- Method selection is independent of project organization model.
- Example: object-oriented methods can run under waterfall or agile organization.

### Prototyping / evolutionary development

- **Prototyping**
  - Definition: quickly building a working model to test design aspects.
  - Use: gathers early user feedback; also called heuristic or evolutionary development.
  - Audit impact: reduces requirement risk but often weakens controls.
- Acts as a risk reduction mechanism at each evolutionary level.
- Combines systematic SDLC steps with an iterative framework.
- Emphasis starts with reports and screens users see most.
- Approach 1: build model to define requirements, then design the real system.
- Approach 2: gradually build the production system using a suitable 4GL.
- Risk: pressure to implement early prototype as final.
- Risk: users miss need for volumes, connectivity, backup, security, privacy and auditability.
- Approach 2 suits small applications; large efforts need a design strategy.
- Risk: change control weak; changes undocumented and unapproved, hurting maintainability.
- Risk: unrequested extras added beyond requirements.
- Enhancements beyond requirements need strategic and cost-effectiveness review.
- Risk: missing controls like backup recovery, security and audit trails.
- Can still give significant time and cost savings.
- Exam traps
  - Prototyping risk answer: poor change control and missing controls, not slow delivery.

### Rapid Application Development (RAD)

- **RAD**
  - Definition: methodology to build strategic systems quickly at lower cost.
  - Use: relies on prototyping as its core tool.
  - Audit impact: does not support enterprise-wide planning or analysis.
- Techniques: small trained teams, evolutionary prototypes, power tools, central repository.
- Also interactive workshops and rigid time frame limits.
- Automates parts of the SDLC and reuses components.
- Stage 1, concept definition: functions, data subject areas and scope.
- Stage 2, functional design: workshops model data and processes; prototype critical parts.
- Stage 3, development: physical database, application, conversion system, user aids, deployment plans.
- Stage 4, deployment: user testing, training, data conversion and implementation.
- Contrast: OOSD and data-oriented development focus on reusable, maintainable code.
- Contrast: BPR converts an existing process rather than making dynamic changes.

### Agile development

- **Agile development**
  - Definition: iterative, incremental approach assuming requirements cannot be fully known upfront.
  - Use: handles change flexibly; Scrum is the best-known method.
  - Audit impact: little documentation and no requirements baseline challenge traditional audit evidence.
- Scrum moves planning and directing from project manager to team.
- Other methods: XP, Crystal, ASD, FDD, DSDM.
- Uses small time-boxed iterations; each forms the basis for the next.
- Sprint is the Scrum iteration.
- Replan and reprioritize at each iteration end.
- Relies more on tacit knowledge than documentation.
- Teams are small, co-located, with business and technical members.
- Daily meetings occur with strict time limits.
- Pair programming shares knowledge and checks quality.
- Project manager becomes facilitator and advocate.
- Plans only the next iteration in detail.
- Adaptive requirements; no emphasis on managing a requirements baseline.
- Proves architecture by building functionality quickly.
- Frequent-build test cycle validates functions and fixes problems early.
- Relies on frequent inspections, not defined repeatable processes.
- Exam traps
  - Agile does not ignore controls; it addresses them differently.
  - Lack of formal documentation is an audit risk, not a reason to skip review.

### Object-oriented system development

- **Object-oriented system development (OOSD)**
  - Definition: grouping data and procedures into one entity called an object.
  - Use: models solutions closer to real-world objects.
  - Audit impact: it is a programming technique, not a methodology.
- **Attributes** are the object's data.
- **Methods** are the object's functionality.
- Contrasts with structured SDLC, which separates data from procedures.
- **Class**: template holding characteristics without specific data.
- **Superclass** (parent) holds basic attributes and methods.
- **Subclass** inherits parent characteristics and may add or remove functions.
- Classes may share data (aggregate grouping) or share objects.
- **Message**: request for service from a client class to a server class.
- **Polymorphism**: objects interpret the same message differently at execution.
- Advantages: manages unrestricted data types.
- Advantages: models complex relationships.
- Advantages: meets demands of a changing environment.
- **Unified Modeling Language (UML)**
  - Definition: general notation for specifying and visualizing complex software.
  - Use: large object-oriented projects.
- Applications: web, ebusiness, CASE, office automation, AI, computer-assisted manufacturing.
- Exam traps
  - OOSD is not itself a development methodology; it pairs with any method.

### Component-based development

- **Component-based development**
  - Definition: assembling applications from packages exposing services through defined interfaces.
  - Use: outgrowth of object-oriented development.
  - Audit impact: reuse brings integration, licensing and attack surface risks.
- In-process client components need a parent host container.
- Stand-alone client components expose services, for example Excel and Word.
- Stand-alone server components provide services via web services, app servers or LDAP.
- In-process server components run in server containers, for example MTS and EJB.
- ⚠ Check: outline says Enterprise JavaBeans; manual example text says Oracle JavaBeans.
- Benefit: reduces development time.
- Benefit: improves quality through pretested code.
- Benefit: lets developers focus on business functionality.
- Benefit: promotes modularity.
- Benefit: simplifies reuse without source code.
- Benefit: reduces development cost.
- Benefit: supports multiple development environments.
- Benefit: compromise between build and buy.
- Poorly defined requirements still doom the project.
- Risk: lack of interoperability standards.
- Risk: reduced system reliability.
- Risk: unintended use introducing vulnerabilities.
- Risk: external exposure from internet connection.
- Risk: larger attack surface from integrating components.
- Risk: update problems and version incompatibility with requirements.
- Risk: licensing problems across components.

### Web-based application development

- **Web-based application development**
  - Definition: approach for easier integration of code modules within and between enterprises.
  - Use: replaces dedicated APIs; XML standardizes module integration.
  - Audit impact: classic risks such as buffer overflows persist.
- CORBA and COM use remote procedure calls (RPCs) but remain complex.
- Web services avoid redundant code, using one common update process.
- Example: one change-of-address update reaches several customer databases.
- Take a risk-based approach: business goals, IT goals, then what can go wrong.
- Typical risks: poor specifications, coding, documentation, QA/QC and change control.
- **SOAP**
  - Definition: XML-based protocol defining APIs across any OS or language.
  - Use: loosely couples modules so one change rarely forces others.
- **WSDL**
  - Definition: XML language naming the SOAP specification and message formats.
  - Use: identifies the web service reachable on intranet or internet.
- **UDDI**
  - Definition: XML-based directory entry listing available web services.
  - Use: lets interested parties discover services.

### Software reengineering

- **Software reengineering**
  - Definition: updating an existing system by extracting and reusing design and components.
  - Use: supports major changes in how the enterprise operates.
  - Audit impact: verify controls survive redesign.
- BPR: thorough analysis and redesign of processes and management systems.
- Service-oriented reengineering applies RAD concepts using RACI charts and modeling.

### Reverse engineering

- **Reverse engineering**
  - Definition: studying an application to build a similar system.
  - Use: decompile object code to source, or black-box test functionality.
  - Audit impact: legal and technical risks need review.
- Advantage: faster development and shorter SDLC.
- Advantage: chance to improve on original drawbacks.
- Risk: license clauses may prohibit reverse engineering to protect trade secrets.
- Risk: decompilers depend on machine, OS and language, so changes need new tools.

### DevOps and DevSecOps

- **DevOps**
  - Definition: integrating development and operations to remove conflicts and barriers.
  - Use: faster delivery; adoption depends on climate, risk tolerance, culture and scope.
  - Audit impact: auditor ensures proper segregation of duties (SoD).
- DevOps changes the control environment and accepted risk level.
- **DevSecOps**
  - Definition: DevOps with security built into the pipeline.
  - Use: promotes security by design.
  - Audit impact: lowers likelihood of vulnerabilities introduced during development.
- Exam traps
  - DevOps audit concern is SoD between developers and production, not speed.

### Business Process Reengineering (BPR) and process change

- **Process**
  - Definition: input is processed and the outcome measured against a goal.
  - Use: monitor effectiveness, efficiency and compliance under the PDCA cycle.
- **BPR**
  - Definition: redesign of processes responding to competitive, economic and customer pressure.
  - Use: usually automates, giving fewer manual interventions and manual controls.
  - Audit impact: key controls may be reengineered out.
- **Package-enabled reengineering (PER)**: BPR achieved through ERP implementation.
- BPR steps:
  - Define areas to review.
  - Develop project plan.
  - Understand the process under review.
  - Redesign and streamline.
  - Implement and monitor.
  - Establish continuous improvement.
- Results include new priorities, process focus and new people approaches.
- Also new technology use, refined supplier roles and redefined customer roles.
- Decompose to the **elementary process**: unit of work with defined input and output.
- Identify customers and process owners responsible end to end.
- Profile each elementary process:
  - Duration, trigger, frequency, effort, responsibility.
  - Input, output, external interfaces, system interaction.
  - Risk and control information, performance measures, problem areas and root causes.
- Document baseline processes as flowcharts for later comparison.
- Be sensitive to culture, structure and direction.
- Major concern: key controls reengineered out.
- Auditor identifies key controls and evaluates impact of removal.
- If a key preventive control goes, management must knowingly accept the risk.
- Auditor checks changes fit culture and strategic plan.
- Auditor checks team minimizes negative impact on staff.
- Auditor checks lessons learned were documented.
- Exam traps
  - First BPR audit concern: key controls removed, and management aware of the risk.

#### Benchmarking process

- **Benchmarking**
  - Definition: continuous, systematic evaluation against world-class reference enterprises.
  - Use: comparing, SWOT, investment decisions, process design and BPR.
  - Audit impact: gap findings should drive action plans.
- Step 1, plan: identify critical processes, measures and data needs.
- Step 2, research: baseline own data first, then identify references.
- Step 3, observe: collect data and visit the partner under agreement.
- Step 4, analyze: gap analysis converted into operational goals.
- Step 5, adopt: translate findings into principles, strategies, then action plans.
- Step 6, improve: continuous improvement tied to enterprise goals.
- Adopt is often the most difficult step.
- Steps three to six may be skipped or adapted after research.

## 3.3.6 System development tools and productivity aids

- Aids include CASE, code generators and 4GLs.

### Computer-Aided Software Engineering (CASE)

- **CASE**
  - Definition: automated tools supporting requirements, design, code, testing and documentation.
  - Use: uniform approach, easier document storage, less manual effort.
  - Audit impact: changes the development process and how specifications are verified.
- **Upper CASE**: business and application requirements, data-object and process definitions.
- **Middle CASE**: detailed designs, screen and report layouts, process flows.
- **Lower CASE**: generates program code and database definitions.
- An IDE spans several categories.
- Some systems build applications from DFDs with no traditional source code.
- Then the DFDs and data elements become the source code.
- Auditor checks specification approvals were obtained.
- Auditor checks users stay involved in development.
- Auditor checks CASE investment yields quality and speed benefits.
- CASE does not ensure design, program or system correctness.
- A project methodology must exist for CASE to work.
- Monitor integrity of data moved between tools or manual processes.
- Reflect application changes in stored CASE data.
- Design application controls as for a traditional application.
- Secure the CASE repository on need-to-know with strict version control.
- Auditor can use CASE for DFDs as alternative flowcharts.
- Auditor can use CASE to develop interrogation software and EAMs.
- Repository reports help understand the system and review development controls.
- Exam traps
  - CASE aids design but never guarantees correct or complete design.

### Code generators

- **Code generator**
  - Definition: tool generating code from analyst parameters or DFD and entity diagrams.
  - Use: often part of CASE, boosting developer efficiency.
  - Audit impact: auditor should be aware of generated source code.

### Fourth-generation languages (4GL)

- **4GL**
  - Definition: high-level languages reducing development effort and cost.
  - Use: nonprocedural and event-driven, using objects, properties and methods.
  - Audit impact: may lack low-level commands for data-intensive or online operations.
- Example: a 4GL treats a report as an object with sort and print methods.
- Environmental independence means portability across architectures and OSs.
- Software facilities: screen painting, help screens, graphical output.
- Programmer workbench concept equals an IDE, tied to CASE.
- Simple language subsets suit less-skilled information center users.
- Query and report generators extract data, as audit software does.
- Embedded database 4GLs use self-contained DBMSs, integrating poorly.
- Embedded 4GL examples: FOCUS, RAMIS II, NOMAD 2.
- Relational database 4GLs are DBMS options, not end-user oriented.
- Relational 4GL examples: SQL+, MANTIS, Natural.
- Application generators produce 3GL code (COBOL, C) for IT staff.

## 3.3.7 Infrastructure development / acquisition practices

- **Physical architecture analysis**
  - Definition: assessing current architecture, defining new one and the roadmap between.
  - Use: decisions affect operations, training, installation and total cost of ownership (TCO).
  - Audit impact: choice must be formal and reasoned, not price alone.
- Conflicting requirements mean no single platform satisfies all equally.
- Examples of conflicts: legacy hardware, zero data loss, 24/7 availability.

### The suggested solution must

- Align with enterprise ICT standards.
- Provide appropriate security and privacy.
- Integrate with current IT systems.
- Consider IT industry trends.
- Provide future operational flexibility for business processes.
- Allow growth without major upgrades.
- Include technical architecture for security and secure storage.
- Ensure cost-effective day-to-day operational support.
- Foster standardized hardware and software.
- Maximize ROI, cost transparency and operational efficiency.

### Project phases of physical architecture analysis

- Review current architecture documents.
- Workshop 1 includes ICT specialists in server, storage, security and infrastructure.
- Workshop 1 output: component list and constraints defining target architecture.
- Operational constraints: ground, size, weight, power supply, environment, physical security.
- Draft functional requirements are written; vendor selection runs in parallel.
- Then analysis and design of the actual physical architecture.
- Functional requirements are written and presented at workshop 2 with all affected parties.
- Workshop 2 is the last checkpoint before sizing and proof of concept.
- Workshop 3 follows delivery of the prototype.
- **Proof of concept (POC)**
  - Definition: demonstration that hardware, software and data meet expectations, including security.
  - Use: deliverable is a running prototype with documents and test protocols.
  - Audit impact: highly recommended before committing to full deployment.
- POC uses a representative subset of target hardware.
- Software may be test versions or vendor-supplied, keeping costs minimal.
- Framework elements are simplified, then extended later.
- Consider outsourcing, offshoring, cloud and virtualization during POC.
- Regulated or high-availability industries need redundancy and privacy safeguards tested.

### Planning implementation of infrastructure

- Prototype should show core security infrastructure setup.
- It shows auditing components work correctly.
- It shows security measures and secured transactions.
- It shows installation constraints: size, power, weight, room security.
- It shows performance and resiliency, including basic failover.
- It shows the funding and costing model.
- Also data and algorithm.
- Use a phased approach; overlapping phases make it iterative.
- Requirements analysis feeds all phases but is not part of them.
- Procurement phase: business and analysis project communicate; requirements statements produced.
- Procurement starts service-level management; negotiations, deliverables, contracts and SLAs are signed.
- Delivery time: delivery plan with priorities, goals, key indicators and responsibilities.
- Installation plan: built with all affected parties, reviewed iteratively.
- Installation test plan: test cases, requirements, processes and measurement information.
- Test plan needs completed architecture analysis and infrastructure decisions.

## 3.3.8 Hardware / software acquisition

- Specifications go to vendors in an invitation to tender (ITT), also called RFP.
- Specifications must define usage, tasks and requirements as completely as possible.
- Hardware may also mean cloud providers replacing traditional hardware.

### Specification contents

- Organizational description: centralized, distributed or outsourced; evaluation assurance levels (EALs).
- Information processing: current and future systems, workload, online or batch, real-time.
- Hardware: CPU speed, disk, memory, CPU count.
- Peripherals required or excluded, usually for security.
- Input devices and direct entry devices such as POS and ATM.
- Networking capability and number of terminals or nodes.
- System software: OS, utilities, compilers, program library, DBMS.
- Also communications, access control and job scheduling software.
- Support: maintenance, training, backups, patching.
- Adaptability: upgrades, compatibility, changeover capability.
- Constraints: staffing, existing hardware capacity, delivery dates.
- Conversion: test time, conversion facilities, pricing schedule.

### Acquisition steps

- Seek testimonials or visits with other users.
- Provide for competitive bidding.
- Analyze bids against requirements and against each other.
- Use predefined evaluation criteria.
- Analyze vendor financial condition.
- Analyze maintenance and support capability, including training.
- Review delivery schedules against requirements.
- Verify hardware pedigree; avoid gray market sources raising malware risk.
- Analyze upgrade capability and security and control facilities.
- Evaluate performance against requirements.
- Review and negotiate price.
- Review contract terms: warranties, penalties, right-to-audit.
- Write a formal report justifying selection by benefits and cost.

### RFP, vendor evaluation, contract, software escrow ⭐

- Criteria and data for evaluating proposals must be planned and documented.
- **Vendor evaluation criteria**
  - Project schedule: vendor timetable compared with client plan.
  - Turnaround time: help desk or vendor fix time from logging.
  - Response time: system answer to a specific user query.
  - System reaction time: time to log in or connect to network.
  - Throughput: useful work per unit time, such as Mbps.
  - Workload: volume of work vendor system handles in a time frame.
  - Compatibility: existing application runs successfully on the new system.
  - Capacity: simultaneous requests and data volume per user.
  - Utilization: availability time versus downtime.
- Auditor checks acquisition began with a business need and hardware requirements.
- Auditor checks several vendors were compared on the criteria.
- Exam traps
  - Turnaround time is fix time; response time is query time. Do not swap.
  - Throughput measures useful work, not raw speed or capacity.

## 3.3.9 System software acquisition

- IT management must know HW/SW capabilities that improve business processes.
- Short- and long-term plans document migration to newer OSs and system software.

### Selection issues

- Business, functional and technical needs.
- Cost and benefits.
- Obsolescence.
- Compatibility with existing systems.
- Security.
- Demands on existing staff.
- Training and hiring requirements.
- Future growth needs.
- Impact on system and network performance.
- Open source versus proprietary code.
- Feasibility study should document the decision to acquire.

### Four cases for software requirement

- Generic process: vendor software implemented without customization.
- Vendor software customized to business processes.
- Software developed by the vendor.
- Software as a service (SaaS) through the cloud, generally for generic processes.

### RFP process

- Project team of technical staff and key users writes the RFP or ITT.
- Write a separate RFP for each case.
- Distribute widely, ideally via public procurement media.
- ITT fits when products and services are known, typically hardware, network, database.
- RFP fits when a solution plus support is needed, such as ERP.
- **Request for information (RFI)**
  - Definition: early request asking vendors for advice on existing system problems.
  - Use: informs the later RFP.
- Compare responses objectively using scoring and ranking.
- Narrow to a short list of two or three vendors.
- Run gap analysis between requirements and software, including needed modifications.
- Check minimum requirements: hardware, OS versions and patches, tools, databases.
- **Agenda-based presentation**
  - Definition: vendor demonstrates scripted business scenarios.
  - Use: compares short-listed vendors objectively, with user participation.
- Finalist arranges site visits to confirm findings in a live environment.
- **Conference-room pilot**: hands-on session with end users after finalist confirmed.
- Pilot reveals needed customizations or workarounds.
- Document reasons for the final selection.

### RFP contents

- Product versus system requirements; decide whether to accept deficiencies or customize.
- Product scalability and interoperability.
- Customer references, checked to validate vendor claims.
- Vendor security requirements, including right to audit.
- Vendor viability and financial stability; new products carry higher risk.
- Availability of complete and reliable documentation.
- Documentation quality hints at design and programming quality.
- Vendor support: help line, onsite training, upgrades, new version notification, maintenance.
- **Software escrow**
  - Definition: third party holds source code, released if vendor fails.
  - Use: protects the buyer if the vendor goes out of business.
  - Audit impact: agreement should include product updates and fixes.
- Years of experience offering the product.
- Recent or planned enhancements with dates; short list suggests stale product.
- Number of client sites and current user list.
- Acceptance testing of the product before purchase commitment.
- Exam traps
  - Escrow protects continuity, so updates and fixes must be covered too.
  - Source code access alone does not replace an escrow agreement.

### Current-user discussions

- Reliability: are deliverables, fixes and enhancements dependable.
- Commitment to service: responsiveness and on-time delivery.
- Commitment to training, support and documentation.

### Contract items

- Specific deliverables and costs.
- Commitment dates for deliverables.
- Documentation, fixes, upgrades, release notification and training.
- Data migration commitments.
- Escrow allowance if source code is not delivered.
- Installation and customization support.
- User acceptance criteria and reasonable acceptance testing period.
- Allowance for changes by the purchaser.
- Maintenance agreement.
- Copying software for business continuity and test.
- Payment schedule tied to actual delivery dates.
- Confidentiality clauses.
- Data protection and compliance clauses.
- Right to ongoing audit, if applicable.
- Legal counsel reviews the contract before signing.
- Manage the contract with status reports, agreed milestones and metrics.

### IS auditor's role in software acquisition

- Involve auditor early to confirm adequate security controls before agreement.
- Missing package security makes data integrity hard to ensure.
- Package risks: inadequate audit trails, password controls and application security.
- Analyze feasibility documentation to judge if acquiring was appropriate.
- Consider common criteria evaluations.
- Review the RFP covers the listed items.
- Confirm vendor security responses are included in the RFP.
- Check the selected vendor is supported by RFP documentation.
- Attend agenda-based presentations and conference-room pilots.
- Ensure legal counsel reviewed the contract before signing.
- Obtain evidence of legal and regulatory compliance, for example an independent auditor report.
- Confirm acquisition followed internal procurement policies.

## Takeaway

- Requirements phase is where auditors advise on security and cost-effective controls.
- Baseline at design freeze; formal change control stops scope creep.
- Match model to need: waterfall stable, iterative large, agile changing requirements.
- Prototyping, agile and CASE speed delivery but can weaken documentation and controls.
- Acquisition needs RFP, objective scoring, escrow, right-to-audit and legal review.
- In BPR, find key controls and ensure management accepts removal risks.
