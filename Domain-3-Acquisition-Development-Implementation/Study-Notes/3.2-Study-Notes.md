## 3.2.0 Basics: Business Case

### Business case
- Definition: a document giving the reasons and benefits for doing a project.
- Use: lets management decide whether a project should proceed.
- Audit impact: auditors judge whether the justification is sound and still valid.
- It answers one question: why undertake or continue this project?
- It must be detailed enough to justify starting and continuing.
- It may be the first project step or a precursor to the project.
- Timing depends on the enterprise and the size of the investment.
- Example: a bank justifies a new loan system by savings and faster approvals.

### Business case across the life cycle
- The business case is a key input to decisions throughout the project.
- It is not a one-time document written only at the start.
- If it becomes invalid, the sponsor or IT steering committee reconsiders the project.
- Definition: stage gates (kill points) are decision points with formal business case review.
- Use: confirm the case is still valid before more money is spent.
- Audit impact: no gate reviews suggests weak project governance.
- If the business case changes, the project is reapproved.
- Reapproval runs through the departmental planning and approval process.
- Example: costs double mid-project, so the sponsor re-submits for approval.
- Exam traps:
  - A changed business case needs reapproval, not simply continued execution.
  - Gate reviews belong to governance, not only to the project manager.

## 3.2.0.1 Feasibility Study

### Purpose and link to the business case
- Definition: an early study checking whether a solution is practical.
- Use: tests the solution against requirements, budget and schedule.
- Audit impact: auditors review it to confirm decisions were unbiased and reasoned.
- It starts after initial approval to move forward.
- It defines the need and identifies alternatives.
- The initial business case normally derives from the feasibility study.
- It is done during project initiation and planning.

### Six elements of a feasibility study
- Elements run in order: scope, current analysis, requirements, approach, evaluation, review.
- Each later element builds on the earlier completed ones.

#### 1. Project scope
- Definition: statement of the business problem or opportunity addressed.
- Use: keeps the study focused on one defined need.
- It must be clear, concise and to the point.
- Audit impact: vague scope leads to unreliable cost and benefit estimates.

#### 2. Current analysis
- Definition: understanding the existing system or software product.
- Use: shows strengths and weaknesses of what the business has now.
- Outcome one: the current system works correctly.
- Outcome two: only minor modifications are needed.
- Outcome three: a complete upgrade or replacement is required.
- Audit impact: confirms a new build is really necessary.
- Exam traps:
  - Replacement is not assumed; the current system may already suffice.

#### 3. Requirements
- Definition: project needs based on stakeholder needs and constraints.
- Use: forms the basis for choosing and testing a solution.
- Requirements for software differ from requirements for whole systems.
- Examples: business, contractual and regulatory processes.
- Examples: end-user functional needs.
- Examples: technical and physical attributes, meaning operational and engineering parameters.
- Audit impact: missing user groups produce requirements that miss real needs.

#### 4. Approach
- Definition: the planned course of action to satisfy the requirements.
- Use: records alternatives considered and why one was preferred.
- It considers existing structures and commercial alternatives.
- Definition: build-versus-buy is choosing to develop in-house or purchase.
- Audit impact: auditors check alternatives were evaluated reasonably, not just one option.
- Example: comparing a custom payroll build against a packaged payroll product.

#### 5. Evaluation
- Definition: examining the cost-effectiveness of the selected approach.
- Use: gives decision-makers the financial case.
- The final report covers the cost of the preferred solution and alternates.
- Cost components: employee hours required to complete.
- Cost components: material and facility costs.
- Cost components: vendor and third-party contractor costs.
- The report also states project schedule start and end dates.
- The report includes a cost and evaluation summary.
- Summary tools: cost-benefit analysis and ROI.
- Definition: ROI is the return gained relative to the investment made.
- Audit impact: benefits and costs must be verifiable and realistic.
- ⚠ Check: manual gives no ROI formula or figures, so none stated.

#### 6. Review
- Definition: formal review of all earlier elements.
- Use: validates completeness and accuracy, then decides.
- Possible decisions: approve, reject, or request corrections first.
- The review involves all key stakeholders.
- If approved, all key stakeholders sign the document.
- If rejected, the rationale is explained and attached.
- The rejection rationale joins a lessons learned list for future studies.
- Audit impact: signatures evidence stakeholder approval and accountability.
- Exam traps:
  - Rejection is documented as lessons learned, not discarded.
  - Approval needs key stakeholders' sign-off, not only the IT team.

## 3.2.1 IS Auditor's Role in Business Case Development

### General responsibilities
- Auditors must understand how the business defines business cases.
- They must understand how ROI determinations are made for development projects.
- Consistently missing ROI objectives suggests weak development and project management practices.
- Auditors review and evaluate the feasibility study.
- Goal: confirm the process and decisions were effective and unbiased.
- Exam traps:
  - The auditor reviews and evaluates; the auditor does not make the project decision.
  - Independence matters: the auditor assesses bias, and must not be biased.

### Reviewing the feasibility study
- Review and evaluate how critical business and IS process requirements are.
- Determine whether existing systems could achieve the solution.
- If not, review alternative solutions for reasonableness.
- Judge the chosen solution's reasonableness using its strengths and weaknesses.
- Confirm cost justifications and benefits are verifiable.
- Verified figures must reflect anticipated costs and benefits actually realized.
- Review the documentation produced for reasonableness.
- Example: auditor finds claimed savings rely on unsupported vendor statements.
- Exam traps:
  - Check "can current systems solve it" before judging a new solution.
  - Unverifiable benefits undermine the business case, however attractive they look.

### Reviewing the requirements definition
- Obtain the detailed requirements definition document.
- Verify accuracy and completeness through interviews with relevant user departments.
- Identify key project team members.
- Verify all affected user groups had appropriate representation.
- Verify project initiation and cost received proper management approval.
- Review conceptual design specifications, such as transforms and data descriptions.
- Purpose: ensure the design addresses user needs.
- Review the conceptual design to ensure control specifications exist.
- Determine whether a reasonable number of vendors received a proposal.
- The proposal must cover project scope and user requirements.
- Review the user acceptance testing (UAT) specifications.
- Definition: UAT is testing by users to confirm the system meets their needs.
- Assess whether the application suits an embedded audit routine.
- Definition: an embedded audit routine is audit code built into the application.
- Use: gives continuous monitoring of transactions.
- If suitable, request it be included in the conceptual design.
- Audit impact: adding it late is costly, so request it early.
- Exam traps:
  - Verify requirements through user interviews, not only by reading the document.
  - Controls are specified at conceptual design, not after build.
  - Embedded audit routine requests belong in design, not at implementation.

## Takeaway
- Business case answers why; it is revisited at every stage gate.
- Changed business case means reapproval through departmental planning.
- Feasibility study has six elements ending in formal stakeholder review.
- Auditors verify costs, benefits, alternatives and user representation.
- Request embedded audit routines and control specifications during conceptual design.
