# 5.10 Security Awareness Training and Programs: Study Notes

[← Domain 5](../README.md)

Scope: 5.10-Security-Awareness-Training-and-Programs.md (Part B: Security Event Management)

## 5.10.0 Why people matter
- Definition: Security awareness programs help people follow security controls correctly.
- Use: Reduce attacks caused by human error, negligence, or ignorance.
- Audit impact: People are treated as the weakest link in information security.
- Audit impact: Staff must know why controls exist and how to support them.
- Audit impact: Staff must also know the consequences of violating controls.
- Audit impact: Effective programs lead to fewer security attacks.
- Audit impact: The IS auditor must tell awareness, training, and education apart.
- Audit impact: The auditor may be asked which program fits the circumstances.

## 5.10.1 Information security learning continuum
- Learning starts with awareness, builds to training, and evolves into education.
- Example: New hire watches a phishing video, then attends a hands-on course.

### Security awareness
- Definition: Passive learning that changes everyday behavior of all staff.
- Use: Delivers the "what" of security, such as policy characteristics.
- Use: Aim is knowledge retention so employees can recall concepts.
- Use: Starts at hire and continues regularly, with varied delivery.
- Use: Typical methods are self-paced e-learning, web-based training, and videos.
- Use: Time frame is short term.
- Use: Testing uses short quizzes, true/false, and multiple choice.
- Audit impact: Varied delivery stops the program from becoming stale or boring.
- Audit impact: Weak retention suggests awareness content or frequency is inadequate.

### Security training
- Definition: More formal teaching of job skills for specific functional areas.
- Use: Delivers the "how" of operating a security component.
- Use: Aim is the practical ability to complete a security task.
- Use: Typical methods are instructor-led classes, demos, labs, and hands-on work.
- Use: Time frame is intermediate term.
- Use: Testing uses application-level problem solving and practical tests.
- Example: Course for security administrators covering all deployed security controls.
- Audit impact: Role-specific training should match the controls each role operates.

### Security education
- Definition: Intensive learning for security professionals or those seeking such careers.
- Use: Delivers the "why" behind a security tool or component.
- Use: Aim is broader understanding with critical analysis.
- Use: Typical methods are seminars, research, and lectures.
- Use: Obtained through colleges, universities, and specialized programs.
- Use: Time frame is long term.
- Use: Testing uses essays, architecture exercises, and examinations.
- Use: Integrates many skills into a multidisciplinary body of knowledge.

### Exam traps
- Match keyword to level: what is awareness, how is training, why is education.
- Awareness is passive and broad; training is skill-focused; education targets professionals.
- Do not choose education for general staff; it suits security professionals.
- Quizzes test awareness; hands-on problem solving tests training.

## 5.10.2 Benefits of a program
- Supports individual accountability.
  - Definition: Staff answer for actions only if they know expectations.
  - Use: Makes enforcement of rules by management easier.
  - Audit impact: Most threats stem from employee negligence or ignorance.
- Serves as a preventive control.
  - Use: Aware staff keep best security practices in daily work.
- Serves as a detective control.
  - Use: Staff learn to spot and report possible violations.
  - Audit impact: Faster reporting lets the organization address incidents quickly.
- Lowers security risk.
  - Use: Technology alone cannot address risk; people operate technical solutions.
  - Audit impact: A trained workforce significantly lowers organizational risk.
- Foundation for other security controls.
  - Use: Best practice is awareness training before deploying any security solution.
  - Audit impact: Controls need people to operate them and interpret automated reports.

### Exam traps
- Awareness is both preventive and detective; do not label it only corrective.
- Awareness complements technical controls; it never replaces them.

## 5.10.3 Approach
- Train all employees and relevant third-party users, with regular updates.
- Larger organizations may add special management-level training.
- Content covers requirements, legal responsibilities, business controls, and correct facility use.
- New employees must be trained before access is granted.
- Training should be part of new employee orientation.
- Audit impact: Access granted before training is a control weakness.

### Planning questions
- Audience: senior management, business managers, IT staff, or end users.
- Message: policies, procedures, or recent events.
- Intended result: better compliance, behavior change, or improved practices.
- Method: computer-based training, all-hands meeting, intranet, or newsletters.
- Culture: consider organizational structure and culture.

### Mechanisms for raising awareness
- Computer-based awareness and training programs.
- Email reminders and security tips.
- Written security policies, procedures, and their updates.
- Nondisclosure statements signed by employees.
- Varied media: newsletters, web pages, videos, posters, login reminders.
- Visible enforcement of security rules.
- Simulated security incidents, such as phishing tests, improve readiness.
- Rewarding employees who report suspicious events.
- Periodic reviews, job descriptions, and performance reviews reinforce expectations.

### Three development steps
- Step 1: design the program, including the program plan.
- Step 2: develop the awareness and training material.
- Step 3: implement the program.

### Exam traps
- Method choice follows audience, message, and intended result.
- Rewarding reporters encourages reporting; punishing mistakes discourages it.

## 5.10.4 Conditions for a successful program
- Build a security culture with a shared, vigilant mindset.
- Write a policy that includes awareness, training, and education.
- Tell everyone their security roles and responsibilities.
- Set up processes for monitoring and reviewing the program.
- Focus on the entire organization, not only IT.
- Management sets the tone at the top for proper behavior.
- Explain rules of behavior in simple terms.
- Establish the basis for penalties and sanctions for noncompliance.
- Center content on the security policy and issue-specific policies.
- Plan the rollout only after a needs assessment.
- Audit impact: Missing sanction basis weakens enforcement of the program.
- Audit impact: Absent management support signals likely program failure.

### Exam traps
- Needs assessment comes before rollout planning, not after.
- Tone at the top beats posters when asked for the key success factor.

## 5.10.5 Conducting a needs assessment
- Definition: Process that determines an organization's awareness and training needs.
- Use: Results justify the program to executive management for funding.
- Use: Consider everyone's needs and involve all key personnel.
- Audit impact: Funding and priority depend on convincing management with assessment results.

### Roles analyzed
- Executive management
  - Need: understand security, its importance, and legal frameworks.
  - Audit impact: Informed leaders provide direction, resources, and commitment.
- Information security personnel
  - Includes architects, security engineers, program managers, and security officers.
  - Need: technical, policy, and best-practice education.
- System owners
  - Need: high-level grasp of policy and controls for their systems.
  - Technical issues beyond them go to security personnel.
- System administrators and IS support
  - Hold high authority, such as granting or denying access.
  - Need: near-security-professional technical knowledge and understanding of impact.
- Operational staff and system users
  - Focus on productivity and profitability, with little security focus.
  - Need: strong awareness and acceptable rules of behavior.
  - Audit impact: Riskiest group, so assessing it should be prioritized.

### Information sources
- Interviews with key personnel or groups.
- Organizational security surveys.
- Review of existing awareness, training, and education material.
- Metrics, for example percentage of users trained in social engineering.
- Security plans, to find appointed individuals and their roles.
- System access databases, to learn individual access levels.
- Findings and recommendations from oversight bodies such as internal audit.
- Discussions with management and system owners relying on IS.
- Analysis of security events, incidents, and attacks.
- Industry trends from academia, government publications, and training organizations.
- Collection methods should be as simple as possible while staying reliable.

### Exam traps
- Highest-risk audience is regular users, not executives or administrators.
- Incident analysis shows which specific personnel need targeted training.
- Not everyone needs the same training; tailor to role.

## 5.10.6 Implementing an awareness and training program
- Planning, implementation, maintenance, and evaluation follow the needs assessment.

### Implementation steps
- Define scope: goals, objectives, strategies, and groups covered.
  - Small organizations may use one program for everyone.
  - Large ones may use a master program plus specific programs.
- Select training staff, internal, external, or both.
  - Audit impact: Trainers need sufficient knowledge of the content.
- Identify target audience using the needs assessment.
  - Use: Channels resources to areas of greatest need.
- Motivate management and trainees by showing organizational benefit.
  - Audit impact: Management commitment ensures adequate resources and delivery frequency.
- Administer the program with visibility, timely materials, and relevant topics.
- Maintain the program because security knowledge keeps changing.
- Evaluate the program to find and fix problems.

### Evaluating effectiveness
- Return on investment is often hard to quantify.
- Alternative measures: information retained, policy compliance, and attitudes and behavior.
- Student questionnaires.
- Observation of employees following procedures.
- Testing employees on covered material.
- Monitoring incidents before and after training.
- Focus groups discussing effectiveness and improvements.
- Interviews with selected trainees.
- Audit impact: A drop in incidents after training supports effectiveness.
- ⚠ Check: Outline says "Maintain" and "Evaluate" separately; manual also lists them separately.

### Exam traps
- 📓 ⭐ Regular awareness training is the best defense against phone-based social engineering.
- Technical tools like IDS do not stop persuaded people from disclosing credentials.
- Email monitoring and physical security do not address voice-based manipulation.
- Social engineering countermeasure is human-focused: train people on verification and reporting.
- ➕ Verify callers through an independent channel before sharing credentials.

## Takeaway
- Awareness is "what", training is "how", education is "why".
- Train new hires before access; reinforce regularly with varied methods.
- Run a needs assessment first; regular users are the riskiest group.
- Management support and tone at the top drive program success.
- Measure effectiveness through retention, compliance, behavior, and incident trends.
