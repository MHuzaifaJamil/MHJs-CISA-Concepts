## 5.7 PKI basics

### Why PKI exists
- Definition: Asymmetric cryptography uses a public key and a private key pair.
- Problem: anyone can claim a public key belongs to them.
- Definition: PKI is the system that proves a public key belongs to its owner.
- Definition: PKI combines policies, procedures, hardware, software and people.
- Use: PKI creates, manages, stores, distributes and revokes public key certificates.
- Audit impact: PKI trust depends on people and process, not only on technology.
- Example: A browser trusts a bank website because a trusted CA signed its certificate.

## 5.7.1 Digital certificates

### Digital certificate
- Definition: An electronic document linking a public key to a named owner.
- Use: Lets others trust that a public key really belongs to that owner.
- Audit impact: A certificate is only as trustworthy as the CA that signed it.

### Certificate authority (CA)
- Definition: The trusted body that issues certificates and signs them digitally.
- Use: The signature vouches that the public key belongs to the stated owner.
- Use: Required proof of identity varies with the certificate type.
- Use: CAs also publish the list of compromised or revoked certificates.
- Audit impact: A compromised CA breaks trust for every certificate it issued.
- Example: A company buys a web server certificate from a commercial CA.

### Certificate policy (CP)
- Definition: A document naming PKI actors and their roles, duties and responsibilities.
- Use: Sets how certificates are used, how keys are generated, how names are chosen.
- Audit impact: Auditors check the CP is approved, current and followed.

### Certificate practice statement (CPS)
- Definition: A statement describing how a specific CA actually issues certificates.
- Use: States the certificate types the CA issues.
- Use: States policies for issuing, renewing and recovering certificates.
- Use: States the cryptographic algorithms and key length used.
- Use: States the certificate lifetime.
- Use: States revocation policies and CRL policies.
- Audit impact: The CPS is the benchmark for testing CA and RA behavior.
- Exam traps: CP says what rules apply; CPS says how the CA meets them.
- Exam traps: Do not swap the CP and CPS roles in answer choices.

### Registration authority (RA)
- Definition: A body the CA delegates administrative registration tasks to.
- Use: Establishes or verifies the identity of the certificate requester.
- Example: National branches of a global firm act as RAs for local staff.
- RA functions: verify information the subject supplied (personal authentication).
- RA functions: verify the subject's right to the requested certificate attributes.
- RA functions: verify proof of possession of the matching private key.
- RA functions: report key compromise or termination cases needing revocation.
- RA functions: assign names for identification.
- RA functions: generate shared secrets for initialization and certificate pick-up.
- RA functions: initiate registration with the CA on the subject's behalf.
- RA functions: initiate the key recovery process.
- RA functions: distribute physical tokens such as smart cards holding private keys.
- Audit impact: RAs verify identity but do not sign certificates.
- Exam traps: Proof of possession means the requester truly holds the private key.
- Exam traps: Signing certificates is the CA's job, never the RA's.

## 5.7.2 Key management

### Key management in general
- Definition: Handling cryptographic keys across their whole life cycle.
- Use: Keeps keys secret, available and usable only by authorized parties.
- Audit impact: Key management is hard with symmetric keys, simpler with asymmetric.
- Audit impact: Weak key handling defeats even very strong algorithms.

### Key creation (generation)
- Definition: Producing new keys with a device or program called a key generator.
- Audit impact: Generation hardware should meet FIPS standards.

### Key distribution
- Definition: Securely transferring a key to a user or system.
- Use: Secure encryption protects keys while in transit.
- Audit impact: Insecure distribution exposes keys before use even starts.

### Key storage and custody
- Definition: Holding keys securely on the device, often in a protected store.
- Example: The Windows certificate store protects keys on a workstation.
- Definition: Dual custody needs two or more people to access a key.
- Definition: Split knowledge divides key parts so no one person knows all.
- Use: Both controls prevent a single person misusing a key.
- Definition: Key escrow means a trusted party holds a copy for later recovery.
- Audit impact: Auditors must know which keys are placed under escrow.
- Exam traps: Escrow aids recovery; it also adds a point of compromise.

### Key rotation
- Definition: Retiring old keys and putting new keys into use.
- Use: Keys should not live forever; age raises theft, loss and failure risk.
- Audit impact: Look for evidence that rotation actually happens on schedule.

### Key recovery and backup
- Definition: Restoring a lost or failed key from escrow or backup.
- Use: Losing a private key often means losing the data it protects.
- Use: PKI products usually offer built-in backup and recovery.
- Audit impact: Confirm escrow, backup and archive procedures exist and work.

### Key destruction
- Definition: Permanently removing keys at end of life or after compromise.
- States: suspended means temporary hold.
- States: revoked means no reinstatement is possible.
- States: expired means inactive until renewed.
- States: destroyed means permanently gone.
- Audit impact: Destruction must be appropriate and leave no recoverable key data.
- ⚠ Check: Manual lists destroyed among key states; revoked is not the same.

## 5.7.3 Certificate revocation

### Revocation process
- Definition: Ending a certificate's use before its validity period ends.
- Use: Decide reasons, map them to revocation policy, then revoke.
- Audit impact: Revoked certificates are invalid; parties must stop relying on them.
- Audit impact: Auditors advise management on risk of using certificates that should be revoked.

### Revocation reasons
- Change in affiliation: person leaves, dies, or changes roles.
- Change in affiliation: also covers a computer account no longer in use.
- Compromise of private key: key lost, stolen or exposed.
- Example: A stolen laptop exposes every private key stored on it.
- Cessation of operation: a server or workstation is decommissioned.
- Superseded: a replacement certificate is issued for any reason.
- Unspecified: no reason given; not recommended because it removes the audit trail.
- CA compromise: CA tampered with or its key stolen, producing illegitimate certificates.
- Audit impact: Revoking a CA invalidates every certificate beneath it.
- ⚠ Check: Manual ties cessation to CA decommissioning; treat that as the CA-level case.
- Exam traps: Revoking a CA is far wider than revoking one certificate.

## 5.7.4 Certificate Revocation List (CRL)

### CRL
- Definition: A list of certificates revoked by the CA before scheduled expiry.
- Use: Browsers and applications check it to decide whether to trust a certificate.
- Use: CAs publish CRLs on a regular periodic schedule.
- Definition: The CRL distribution point (CDP) is where the CA publishes CRLs.
- Definition: A CDP is typically an LDAP directory or web server.
- Audit impact: Stale or unreachable CRLs let revoked certificates keep being trusted.

### Revocation states
- Revoked: irreversible; used for CA error, key compromise or policy breach.
- Hold: reversible; used when loss or theft of a key is uncertain.
- Example: A user unsure about a lost key puts the certificate on hold.
- Example: If the key is found unused, the certificate is reinstated.
- Exam traps: Only hold can be reversed; revoked can never return to valid.

### CRL contents
- Issuer name (common name).
- Revocation date and time.
- Reasons for revocation.
- Specific revocation time period.
- Certificate extensions.
- Signature algorithm.
- Certificate serial number.
- Date the next CRL will be issued.
- ⚠ Check: Outline omits the manual's "revocation time period" item; verify before relying.

### Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP)
- Definition: A protocol that requests one certificate's revocation status from the CA.
- Use: Returns an instant answer: valid, revoked or unknown.
- Use: Gives fresher data than periodically downloaded CRLs.
- Audit impact: OCSP responders must be available, automated and trustworthy.

### OCSP stapling
- Definition: The web server caches the CA's OCSP response and attaches it.
- Use: Browsers no longer query the CA directly.
- Use: Removes the delay of contacting the issuing CA.
- Use: Shrinks attack surface and improves user privacy.
- Example: The CA sees requests per website, not per visiting user.
- Exam traps: Stapling is an OCSP enhancement, not a replacement for CRLs.

## 5.7.5 PKI infrastructure risk

### Policy and governance risks
- No clear renewal and revocation policy is the biggest concern 📓⭐.
- Why: Policy gaps let every other control fail silently.
- Lack of policies: inconsistent rules risk noncompliance, fines and penalties.
- Unclear certificate ownership: nobody manages or approves the certificate life cycle.
- Insufficient skills and resources: PKI needs skilled staff, which are scarce.

### Cryptographic and key risks
- Outdated protocols leave the organization open to incidents and breaches.
- Weak keys: lengths under 2,048 bits are considered insecure.
- Infrequent key rotation gives attackers longer time to exploit keys.
- Poor private key management risks attackers decrypting sensitive information.
- ⚠ Check: Manual repeats the key-length sentence under rotation; treat as editing slip.
- ⚠ Check: The 2,048-bit threshold is manual-stated; confirm against current standards ➕.

### Operational risks
- Mismanaged certificates: failure to issue, renew or revoke properly.
- Example: An expired certificate causes outages and opens lateral movement paths.
- Lack of automation: manual tracking of owners and expiry dates causes errors.
- Limited visibility: no central inventory lets rogue certificates hide.
- Poor patch management delays detecting and fixing PKI vulnerabilities.

### Root CA risk
- Definition: The root CA signature anchors the whole chain of trust.
- Risk: A compromised root breaks trust for the entire PKI.
- Control: Store the root CA offline in a well-protected vault.
- Exam traps: Online, always-on root CAs are the wrong design.

### Exam traps for risk ranking
- Choose the missing governance policy over a single outdated certificate.
- Multiple CAs are not inherently a defect; unmanaged ones may be.
- A missing backup plan matters, but policy gaps rank higher in the manual's logic.
- Ask which gap causes uncontrolled, enterprise-wide failure first.

## 5.7.6 Audit procedures for PKI

### CA audit
- Check an information security policy exists.
- Evaluate the CA's physical controls.
- Verify background checks on CA personnel.
- Confirm audit logs are backed up.
- Inspect key management across the whole life cycle.
- Verify CA systems are appropriately isolated.

### RA audit
- Verify RA enrollment follows the CPS.
- Confirm the RA role is limited to submitting details to the CA.
- Check onboarding and termination procedures are defined.
- Confirm CA-RA communication channels are secure.
- Verify all RA activity is logged.
- Verify RA access to CA systems uses multifactor authentication.
- Inspect prior audit reports for noncompliance.
- Review RA violations and the corrective actions taken.
- Exam traps: An RA given extra roles is a segregation-of-duties finding.

### Certificate audit
- Verify certificate information is published on the CA website.
- Check renewal follows the CPS.
- Check suspension and revocation follow the CPS.
- Confirm a secure certificate distribution system exists.

### Key management audit
- Check key-generation hardware is FIPS compliant.
- Verify keys are distributed by secure mechanisms.
- Assess procedures for handling key compromise.
- Verify key storage is secure and appropriate.
- Verify the CA maintains key escrow.
- Check key backup and archiving procedures exist and operate.
- Confirm key destruction leaves no data behind.

### CP and CPS audit
- Verify an approved CP is in place.
- Check the CP is current.
- Check all PKI parties follow the CP; document gaps.
- Check the CPS complies with applicable laws and regulations.
- Verify the CPS is available to relevant parties.
- Confirm responsibility for maintaining the CPS is assigned.
- Verify CPS changes follow defined processes.
- Confirm the CPS has a valid object identifier.

### CRL and OCSP audit
- Verify a CRL or OCSP service is in place.
- Confirm the CA digitally signs each CRL.
- Verify secure CRL distribution.
- Sample entries: revoked certificates stay listed until validity period ends.
- Confirm OCSP response processes are automated.
- Check CRL and OCSP services meet industry standards and regulations.
- Exam traps: Removing a revoked entry early could let it be trusted again.
- ⚠ Check: Manual says OCSP replaces annual CRL review; wording is unclear.

## Takeaway
- PKI binds public keys to owners through CA-signed certificates.
- CP states the rules; CPS states how the CA follows them.
- Key life cycle: create, distribute, store, rotate, recover, destroy.
- Revoked is permanent; hold is reversible.
- OCSP gives real-time status; stapling adds speed and privacy.
- Missing renewal and revocation policy is the top PKI risk.
