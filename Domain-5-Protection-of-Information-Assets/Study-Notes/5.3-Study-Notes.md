## 5.3.0 Basics: Identification and Authentication (I&A)

- Identification
  - Definition: claiming who you are, usually with a user ID.
  - Use: links every action to a named subject.
  - Audit impact: unidentified users make accountability impossible.
- Authentication
  - Definition: proving the claimed identity is genuine.
  - Use: password, token, certificate, or biometric check.
  - Audit impact: weak proof defeats every later control.
- I&A is the first line of defense and enables accountability.
- Identity stays constant; authentication tokens must be replaced regularly.
- Example: an employee ID never changes, but the password rotates.
- Common I&A vulnerabilities
  - Weak methods: short length, low complexity, rare changes.
  - Simple or easily guessed passwords.
  - Users bypassing the authentication mechanism.
  - Stored authentication data lacks confidentiality and integrity.
  - Authentication data sent unencrypted in transit.
  - Users unaware of risks from sharing passwords and tokens.
- Exam traps
  - Weak passwords are fixed by policy and education, not by more logging.
  - Encrypting storage alone is incomplete; transit must be encrypted too.

## 5.3.1 IAM

- IAM framework
  - Definition: policies, procedures, processes, and technologies managing identity and access information.
  - Use: gives the right people the right access at the right time.
  - Audit impact: gaps here cause unauthorized access and failed compliance.
- IAM service components (Figure 5.5)
  - Authentication services: SSO, FIM, MFA, biometric, adaptive, session management.
  - Authorization services: rule-based, RBAC, attribute-based, PAM, remote authorization.
  - User management: provisioning, deprovisioning, self-service, credentials, delegated administration.
  - Central user repository: directory services, IdPs, data synchronization, identity stores.
  - Repository also includes directory federation and virtual directory.
- Benefits
  - Improves regulatory compliance; user activity can be tracked as audit proof.
  - Enhances data security through extra authentication layers (defense in depth).
  - Limits lateral movement of attackers.
  - Promotes digital transformation by centralizing secure access.
  - Improves productivity through SSO.
  - Streamlines access control, notably in cloud environments.
- IAM life cycle management
  - Definition: creating and maintaining a digital identity for any human or non-human entity.
  - Use: covers new users, account updates, and removal.
  - Audit impact: each stage has a distinct risk to test.
- Enrollment
  - Definition: creating or registering the account.
  - Methods: automatic, self-enrollment, or manual.
- Role determination
  - Definition: deciding the user's role, which then drives access needs.
  - Exam trap: a role does not itself contain permissions.
  - Exam trap: do not confuse role determination with provisioning.
- Provisioning
  - Definition: granting specific access levels, often via RBAC policies.
  - Example: one user may update accounts; another is viewer only.
- Review/updating
  - Definition: adjusting access as roles change, such as promotions.
  - Audit impact: watch for privilege creep here.
- Deprovisioning
  - Definition: removing access when someone leaves.
  - Options: delete, disable, or retain then disable later.
  - Audit impact: lingering former-user access is the risk addressed.
- IAM best practices (Figure 5.7)
  - Approval
    - Authorized approver required for access, transfer, and creation requests.
    - Reevaluate access when staff transfer or change roles.
    - Privileged and group shared access need two-level approval.
    - Two levels: reporting manager, then manager's manager or system owner.
  - Shared accounts
    - Apply least privilege (POLP) to shared accounts.
    - Limit the number of servers holding the shared account.
    - Account owners publish the list of users sharing it.
    - Validate that shared-account activity is logged.
    - Change shared passwords on departure, role change, or suspected compromise.
  - Termination and communication
    - Revoke user IDs as soon as termination is requested.
    - Send passwords by encrypted email or sealed envelope.
  - Password parameters
    - Minimum length depends on the threat model (NIST SP 800-63B).
    - Use mixed case, numbers, and punctuation.
    - Avoid dictionary words, user ID derivatives, and personal details.
    - Keep an encrypted password history of at least the last 13.
    - ⚠ Check: manual says 13 here, 12 in 5.3.14; treat as examples.
    - Change every 30 days for privileged, 90 days for regular IDs.
    - Force password change at first login.
    - Suspend the account after five consecutive failed attempts.
    - Screensaver time-out after 15 minutes of inactivity.
    - Encrypt passwords in storage and transit.
    - Each user ID unique; show last login date and time.
  - Policy administration
    - Review policy yearly.
    - Document must state owner, approver, scope, roles, KPIs, workflows.
  - Validation
    - Revalidate regular accounts every six months, privileged every three months.
    - Privileged accounts revalidated quarterly.
    - Terminate or lock unvalidated accounts within one working day, five at most.
    - No reinstatement without valid authorization.
    - Managers, resource owners, or data owners review the validations.
  - SoD and logging
    - Every IAM request checked against SoD policy; failures blocked at request.
    - Use automated tools to enforce SoD.
    - A log management process must exist.
  - Account hygiene
    - Investigate and remove dormant and orphan accounts.
    - Identify an owner or custodian for every account.
- Exam traps
  - Shared privileged account with multiple admins is the most serious IAM finding.
  - Minor gaps (history depth, review cadence) rank below lost accountability.
  - Quoted numbers are manual good-practice values, not universal laws.

## 5.3.2 Authentication, Authorization and Accountability (AAA)

- AAA chain
  - Order: identification, authentication, authorization, accountability.
  - Protocols commonly used: RADIUS, Kerberos, SAML, OAuth.
- Subject and object
  - Subject: active entity, such as a user or running process.
  - Object: passive entity holding information or resources.
  - Example: a user (subject) opens a payroll file (object).

### Authentication methods

- Authentication
  - Definition: proving the subject is who it claims, against a directory.
  - Use: gate before any access is allowed.
  - Audit impact: weak methods undermine all downstream controls.
- Password authentication
  - Definition: something you know.
  - Audit impact: weakest factor alone; check policy strength.
- Multifactor authentication (MFA)
  - Definition: combining knowledge, possession, and biometric factors.
  - Use: stops attacks that steal only one factor.
  - Audit impact: two passwords are not MFA; factors must differ.
- Adaptive (risk-based) authentication
  - Definition: demands extra factors when context looks risky.
  - Example: login from an untrusted device triggers an extra challenge.
- Token-based authentication
  - Definition: something you have; a token proves identity.
  - Use: common in RESTful APIs.
  - Audit impact: token theft grants access, so expiry and storage matter.
- Certificate-based authentication
  - Definition: digital certificates prove identity.
  - Audit impact: verify certificate issuance and revocation processes.
- Biometric authentication
  - Definition: unique physical or behavioral traits; see 5.3.16.
- LDAP authentication
  - Definition: bind request carrying an identifier and password.
  - Audit impact: unencrypted binds expose credentials.
- Smart card authentication
  - Definition: card with embedded microprocessor holding credentials.
  - Audit impact: lost cards need rapid revocation.
- Single sign-on (SSO)
  - Definition: one credential set grants access to many applications.
  - Use: portal issues a certificate or token; SAML shares keys among providers.
  - Primary domain handles credentials first; secondary domains reuse them.
  - Advantages: stronger password choice, easier administration, fewer resets, faster logins.
  - Limitations: hard to support all operating systems; integration cost.
  - Limitation: single point of failure and total compromise.
  - Mitigation: strong authentication and biometrics.
  - Audit impact: one stolen credential opens everything.
- Exam traps
  - SSO's key risk is concentrated compromise, not productivity loss.
  - Adaptive authentication reacts to risk; MFA applies factors always.

### Authorization

- Authorization
  - Definition: granting service access based on completed authentication.
  - Use: restricts by location, IP address, or time of access.
  - Audit impact: must enforce least privilege, need-to-know, and SoD.
- Coarse-grained authorization: role-level access to an object.
- Fine-grained authorization: conditional access, for example tenure-based.
- File-level access types
  - Read/inquiry/copy.
  - Write/create/update/delete.
  - Execute.
  - Read-only is the least dangerous access type.
- Access control lists (ACLs)
  - Definition: list of users and their permitted access types.
  - Basic ACLs: owner, group, world.
  - Advanced ACLs: user-defined groups and explicit deny.
  - Audit impact: unremoved old rights after role change create SoD conflicts.
- Logical access security administration
  - Decentralized advantages: onsite, timely resolution, frequent monitoring.
  - Decentralized risks: local standards, weaker management, no central audits.
  - Remote site controls: software access controls, lockable terminals, restricted documentation.
  - Centrally monitor remotely processed data; control replicated files.
- Exam traps
  - Authorization follows authentication; granting access before proof is wrong.
  - Stale rights after transfers are the classic ACL finding.

### Accountability

- Accountability
  - Definition: tracking user activity and resource consumption (accounting).
  - Use: logs form the audit trail.
  - Supports nonrepudiation, deterrence, fault isolation, and legal action.
  - Audit impact: no unique IDs means no accountability.
- Auditor checks
  - Management culture and policy; no shared passwords.
  - Default admin accounts removed; service IDs not interactive.
  - Proximity cards and video recordings matched to event logs.
- Shared privileged accounts violate individual accountability and non-repudiation. 📓⭐
- Exam traps
  - Compensating logging does not restore accountability on shared accounts.

## 5.3.3 Zero-Trust Architecture (ZTA)

- ZTA
  - Definition: "never trust, always verify" every access request.
  - Use: replaces trust based on network location.
  - Audit impact: every request must be authenticated, authorized, and validated.
- Basics (NIST SP 800-207)
  - All data and computing services count as resources.
  - Communication secured regardless of network location.
  - Access granted per session, per request.
  - Dynamic policy-based access control (PBAC) decides access.
  - Asset posture is monitored and measured.
  - Authentication and authorization are dynamic.
  - Intensive information gathering improves decisions.
- ZTA best practices
  - Monitor network traffic and connected devices.
  - Update devices regularly.
  - Implement POLP and segment the network.
  - Prefer security keys for MFA over soft tokens.
  - Incorporate threat intelligence.
  - Keep good password practices.
- Implementing IAM using ZTA
  - Manage identities centrally.
  - Secure access with MFA plus adaptive authentication.
  - Control by role, department, or attribute.
  - Secure privileged accounts.
- Example: a user inside the office network still re-authenticates per resource.
- Exam traps
  - Being on the internal network never grants implicit trust in ZTA.
  - A perimeter firewall alone does not make an environment zero trust.

## 5.3.4 Privileged Access Management (PAM)

- PAM
  - Definition: IAM component securing elevated access.
  - Use: controls, vaults, and monitors powerful accounts.
  - Audit impact: privileged misuse causes the largest breaches.
- Privileged accounts
  - Administrative accounts.
  - Emergency ("break glass") accounts.
  - Service accounts and application accounts.
  - SSH keys giving root access.
- Benefits
  - Reduces attack surface.
  - Supports least privilege and prevents privilege creep.
  - Streamlines rights provision.
  - Monitors privileged accounts.
  - Resolves shared and static password risks.
  - Reduces data breaches.
- PAM risks
  - Manual credential management is error-prone.
  - Privileged activity is hard to track.
  - Threat analysis becomes ineffective.
  - Cloud privileged user access adds exposure.
  - Authentication protocol weaknesses, such as Kerberos.
- PAM best practices
  - Implement POLP first.
  - Track all privileged accounts.
  - Use temporary privilege escalation.
  - Implement RBAC and automate PAM processes.
  - Monitor and audit privileged activity.
  - Keep infrastructure accounts in a central digital vault.
  - Limit lateral movement; remove local admin groups.
  - Vault third-party credentials; no hardcoded credentials.
  - Run periodic red team exercises.
- Exam traps
  - Standing permanent admin rights are weaker than temporary elevation.
  - Hardcoded credentials in code are a finding even if encrypted elsewhere.

## 5.3.5 Directory Services

- Directory service
  - Definition: database of users and resources, with names, passwords, preferences.
  - Use: central lookup for authentication and authorization.
  - Audit impact: directory compromise affects the whole enterprise.
- Naming rules: names must be unique and unambiguous.
- DNS maps host names to IP addresses.
- Best practices
  - Implement POLP.
  - Remove open identifiers: Everyone, Authenticated Users, Domain Users.
  - Change default passwords; disable local administrator account.
  - Enforce effective password policies.
  - Back up at least one domain controller.
  - Monitor regularly and run penetration tests.
  - Audit all directory changes.
  - Educate employees on phishing.
  - Use a secure admin workstation (SAW) for privileged tasks only.
- Exam traps
  - Broad groups such as Everyone on sensitive objects are an audit finding.
  - A SAW is dedicated; browsing and email on it defeats purpose.

## 5.3.6 Identity Governance and Administration (IGA)

- IGA
  - Definition: tracking and monitoring user actions on resources.
  - IGA is a subcategory of IAM.
  - Audit impact: gives visibility to prove who had what access.
- Challenges addressed
  - Efficient identity management and increased visibility.
  - Streamlined governance and administration.
  - Improved accountability and security posture.
  - Reduced security risk and promoted compliance.
  - Improved productivity and organizational scalability.
- Elements: identity administration (IA)
  - Connectors to target systems.
  - Automated workflows.
  - Role-based access control (RBAC).
  - Entitlement management.
  - Provisioning life cycle management.
- Elements: identity governance (IG)
  - Separation of duties (SoD).
  - User access review.
  - Analytics and reporting.
- Exam traps
  - Administration creates access; governance reviews and challenges it.

## 5.3.7 Identity as a Service (IDaaS)

- IDaaS
  - Definition: cloud-delivered IAM on a subscription basis.
  - Use: should support SSO, MFA, identity management, provisioning, cloud directory.
  - Audit impact: identity control moves to a third party.
- Benefits: reduced costs, improved security, scalability, flexibility, simpler identity management.
- Risks
  - Dependence on Internet connectivity.
  - Limited customization.
  - Security concerns about the third-party provider.
  - Regulatory compliance challenges.
  - Integration challenges.
- Best practices
  - Gain senior management buy-in.
  - Perform due diligence on the provider.
  - Require strong authentication.
  - Manage logs.
  - Train employees.
  - Maintain DR/BC plans.
- Exam traps
  - Outsourcing the service does not outsource accountability.

## 5.3.8 System Access Permission

- System access permission
  - Definition: prerogative to act on a resource: read, create, modify, delete, execute.
  - Established at both physical and logical levels.
  - Guided by need-to-know, least privilege, accountability, traceability.
- Four logical layers: networks, platforms, databases, applications.
- Information owners give written authorization to security administrators.
- Access rules define user, resource, level, and conditions (time, terminal).
- Review periodically against an authorization matrix.
- Audit impact: reviews prevent authorization creep.
- Integrate access review with HR transfers and terminations.
- Nonemployees are accountable too: contractors, vendors, auditors, visitors, consultants.
- Exam traps
  - Owners authorize; administrators only implement the authorized access.

## 5.3.9 Types of Access Controls

- Mandatory access control (MAC)
  - Definition: system compares sensitivity labels with user clearance.
  - Users and data owners cannot change it.
  - Prohibitive: anything not expressly permitted is forbidden.
- Discretionary access control (DAC)
  - Definition: data owner decides who may access.
  - Cannot override MAC.
- Rule-based access control (RuBAC): predefined rules, usually an ACL.
- Role-based access control (RBAC)
  - Definition: access assigned by job role.
  - Industry-standard good practice.
- Attribute-based access control (ABAC): uses user, object, and environmental attributes.
- Policy-based access control (PBAC): policy decides which privileges a role gets.
- DAC, MAC, RBAC as the core trio ➕
- Example: military labels (MAC) versus a user sharing a folder (DAC).
- Exam traps
  - Owner-controlled sharing means DAC; label-enforced means MAC.
  - DAC never overrides MAC.
  - Role does not hold permissions; policy or assignment does.

## 5.3.10 Information Security and External Parties

- Identifying risk from external parties
  - Assess risk before granting access.
  - Secure the right to audit controls.
  - Define facilities accessed and access type: physical, logical, network.
  - Define onsite or offsite access.
  - Consider value and sensitivity of the information.
  - Identify personnel involved and verify their identity.
  - Plan incident handling and continuation of access.
  - Meet legal and regulatory requirements.
  - Grant access only after controls and contract exist.
  - Use nondisclosure agreements for confidentiality.
- Addressing security with customers
  - Describe the product or service.
  - Require reporting of inaccuracies and incidents.
  - Set target and unacceptable service levels.
  - Reserve the right to monitor and revoke activity.
  - State liabilities, legal responsibilities, and intellectual property.
- Addressing security in third-party agreements
  - Indemnification provisions.
  - Compliance with security policy.
  - Asset protection and malware controls.
  - Return or destruction of information at contract end.
  - Restrictions on copying and disclosure.
  - User and administrator training.
  - Change management process.
  - Access control policy; all non-authorized access forbidden.
  - Incident reporting arrangements.
  - Service levels and performance monitoring.
  - Right to audit and service auditor report.
  - Escalation process and service continuity requirements.
  - Subcontractor involvement.
  - Renegotiation and termination conditions.
  - Non-assignability of the contract.
  - Outsourcing: liability, transition planning, due diligence.
  - Certified compliance, such as ISO 27001 or SOC 2.
  - Imposed agreements must not weaken own security.
- Exam traps
  - Contract and controls come before access, never after.
  - Right-to-audit clause beats relying on vendor self-assertion.

## 5.3.11 Digital Rights Management (DRM)

- DRM
  - Definition: controls how content is used to protect copyright.
  - Use: applied to copyrighted content, software, confidential documents, IP, government documents.
  - Integrates with DLP: DLP detects, DRM denies.
  - Audit impact: protection travels with the file.
- DRM restrictions
  - Copy, edit, share, and print restrictions.
  - Location restriction and expiration.
- DRM technologies
  - Password protection.
  - Digital watermarking (steganography).
  - Device control.
  - Restrictive licensing is legal, not a technical control.
  - Digital trust infrastructure (blockchain).
  - Hashing.
  - Secure communication protocols (TLS).
  - Timebound decryption keys.
- Best practices
  - Build a content inventory.
  - Take a risk-based approach.
  - Use free DRM tools first ➕
  - Gain employee buy-in.
  - Integrate with DLP.
  - Minimize shadow IT.
  - Automate DRM.
- Exam traps
  - Licensing terms alone do not technically stop copying.
  - DLP detects leakage; DRM enforces usage limits.

## 5.3.12 Logical Access

- Logical access
  - Definition: interacting with resources via identification, authentication, authorization.
  - Primary means of protecting information assets.
- Logical access exposures
  - Data leakage: original remains, so loss may go undetected.
  - Computer shutdown via terminals or PCs, needing high-level logon ID.
  - Others: unauthorized software, data modification, data destruction, spying on data flow.
- Familiarization with the IT environment
  - Review network, OS platform, database, and application security layers.
- Paths of logical access
  - Direct path: terminal tied to mainframe.
  - LAN access: network is itself an IS resource.
  - Combined direct, local network, and remote paths are most common.
  - Front-end systems face untrusted networks; back-end systems hold databases.
  - Intermediate devices act as security doors.
- General points of entry
  - Primary domain controllers.
  - Secondary entry points: application servers, databases.
  - Wired or wireless network connectivity with I&A to the domain controller.
  - Network management devices (routers, firewalls) strictly controlled.
  - Remote access: VPN for full network access.
  - Auditor confirms all points of entry are known.
- Exam traps
  - Unknown entry points are a bigger concern than weak known ones.

## 5.3.13 Access Control Software

- Access control software
  - Definition: tools preventing unauthorized access and modification.
  - Use: protects critical functions across networks, platforms, databases, applications.
  - Greatest protection sits at network and platform/OS levels.
- OS and application functions
  - Create or change user profiles.
  - Assign user I&A.
  - Apply logon limitation rules.
  - Grant access commensurate with job responsibilities.
  - Notify of proper use before login.
  - Log user activities for accountability.
  - Establish resource access rules.
  - Log events and report.
- Database and application functions
  - Create or change data files and database profiles.
  - Verify authorization at application, transaction, and field level.
  - Verify subsystem authorization at file level.
  - Log access violations.
- Upper layers rely on lower infrastructure layers.
- Exam traps
  - Strong application controls fail if the OS beneath is weak.

## 5.3.14 Logon IDs and Passwords

- Logon ID and password
  - Definition: ID identifies; password proves, something you know.
  - Logon ID is individual, not group, in standardized format.
- Password features
  - Easy to remember, hard to guess.
  - Initial password random; forced change at first login.
  - Lockout after set failed attempts, permanent or temporary.
  - Reset only by security administrator after verifying identity.
  - Verify via callback or supervisor.
  - Hash passwords with a strong algorithm.
  - Mask on screen and reports; never write down.
  - Change regularly, for example every 30 days, by users themselves.
  - Password history: no reuse of last 12 passwords.
  - Known only to the individual user.
  - Admin password in sealed envelope: firecall ID.
  - Formal password policy with acknowledgment.
- Password attacks
  - Password sniffing
    - Definition: passive interception of unencrypted passwords.
    - Defense: encrypt transmission.
  - Phishing: social engineering to obtain passwords.
  - Brute force: systematic guessing, often using personal details.
  - Dictionary attack: tries common words and phrases.
  - Lockout policy prevents brute-force and dictionary attacks.
  - Keylogger: prevention is the best defense.
  - Rainbow attack: precomputed hash table; salting mitigates.
  - Credential stuffing: reused stolen credentials; user education helps.
  - Password spraying: few passwords across many users; targets SSO and cloud.
- Good practices
  - Keep the logon ID naming rule confidential.
  - Rename or disable default accounts (Guest, Administrator, Admin).
  - Deactivate unused logon IDs.
  - Enforce session timeout after inactivity.
  - Apply stricter rules to privileged accounts.
- Exam traps
  - Salting defeats rainbow tables; lockout does not.
  - Spraying avoids lockout by trying few passwords per account.
  - Hashing is one-way; encryption is reversible, so hash stored passwords.
  - Users change their own passwords, not administrators.

## 5.3.15 Remote Access Security

- Remote users: employees, vendors, consultants, partners, customers.
- Connectivity: TCP/IP, SNA, terminal emulation, modem, ISDN, frame relay, DSL.
- Remote access risks
  - Denial of service (DoS).
  - Malicious third parties.
  - Misconfigured communications software and devices.
  - Unsecured hosts.
  - Physical security weaknesses of remote computers.
- Remote access controls
  - Policy and standards.
  - Proper authorizations.
  - Strong I&A.
  - Encryption (VPN, TLS).
  - Robust system and network management.
  - Continuous monitoring (IDS/IPS, DLP).
- Exam traps
  - Strong I&A plus encryption beats network location as a control.

## 5.3.16 Biometrics

- Biometrics
  - Definition: authentication by unique physical or behavioral trait.
  - Use: strong "something you are" factor.
  - Audit impact: traits cannot be reissued if compromised.
- Enrollment steps
  - Acquire a sample.
  - Extract unique data into a mathematical code.
  - Create an initial template.
  - Compare new samples; develop the final template.
- Feature changes (scars, voice) reduce effectiveness.
- Management of biometrics
  - Protect data integrity, authenticity, and nonrepudiation.
  - Manage life cycle: enrollment, transmission, storage, verification, identification, termination.
  - Use one-to-one (verification) and one-to-many (identification) matching.
  - Encapsulate data; secure transmission and storage.
  - Approve a biometric policy (BIMS).
  - Provide backup authentication if the device fails.
  - Cover biometrics in the BCP.

### Performance metrics

- Failure to enroll rate (FER)
  - Definition: proportion of people who cannot enroll.
  - Causes: physical differences, training gaps, environmental conditions.
- False rejection rate (FRR)
  - Definition: authorized user wrongly rejected; Type 1 error.
  - Impact: frustration and revalidation overhead, not a security breach.
- False acceptance rate (FAR)
  - Definition: impostor wrongly accepted; Type 2 error.
  - Impact: worst error for security.
- Crossover error rate (CER)
  - Definition: point where FRR equals FAR; also called EER.
  - Lower CER means a more accurate system.
- Higher sensitivity raises FRR and lowers FAR.
- Lower sensitivity does the reverse.
- Verification: raising sensitivity rejects more users, so FRR up and FAR down; consistent with the CER intersection.
- ⚠ Check: manual's FRR/FAR formulas read inverted; correct form is errors divided by attempts.
- Example: a vault door tuned for low FAR will reject more valid staff.

### Physical and behavioral types

- Ranking by best response time and lowest CER: palm, hand, iris, retina, fingerprint, voice.
- Palm: analyzes ridges and valleys.
- Hand geometry
  - Oldest technique; template only 10 to 20 bytes.
  - Weakness: low uniqueness; injury changes measurements.
- Iris
  - Over 400 characteristics; about 260 used.
  - No contact needed; costly and storage heavy.
- Fingerprint
  - Low cost, small device, easy integration.
  - Needs contact; dirt and oil degrade images.
  - Template holds minutiae, not the full image.
- Face recognition: lacks uniqueness; lookalikes can fool the device.
- Retina scan: scans blood vessel pattern at the back of the eye.
- Signature dynamics: speed, pen pressure, direction, stroke length.
- Voice recognition: pitch, dynamics, waveform.
- Voice weaknesses: recorded passphrase, noise, voice changes.
- Audit considerations
  - Privacy concerns and personal health.
  - Expertise and robustness.
  - Cost and ROI.
  - Accuracy, especially FAR.
  - Security: iris and vein patterns are hard to replicate.
  - Compliance requirements.
- Exam traps
  - FAR is the security-critical error; FRR is mainly an inconvenience.
  - Lowest CER is best overall accuracy, not lowest FRR alone.
  - Failed device needs backup authentication, not disabled controls.

## 5.3.17 Naming Conventions for Logical Access Controls

- Naming conventions
  - Definition: rules stating which users access which resources, at what level.
  - Support personal accountability and SoD.
  - Set by data owners with the security officer.
- A common high-level qualifier lets generic rules cover many resources.
- Audit impact: fewer rules simplify administration and reduce error.

## 5.3.18 Federated Identity Management (FIM)

- FIM
  - Definition: enterprises share identity data across security domains.
  - Requires mutual trust, usually by contract, and a secure channel.
  - Audit impact: trust relationships extend your attack surface.
- Identity provider (IdP) authenticates users; service provider (SP) offers the service.
- FIM process
  - User requests service from the SP.
  - SP checks with the IdP for authentication.
  - User logs in at the IdP.
  - IdP issues an access token used for the application.
- FIM technologies
  - SAML: XML format for authentication and authorization exchange.
  - OAuth: authorization tokens without sharing credentials.
  - OpenID Connect (OIDC): identity layer on OAuth 2.0, using JSON.
  - SCIM: automates identity exchange for provisioning.
- Benefits
  - Enhanced security through fewer logins.
  - Enhanced user experience.
  - Single-point provisioning.
  - Secure resource sharing.
  - Easier information management.
  - Reduced costs and increased productivity.
- Limitations
  - Increased dependency on the IdP.
  - Reduced control and flexibility.
  - Increased costs.
- FIM versus SSO
  - FIM spans multiple domains; SSO works within one organization.
  - FIM is decentralized; SSO is centralized.
  - FIM is common on consumer sites; SSO in business.
  - FIM is less secure: compromised credentials work across entities.
- Exam traps
  - OAuth authorizes; SAML and OIDC carry identity assertions.
  - Cross-organization trust means FIM, not plain SSO.

## 5.3.19 Auditing Logical Access

- Familiarization with the IT environment
  - First step: interviews, walk-throughs, documents, risk assessments.
  - Document and test controls over access paths.
  - Evaluate the security environment against standards.
- Assessing and documenting access paths
  - A path runs from terminal to data.
  - Check origination, authorization, and validity of input data.
  - Check OS maintenance: patching, hardening, closing ports.
  - Firewalls do not stop HTTP-borne application attacks.
- Interviewing systems personnel
  - Talk to security administrator, network control manager, systems software manager.
  - Expand testing if administrator answers are weak.
  - Sample end users on policy awareness.
- Reviewing access control software reports
  - Review unsuccessful attempts by time, terminal, logon, file.
  - Check that reports support investigation.
- Reviewing the application systems operations manual
  - Covers platform, DBMS, compilers, interpreters, telecom monitors.
- Exam traps
  - Understanding the environment comes before testing controls.
  - Weak interview answers justify wider testing, not acceptance.

## Takeaway

- Identification, authentication, authorization, accountability form one chain; unique IDs enable it.
- Shared privileged accounts destroy accountability and are the top finding.
- Least privilege, SoD, and periodic revalidation prevent privilege creep.
- FAR is the security-critical biometric error; CER measures overall accuracy.
- Zero trust verifies every request; FIM extends trust across organizations.
- Contracts, right to audit, and controls must precede external-party access.
