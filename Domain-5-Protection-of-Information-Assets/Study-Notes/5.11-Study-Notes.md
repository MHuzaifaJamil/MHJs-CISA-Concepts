# 5.11 Information System Attack Methods and Techniques: Study Notes

**Scope:** Outline file 5.11-Information-System-Attack-Methods-and-Techniques.md (5.11.1 to 5.11.5). Conceptual, for audit and defense only.

## 5.11.1 Fraud risk factors

### Fraud and the fraud triangle
- Definition: fraud is using dishonest methods to take something valuable from someone.
- Use: the fraud triangle explains why people commit fraud.
- Audit impact: auditors use it to spot where fraud can arise.
- Definition: Cressey's fraud triangle has three elements: motivation, rationalization, opportunity.
- Use: remove or weaken at least one side to reduce fraud likelihood.

### Motivation
- Definition: a perceived financial or other need, such as debt or a grudge.
- Use: explains the "why" behind the act.
- Audit impact: organizations can rarely see or control it.
- Example: an employee with gambling debts feels pressure to take company money.

### Rationalization
- Definition: the way fraudsters justify the act to themselves.
- Use: lets the person keep a self-image as honest.
- Audit impact: hard to detect; fair treatment and ethics culture help.
- Example: "I am only borrowing it" or "my employer treats me unfairly".

### Opportunity
- Definition: the method and chance to commit the crime, via weak controls.
- Use: the only side organizations and auditors control most. ⭐
- Audit impact: audit focus is control weaknesses and missing fraud-detection procedures.
- Controls limiting opportunity: logical access, segregation of duties (SoD), HR security.
- Poor management oversight and abuse of position also create opportunity.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: pick the element the organization controls, which is opportunity.
  - Motivation and rationalization are personal, so controls cannot directly remove them.

## 5.11.2 Computer crime issues and exposures

### Threats to business
- Definition: computer crime is misuse of systems or data for gain.
- Use: lets auditors rank business impact of security violations.
- Audit impact: legal definition varies by jurisdiction; crime versus abuse affects required response.
- Financial loss: direct (stolen funds) or indirect (cost of correcting the exposure).
- Legal repercussions: privacy and human rights laws can also shield the perpetrator.
- Poor security can bring lawsuits from investors and insurers.
- Auditor should obtain legal assistance on legal issues. 
- Loss of credibility or competitive edge hurts firms that depend on public trust.
- Blackmail, industrial espionage, organized crime: extortion or selling proprietary data to competitors.
- Disclosure of confidential or embarrassing information brings legal and regulatory action.
- Sabotage aims at damage, not profit.
- Hacktivism is nonviolent use of illegal or ambiguous digital tools for political ends.
- If a crime is suspected, evidence must be protected and the incident reported.

### Possible perpetrators
- Hackers: skilled people testing system limits, often without intent to destroy.
- Crackers: break in with malicious intent; law enforcement targets them.
- Audit impact: auditor should not treat hacker and cracker as the same.
- Script kiddies: run tools written by others; cannot write their own.
- Employees: authorized or not; background screening is a key preventive control.
- IT personnel: easiest access as custodians; SoD and supervision reduce abuse.
- End users: broad knowledge of internal information and easy access.
- Former employees: risk if access is not blocked at termination, or back doors exist.
- Nations: attack critical infrastructure, such as SCADA systems.
- Outsiders: competitors, terrorists, organized criminals, phreakers.
- Part-time and temporary personnel, such as cleaners, have broad physical access.
- Third parties: vendors, visitors, consultants gain access through projects.
- Opportunists exploit easily accessible information or systems.
- Accidental unaware: someone who unknowingly commits a violation.
- Cross-border investigations may be hindered by political issues.
- Audit impact: add preventive measures for systems exposed to international attacks.

### Computer crime categories (Figure 5.57)
- Computer is the target: attack aims at a specific computer. Example: DoS, hacking.
- Computer is the subject: attacker uses one computer against another; target may be undefined.
- Example: distributed DoS and malware.
- Computer is the tool: crime committed with a computer; target is data. Example: fraud.
- Computer symbolizes the crime: attacker lures a user into giving confidential information.
- Example: phishing, fake websites, scam mail, fake resumes.
- Exam traps
  - Classify by role of the computer, not by the harm caused.
  - Attackers often chain several methods together.

### Social engineering 📓
- Definition: manipulating people instead of technology to obtain information or access.
- Use: bypasses firewalls, encryption, and authentication through human error.
- Audit impact: strong technology still fails if staff reveal passwords or addresses.
- Forms: phone impersonation (pretexting), dumpster diving, shoulder surfing.
- Best defense: ongoing awareness program for employees and third parties. ⭐
- Exam traps
  - Technical tools cannot fix a human-targeted attack; awareness training is the answer.

### Common attack methods (Figure 5.58)

#### Alteration attack
- Definition: unauthorized changes to data or code that harm integrity.
- Example: unauthorized library added during recompilation, or altered code in the SDLC.
- Control: cryptographic hash is the primary defense.
- Audit impact: compare hashes of code and data to detect change.

#### Botnets
- Definition: networks of compromised "zombie" computers run by an attacker.
- Use: DoS attacks, adware, spyware, spam.
- Spread: via worms, Trojan horses, or back doors.
- Audit impact: infected internal hosts can attack others and create legal exposure.

#### Denial of service (DoS)
- Definition: attack that makes a system or service unavailable.
- Audit impact: targets availability; mitigation relies on capacity, filtering, and monitoring.
- ICMP flood family overwhelms a target with ICMP traffic.
- Smurf: misconfigured devices relay packets to all hosts via a broadcast address.
- Ping flood: target drowned in ping packets.
- SYN flood: many TCP SYN requests with forged sender leave half-open connections.
- Teardrop: malformed overlapping IP fragments sent to the target.
- Peer-to-peer attack: file-sharing clients redirected to a victim website.
- Permanent DoS (phlashing): damages hardware until it must be replaced.
- Buffer overflow: consumes memory or CPU time.
- Bandwidth-saturating flood: works when attacker has more bandwidth than victim.
- Banana attack: redirects outgoing messages back onto the client.
- Pulsing zombie: hostile pinging by different computers over a long period.
- Nuke: fragmented or invalid ICMP packets slow a computer to a halt.
- Reflected attack: forged requests with the victim's spoofed address cause replies to flood.
- Unintentional attack: a sudden popularity spike denies the site without malice.
- ⚠ Check: manual lists brute force under DoS; 5.11.3 lists it as active attack.
- Distributed DoS (DDoS): many compromised systems flood one target together.
- Flooding: filling a host's buffer with connections that cannot be completed.

#### Eavesdropping
- Definition: intruder listens to network traffic to read its contents.
- Use: captures email, passwords, and card data.
- Audit impact: breaks confidentiality; encryption in transit is the control.

#### Phishing
- Definition: fraud that masquerades as a trusted entity to obtain sensitive data.
- Techniques: social engineering, link manipulation, website forgery.
- Spear phishing: pinpoint attack on a subset of people.
- Whaling: targets leadership; ransoms are likely paid to avoid reputation damage.
- Smishing: phishing by SMS text message.
- Vishing: phishing by phone call, often claiming a frozen account.
- Control: user awareness plus email filtering.

#### Interrupt attack
- Definition: malicious action by making the OS run a particular system call.
- Example: a boot sector virus issues an interrupt to write to the boot sector.

#### Juice jacking
- Definition: malware installed or data copied through a public USB charging port.
- Use: the charging port doubles as a data connection.
- Control: avoid public ports; use own charger or a data-blocking cable ➕.

#### Malicious code
- Trojan horse: program disguised as useful that performs unintended actions, like opening ports.
- Logic bomb: code triggered by a condition, time, or event; often a disgruntled insider.
- Example: a fired programmer's code deletes databases after termination.
- Honey tokens (pseudo flaws): fake flaws that act as alarms when exploited.
- Trap doors (back doors): code left by programmers for testing, or planted after compromise.
- Controls for logic bombs and back doors: threat vector analysis, SoD, code audits.

#### Man-in-the-middle attack
- Definition: attacker sits between two parties and poses as each to the other.
- Use: relays authentication so the attacker gains access.
- Audit impact: mutual authentication and encryption reduce the risk ➕.

#### Masquerading
- Definition: active attack presenting an identity other than the real one.
- Covers impersonation by people and machines.
- IP spoofing: machine masquerading with a forged IP address, often to break a firewall.
- Also hijacks a genuine authenticated session.

#### Message modification
- Definition: capturing a message, then altering, deleting, reordering, or delaying it.
- Example: changing a bank payment instruction in transit.

#### Network analysis
- Definition: systematic footprinting to profile a target's network.
- Gathers aliases, functions, internal addresses, gateways, and firewalls.
- Port scanning: probing a system to learn running services and OS.
- Audit impact: passive reconnaissance that precedes active attacks.

#### Packet replay
- Definition: passively capturing packets, then reinserting them as genuine traffic.
- Most effective when the receiving end is automated, with no human review.
- Combines passive and active modes.

#### Pharming
- Definition: redirecting users to a fake site by DNS poisoning.
- Methods: altering the hosts file, or exploiting DNS server software.
- Audit impact: antivirus and spyware removal cannot stop it.
- Control: antipharming measures are needed.

#### Piggybacking
- Definition: following an authorized person through a secure door.
- Also means attaching electronically to an authorized telecom link.
- Audit impact: classed as a physical access exposure.

#### Race conditions (TOC/TOU)
- Definition: exploiting the gap between security check and use of a service.
- Exposure grows with the time difference between check and use.
- Sequence or nonatomic cause: untrusted processes slip between steps of a secure program.
- Deadlock, livelock, or locking failure: trusted processes of same program interfere.
- Controls: careful programming and good administration.

#### Remote maintenance tools
- Definition: tools for remote system upkeep.
- Audit impact: poorly secured tools let hackers gain elevated access remotely.

#### Resource enumeration and browsing
- Definition: listing names, directories, privileges, shares, and policies on targets.
- Browsing is manual enumeration aided by commands and tools.
- Audit impact: least privilege and restricted shares limit what is exposed.

#### Salami technique versus rounding down
- Definition: slicing tiny amounts of money from many transactions.
- Rounding down: rounds off only the smallest monetary fraction.
- Salami: truncates more trailing digits, based on the program's formula.
- Example: 1,235,954.39 becomes 1,235,954.35 by rounding down.
- Example: the same amount becomes 1,235,954.30 or 1,235,954.00 by salami.
- Verification: both examples keep the leading digits; only the cents differ, per the manual.
- Control: program code review and reconciliation of totals ➕.

#### Injection attack (SQL)
- Definition: database commands inserted into input fields in place of expected data.
- Impact: loss, deletion, or modification of data and administrator-level operations.
- Control: input validation and sanitization (the manual ties this to XSS).

#### Zero-day exploit
- Definition: attack on a vulnerability that has no fix yet.
- Audit impact: usually cannot be prevented; antivirus is ineffective since it is unknown.
- Controls: continuous patching, sandboxing, and an updated incident response plan (IRP).

#### Cryptojacking
- Definition: hijacking devices to mine cryptocurrency without the owner's knowledge.
- Detection: monitor CPU usage on all network devices.
- Control: train staff to report slowdowns and suspicious emails.

#### Cross-site scripting (XSS)
- Definition: scripts that infect visiting users or redirect them to malicious sites.
- Control: input validation and sanitization, same as for SQL injection.
- Audit impact: check that web forms validate input.

#### Cross-site request forgery (CSRF)
- Definition: forces an authenticated user into unwanted actions on a web application.
- Use: often delivered as a link through chat or email.
- Impact: ordinary users lose funds; an admin victim compromises the whole application.
- Exam traps
  - XSS exploits trust in the site; CSRF exploits trust in the logged-in user ➕.

#### DNS tunneling
- Definition: hiding malware or data inside DNS queries to create a persistent channel.
- Impact: most firewalls miss it because DNS traffic often goes unmonitored.
- Defenses: block malicious DNS, denylist exfiltration destinations, analyze queries in real time.

#### URL poisoning (URL interpretation)
- Definition: altering or guessing URLs to reach admin or back-end pages.
- Control: MFA on sensitive areas of the website.

#### DNS spoofing
- Definition: altered DNS records send victims to a fake website.
- Impact: victims enter sensitive data; crimes may be committed in the organization's name.
- Control: keep DNS servers up to date.
- ⚠ Check: pharming and DNS spoofing overlap; the manual treats them as separate entries.

#### Traffic analysis
- Definition: inferring information from communication patterns, not message content.
- Use: attacker's option when messages are encrypted.
- Audit impact: encryption alone does not stop it.

#### Unauthorized access through the Internet or web services
- Cause: vulnerable Internet software and systems that are large and hard to configure.
- Examples: email forgery, Telnet passwords sent in clear, altered DNS bindings.
- Other examples: CGI scripts with full server control, client-side scripts from arbitrary locations.

#### Viruses, worms, spyware
- Virus: inserts code into other programs, self-replicates, may lie dormant until triggered.
- Worm: runs independently, travels across networks, does not alter other programs.
- Spyware: collects data such as keystrokes and card numbers, then transmits it.
- Exam traps
  - Worm needs no host program; virus attaches to one.

## 5.11.3 Internet threats and security

### Internet basics
- Definition: IP only addresses and routes packets.
- Impact: no proof of delivery, no address verification, receiver cannot confirm sender.
- Audit impact: other protocols must fill these gaps.

### Network security threats
- Definition: probing for network information, which leads to active attacks.
- Use: intruder gathers data to choose a target system.

### Passive attacks
- Definition: gather information without altering systems.
- Examples: network analysis, eavesdropping, traffic analysis.
- Audit impact: hard to detect; prevention through encryption and hardening.

### Active attacks
- Definition: attacks that modify data, deny service, or gain control.
- Examples: brute force, masquerading, packet replay, phishing, message modification.
- More examples: unauthorized Internet access, DoS, remote access penetration.
- Also: email bombing, spamming, and email spoofing.
- Impact: integrity, availability, and authentication attributes.
- Exam traps
  - Passive means observe only; active means alter or disrupt.
  - Traffic analysis is passive, packet replay is active.

### Causal factors for Internet attacks
- Free tools: scanners such as nmap, netcat, and strobe.
- Password crackers: John the Ripper and L0phtCrack.
- Lack of security awareness and training.
- Unpatched known vulnerabilities and poor configuration.
- Inadequate firewall and host OS security expose internal addresses.
- Audit impact: most intrusions are preventable through patching and configuration.

### Targeted attacks
- Definition: multilevel attack on one organization using custom malware that opens a back door.
- Attacker hides evidence, explores systems, and sends data out for a sustained period.
- Detection: egress monitoring and dummy accounts as indicators of compromise.
- Preventive: baselining, hardening, exception monitoring, endpoint controls.
- Example: stolen data leaves slowly through the back door until the breach surfaces.

### The OWASP Top 10
- Definition: list of the ten most common and impactful web application vulnerabilities.
- Use: checklist for web application security testing and review.
- Audit impact: compare application controls and test scope against it.

## 5.11.4 Malware

### What malware is
- Definition: malicious programs that attach to or run on host systems.
- Four targets: executable files, file directory, boot and system areas, data files.
- Types: ransomware, worms, viruses, adware, spyware, rootkits.
- Worms exploit configuration weaknesses and are severe in decentralized client-server settings.
- Entry routes: downloads, email attachments, online services, social media, LANs, shrink-wrapped software.

### Virus and worm controls
- Audit impact: preventive policy and detective technology are both required; neither works alone.
- Have vendors run demonstrations on their own machines.
- Scan shareware and new software before installing; commercial software may carry Trojans.
- Field technicians scan disks on a test machine first.
- Run antimalware on workstations and servers, with current releases.
- Ensure bridge, router, and gateway updates are authentic.
- Scan selected backups once malware is detected.
- Educate users; review the antimalware policy at least yearly.
- Prepare an eradication procedure and incident management procedures.
- ⚠ Check: manual also suggests encrypting files before execution; outline omits it ➕.

### Management procedural controls
- Build systems from clean original master copies.
- Scan media on a standalone machine used for nothing else.
- Update signatures frequently.
- Protect removable media from theft and hazards.

### Technical controls
- Boot malware protection: firmware-based protection built in.
- Remote booting: diskless workstations.
- Hardware-based password.
- Firewall blocks insecure protocols from external segments.
- Antimalware software is the most common and effective tool, both preventive and detective.
- Audit impact: it fails unless updated regularly.

### Antimalware tool types
- Scanners look for signature bit sequences typical of malware.
- Malware masks or signatures: specific code strings; can handle polymorphic variants.
- Heuristic scanners: judge code by statistical probability of malice.
- Audit impact: heuristic scanners produce high false positives.
- Active monitors: watch command and BIOS calls for malware-like actions.
- Limit: they cannot tell user requests from malware requests, so users confirm.
- Integrity CRC checkers: store checksums of clean programs and report changes.
- Limit: detect only after infection and miss new files not in the database.
- Behavior blockers: detect abnormal actions, such as writing to the boot sector.
- Behavior blockers can catch malware early; most hardware-based tools use them.
- Immunizers: append themselves to files or fool malware into thinking it already infected.
- Limit: cannot immunize against all known malware.
- Eradication programs: remove malware, sometimes deleting infected files.
- Inoculators: block a program from running if it contains malware.
- Exam traps
  - Signature scanners miss unknown malware; heuristics find it but add false positives.
  - CRC checkers assume files were clean at the start.

### Antimalware implementation strategies
- Principle: detect malware at its point of entry.
- Scheduled scans: for example daily or weekly.
- On-demand scans: run when the user requests.
- Continuous on-the-fly scanning: files scanned as processed.
- Malware walls scan incoming traffic as part of firewall technology.
- Three levels: SMTP protection, HTTP protection, FTP protection.
- Walls update signatures automatically and log incidents.
- Audit impact: a wall covers one channel only; endpoint software is still needed.
- Scanner features: detection reliability, resident memory, and efficiency.

## 5.11.5 Ransomware

### What ransomware is
- Definition: malware that encrypts data and demands payment for access.
- Use: often spreads over networks to paralyze file and database servers.
- Examples: WannaCry and Maze.
- Double extortion: also threatens to publish stolen data.
- Audit impact: restoring from backup does not remove the data-leak threat.

### Three stages (Figure 5.59)
- Stage one: access, mainly through a phishing email link or attachment.
- Stage two: files encrypted with asymmetric encryption; attacker holds private key.
- Stage three: ransom demand, such as a note demanding cryptocurrency.
- If paid, the operator supplies the private key.

### Best practices
- Secure networks: avoid public Wi-Fi; use a VPN.
- Continuous automated data backups allow recovery without paying.
- Secure immutable backups: ransomware seeks out and encrypts or deletes backups.
- Effective patch management closes exploited holes.
- Strong authentication, especially for RDP with stolen credentials.
- Minimal attack surface: prevention is the best mitigation.
- Antiransomware solutions detect the unique fingerprint of mass file encryption.
- Exam traps
  - Immutable, offline-style backups beat online backups, which ransomware can reach.
  - Prevention outranks paying or negotiating.

### Mitigating an active infection
- Quarantine the machine to stop spread to connected drives.
- Contain the spread; disconnecting the whole network may be needed.
- Do not switch systems off, to preserve volatile memory.
- Back up encrypted files; a free decryptor may appear later.
- Check for available decryptors.
- Locate patient zero to find the initial point of attack.
- ⚠ Check: manual defines patient zero oddly, as an organization alerted to breach.
- Wipe and restore from a clean backup or OS installation.
- Report to authorities; compliance implications may follow.
- Exam traps
  - First actions are isolate and preserve, not power off or pay.

### Ethical considerations
- Law enforcement urges not paying, because payment encourages more ransomware.
- In practice, firms run a cost-benefit analysis and plan for possible payment.
- Downside: attackers may give no key, or the key may fail.

## Takeaway
- Opportunity is the fraud-triangle element organizations control best.
- Human-targeted attacks need ongoing awareness training, not just technology.
- Passive attacks observe; active attacks alter, deny, or impersonate.
- Patching, hardening, and input validation block most Internet and web attacks.
- Combine preventive policy with detective antimalware; neither works alone.
- Immutable backups plus strong authentication are the core ransomware defenses.
