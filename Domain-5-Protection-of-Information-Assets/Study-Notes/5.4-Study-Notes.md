## 5.4.0 Big picture

- Perimeter security
  - Definition: controls placed at the border between trusted and untrusted networks.
  - Use: prevent and detect most attacks before they reach internal systems.
  - Audit impact: firewalls and IDSs need correct implementation and ongoing maintenance.
- Trusted network: internal network the organization controls.
- Untrusted network: any network outside organizational control, such as the Internet.
- Understanding a security tool requires understanding the infrastructure and protocols beneath it.
- Defense in depth: several layers so one failure does not expose everything.

## 5.4.1 IS network infrastructure

- Telecommunication link
  - Definition: a digital line that connects network devices over distance.
  - Use: carries data between sites.
  - Audit impact: link type affects cost, shared exposure and availability.
- Links are classified by provider type or technology type.
- Dedicated circuit (leased line)
  - Definition: a line reserved for one customer only.
  - Use: constant private capacity between two points.
  - Audit impact: costly, but not shared with other customers.
- Switched circuit
  - Definition: a connection set up on demand through the carrier network.
  - Use: temporary links, such as dial-up style connections.
- Packet switching
  - Definition: data is split into packets that share carrier infrastructure.
  - Use: lower cost than leased lines because carriers use capacity efficiently.
  - Audit impact: carrier network is shared, so rely on encryption and agreements.
- Virtual circuit: a logical path the carrier builds between customer sites.
- Cloud (carrier): the shared part of the carrier network.
- Packet-switching examples: ATM and SMDS.
- Example: a company links ten branches over carrier packet service instead of leased lines.
- Signal transmission methods on analog links
  - Baseband
    - Definition: signal sent directly on the link without frequency shifting.
    - Use: one channel at a time, usually half-duplex.
  - Broadband
    - Definition: several carrier frequencies on one medium, separated by gaps.
    - Use: many channels at once; full-duplex is possible.
- Half-duplex: send or receive, not both at once.
- Full-duplex: send and receive at the same time.
- ⚠ Check: full-duplex baseband modems exist; half-duplex is only typical.
- Exam traps
  - Leased line means dedicated and costly; packet switching means shared and cheaper.
  - Baseband carries a single channel; broadband multiplexes many channels.

## 5.4.2 Enterprise network architectures

- Modern enterprise network
  - Definition: centrally managed internetwork of high-speed LANs and WANs.
  - Use: serves client-server environments across the organization.
  - Audit impact: auditor must grasp design from business, performance and security views.
- Network segments group IT functions, each specialized for one task.
- Typical segments
  - Web front-end servers, public or private.
  - Application servers and database servers.
  - Mainframe servers reached through terminal emulation.
- Terminal emulation
  - Definition: software that lets a PC act as a legacy terminal.
  - Use: access to back-end legacy systems.
- Users are clustered in their own LANs with rapid access to resources.
- Service-oriented architecture (SOA)
  - Definition: web components interoperate in a loosely coupled, distributed way.
  - Use: share services using SOAP and XML.
  - Audit impact: central management helps troubleshooting, but many interfaces need securing.
- SOAP: Simple Object Access Protocol, an XML-based messaging protocol.
- XML: Extensible Markup Language, a format for structured data exchange.
- Auditor needs working knowledge of LAN and WAN specifications.
- Exam traps
  - Segmenting by function is a design choice, not a security control alone.
  - SOA components are loosely coupled, not tightly integrated.

## 5.4.3 Types of networks

- Personal area network (PAN)
  - Definition: network for one person's devices.
  - Use: connect phones, tablets, printers, cameras and scanners.
  - Audit impact: short range still allows nearby eavesdropping.
- PAN range is about 33 feet (10 meters).
- Wired PAN example: USB bus.
- Wireless PAN technologies: IrDA and Bluetooth.
- Piconet
  - Definition: Bluetooth network of up to eight active devices.
  - Use: master-slave communication among personal devices.
  - Audit impact: ideal conditions can stretch range to about 328 feet.
- Local area network (LAN)
  - Definition: network covering a limited area such as home, office or campus.
  - Use: high data rates over small geography.
- Ethernet and Wi-Fi (WLAN) are the two most common LAN technologies.
- Storage area network (SAN)
  - Definition: dedicated network linking storage devices to servers.
  - Use: centralizes storage and gives block-level access.
  - Audit impact: weak authentication and lateral movement make SANs attractive targets.
- SAN versus NAS
  - SAN gives block-level storage; NAS gives file-level storage.
  - NAS is a single RAID-based device; SAN uses a network of devices.
  - SAN devices may include SSDs, cloud storage and flash storage.
- SAN risks
  - Physical threats to the data.
  - Protocol and configuration vulnerabilities.
  - Strict authentication often not enforced.
  - Attackers move laterally using stolen credentials.
- SAN controls
  - Recommend strong, frequent authentication.
  - Track who may access each application or database.
  - Each department protects the sensitive data it stores.
- Wide area network (WAN)
  - Definition: network covering a city, region, nation or international link.
  - Use: connect LANs and other networks across locations.
- The Internet is the largest WAN.
- WANs may be private, or ISP-built to connect LANs to the Internet.
- Wireless WAN (WWAN): a WAN using wireless links.
- Metropolitan area network (MAN)
  - Definition: WAN limited to a city or region.
  - Use: faster transfer rates than typical wide-area links.
- Exam traps
  - Block-level means SAN; file-level means NAS.
  - Piconet is Bluetooth, master-slave, up to eight active devices.
  - Internet equals largest WAN, not a MAN.

## 5.4.4 Network services

- Network service
  - Definition: function provided by OS software that allows orderly use of network resources.
  - Use: users and applications request it through defined interfaces.
- Network file system: shares files, printers and other resources.
- Email services: send unstructured messages to people or groups.
- Print services: a print server manages requests from other devices.
- Remote access services: let a device act as if directly attached.
- Directory services
  - Definition: store information about network resources and help locate them.
  - Use: manage user access to network resources.
  - Audit impact: directory misconfiguration can expose or deny many resources.
- Network management
  - Definition: functions that control and maintain the network.
  - Use: reports line status, active terminals, queue length, error rate and traffic.
  - Audit impact: gives operators early warning before reliability is affected.
- DHCP
  - Definition: server assigns IP address, gateway, subnet mask and DNS addresses.
  - Use: automatic client configuration without manual pool management.
  - Audit impact: leases keep addresses unique while an assignment is valid.
- DNS
  - Definition: translates node names into IP addresses.
  - Use: lets users reach hosts by name.
  - Audit impact: tampered DNS redirects users, so protect and monitor it.
- Example: a laptop joins Wi-Fi, gets an IP lease, then resolves a website name.
- Exam traps
  - DHCP assigns addresses; DNS translates names. Do not swap them.
  - The server, not a human administrator, manages the DHCP pool.

## 5.4.5 Network standards and protocols

- Network architecture standard
  - Definition: reference model for structuring computer and network communication.
  - Use: builds an integrated, scalable, reliable and secure LAN and WAN environment.
- Challenges that standards help meet
  - Interoperability: different systems, media and speeds working together.
  - Availability: continuous, reliable, secure 24/7 service.
  - Flexibility: scalability for expansion and new applications.
  - Maintainability: central support across heterogeneous but integrated systems.
- Organizations must define specifications for the LANs and WANs they build.
- FTP 📓⭐
  - Definition: File Transfer Protocol for moving files between hosts.
  - Use: bulk file transfer.
  - Audit impact: cleartext transmission and complex port usage make it high risk.
- SMTP, SOAP and HTTP risk comparison ➕
- ⚠ Check: manual 5.4.5 has no protocol risk ranking; source is my notes.
- ⚠ Check: my notes flag both FTP and HTTP; resolve before exam.
- Exam traps
  - Cleartext credentials plus multiple ports points to FTP as greatest exposure.
  - Choose the protocol with weakest built-in protection, not the most popular one.

## 5.4.6 Virtual Private Networks (VPN) 📓

- VPN
  - Definition: encrypted packets sent over virtual connections on the public Internet.
  - Use: extend the corporate network to remote offices, workers and partners.
  - Audit impact: cheaper than leased lines, but transmission security needs review.
- VPNs are platform independent; only remote software must be installed.
- VPN protects data on public Wi-Fi 📓
- Example: an employee at a cafe connects through a VPN client.
- VPN types
  - Remote-access VPN: telecommuters and mobile users to enterprise WAN.
  - Intranet VPN: connects branch offices within one enterprise.
  - Extranet VPN: gives business partners limited access to each other's networks.
- Intranet versus extranet differ only in who manages the VPN.
- Extranet control is weaker, so each company manages its own VPN.
- Primary VPN issues
  - Transmission security: preventing hijacking and malware entry.
  - Managing the technology.
  - Configuration management.
  - Keeping information unaltered, accurate and reliable.
- Connectivity decisions use policy, business model, server access and data sharing needs.
- Intrusion risks include reputational, regulatory, contractual, operational and financial harm.

### VPN protocols

- IPSec
  - Definition: suite securing IP communications, used often for site-to-site VPNs.
  - Use: tunnel mode encrypts the whole packet including header.
  - Audit impact: transport mode encrypts only the data portion.
- IKEv2
  - Definition: key exchange protocol that can re-establish lost connections.
  - Use: suits mobile devices switching between Wi-Fi and mobile data.
- PPTP
  - Definition: older tunneling protocol wrapping PPP frames using GRE.
  - Use: remote access and site-to-site links.
  - Audit impact: known vulnerabilities, so pair with compensating controls.
- SSTP
  - Definition: wraps PPP traffic inside an SSL channel over HTTPS.
  - Use: passes through firewalls and web proxies that block other protocols.
- L2TP
  - Definition: tunnels multiprotocol traffic, combining PPTP and L2F features.
  - Use: relies on IPSec transport mode for encryption (L2TP/IPsec).
  - Audit impact: both client and server must support L2TP and IPsec.
- L2TP/IPsec can implement perfect forward secrecy.
- OpenVPN
  - Definition: open-source VPN using SSL/TLS for key exchange.
  - Use: traverses NAT and firewalls; supports site-to-site and remote access.
  - Audit impact: authenticates by secret key, certificate or username and password.
- Perfect forward secrecy: a stolen key cannot decrypt past sessions.

### VPN best practices

- Establish a VPN policy covering passwords, authentication, encryption, access and logging.
- Review and update the VPN policy regularly.
- Prefer standards-based VPNs such as IKE/IPSec over custom SSL/TLS code.
- Disable any custom SSL/TLS fallback tunnel if the VPN offers one.
- Use strong cryptography: TLS 1.3 and AES 256-bit.
- Prefer FIPS-validated encryption algorithms.
- Use MFA and replace passwords with client certificates or tokens.
- Patch VPN software continuously; confirm third-party vendor code is current.
- Strict access control
  - Allow only UDP ports 500 and 4500 for IKE/IPsec.
  - Limit the VPN endpoint using an IP allowlist.
  - Block management interfaces reachable through the VPN.
- ⚠ Check: manual says TCP port 433 for SSL/TLS VPNs; likely typo for 443.
- VPN encrypts but does not inspect or filter tunnel traffic.
- Pass VPN traffic through a full stack: WAF and IDS/IPS.
- Harden configuration: strong authentication, certificates, logging, auditing and an IPS.
- VPN stops eavesdropping and man-in-the-middle, but not malware.
- Use company hardware with antivirus and antimalware for VPN access.
- Test VPN capacity before deployment; extra security features add latency.
- Monitor and log VPN usage to spot unauthorized attempts.
- Exam traps
  - Public Wi-Fi interception: answer is VPN or encryption in transit.
  - A VPN protects confidentiality in transit, not endpoint malware.
  - Tunnel mode hides headers; transport mode protects payload only.
  - Extranet VPN: each partner manages its own end.

## 5.4.7 Network Attached Storage (NAS)

- NAS
  - Definition: centralized file server letting many users store and share files.
  - Use: simple shared file storage over the network.
  - Audit impact: NAS security supports compliance, accountability and reduced data theft.
- NAS vulnerabilities
  - Unsecure network configuration: open networks and unencrypted sessions expose devices.
  - Unsecure passwords: unencrypted or carelessly shared credentials can be stolen.
  - Insufficient authentication: attackers gain access then launch further attacks.
  - Leakage from other devices: IoT devices can pass malware to NAS.
  - Malware exposure: worms, viruses and ransomware target poorly secured NAS.
  - Command injection: attackers may control drives and sometimes gain root access.
- NAS best practices
  - Train all employees to recognize social engineering such as phishing.
  - Keep visitors away from the NAS location.
  - Update OS and firmware promptly.
  - Secure Internet connections: IP allowlists, HTTPS and encryption in transit.
  - Store passwords securely; never share them by email.
  - Enable MFA.
  - Use HTTPS rather than HTTP for remote access.
  - Turn on the built-in firewall, allowing trusted sources only.
  - Connect through a VPN, especially for remote access.
- Example: ransomware spreads from an unpatched IoT camera to an open NAS.
- Exam traps
  - Best fix for exposed remote NAS access: VPN plus MFA, not only passwords.
  - NAS means file-level; do not confuse it with SAN block storage.

## 5.4.8 Content Delivery Networks (CDN)

- CDN
  - Definition: interconnected servers placed near users to cut distance 📓
  - Use: speed delivery, balance load and improve availability, helping against DDoS 📓
  - Audit impact: a CDN breach affects many third parties at once.
- Request flow
  - Request routes to the nearest server location.
  - Cache hit: the CDN server answers directly.
  - Cache miss: request goes to origin, which answers and caches a copy.
- Example: a user in South Africa receives files from a nearby African server.
- CDN gives geographic and logical load balancing with low latency.
- Content types
  - Dynamic: generated on the fly, such as PHP or Java output.
  - Static: unchanging items such as images, CSS and JavaScript.
  - Streaming: video or audio played in the browser.
- Benefits
  - Better user experience and lower bounce rate.
  - Global accessibility through multiple points of presence (PoPs).
  - Scattered PoPs speed delivery near users.
  - Automatic data analytics, since charges follow data volume.
  - Lower network congestion by redirecting users to quieter paths.
  - Higher content quality and trust.
  - Lower overhead through one global provider.
- Security risks
  - Session hijacking: cached CDN servers can be hijacked; CDNs do not block bad traffic.
  - Credential theft: shared cache exposes many organizations' customers.
  - Distributed DoS: exploits can make servers repeat commands until overloaded.
  - Compromised server: attacker may read or modify all cached content.
  - Performance risk: misconfiguration or outage can make the site unavailable.
  - Increased cost profile: expensive to build and maintain.
  - Loss of control: dependence on the provider, including provider failure.
- Best practices
  - Evaluate providers: caching behavior, penetration testing frequency, failure handling.
  - Implement a WAF with CDN capability.
  - Check SSL certificate compatibility, especially for card payments.
  - Route content properly and restrict access by country or region.
  - Delete content at origin and manage caching rules.
  - Maintain DR/BC procedures and fallback facilities.
- Exam traps
  - A CDN improves availability but is not a firewall.
  - Shared cache means one breach reaches many tenants.

## 5.4.9 Network Time Protocol (NTP)

- NTP
  - Definition: synchronizes system clocks over packet-switched networks.
  - Use: runs over UDP at the OSI application layer.
  - Audit impact: Kerberos and log correlation depend on consistent time.
- Supports log timestamp synchronization 📓
- Benefits
  - Improves security by keeping time consistent across systems.
  - Reduces spoofing, since servers can detect clock-slowing attempts.
  - Improves accuracy versus manual methods.
  - Easy configuration by staff with limited security knowledge.
  - Improves availability and consistent timekeeping.
  - Improves reliability using redundant servers; no Internet needed.
  - Improves network management through monitoring and logs.
  - Improves compliance with a traceable time source.
- Healthcare and finance often need traceable time for legal reasons.
- NTP risks
  - Weak security options: MD5 offers little, SHA-1 is weaker, Autokey is insecure.
  - Clients remain exposed to continued spoofing.
  - Fake UDP fragments change delivery time, and detection is difficult.
  - Kiss-of-death (KoD) abuse stops clients from querying for long periods.
  - Time-dependent protocols: advancing time expires records early; setting time back enables replay.
- Unprotected NTP lets attackers change log sequence and hide attacks.
- Unsynchronized clocks make cross-system log correlation hard.
- Best practices
  - Use public NTP for external-facing hosts.
  - Use hierarchy with Stratum 0 or 1 appliances; a private server is cost-effective.
  - Standardize on UTC.
  - Evaluate need for cryptography; key management adds cost.
  - Use at least two servers for redundancy.
  - Limit stratum levels, since each adds accuracy loss.
  - Monitor server status, GPS signal loss and SNMP data.
- Example: skewed clocks break Kerberos tickets and misorder incident logs.
- Exam traps
  - Time sync matters for forensics: logs from different systems must align.
  - Best fix is redundant, hierarchical, monitored servers, not a single source.

## 5.4.10 Applications in a networked environment

### Client-server technology

- Client-server
  - Definition: each node is a server giving services or a client using them.
  - Use: splits processing between client and server machines.
  - Audit impact: tasks run on both sides, creating several access routes.
- Server functions: file sharing, printing, database access, email and application logic.
- Thin client
  - Definition: depends mainly on the server for processing.
  - Use: conveys input and output, often just a browser.
- Thick (fat) client
  - Definition: does most processing locally.
  - Use: sends only data for communication and storage.
- Two-tier architecture
  - Definition: thick client running application logic plus database servers.
  - Audit impact: clients must stay synchronized; scalability is limited.
- Three-tier architecture
  - Definition: thin client, application servers, database servers.
  - Use: separates program logic from other code.
  - Audit impact: cheaper clients, better scalability, load balanced across servers.
- Three-tier can scale to thousands of concurrent users.
- N-tiered (multi-tiered): more than two tiers; harder to build and maintain.
- N-tiered designs depend on middleware for load balancing and failover.
- Sequence diagrams (UML) describe client-server interaction.
- Client-server security depends on LAN, client, OS, database and middleware.
- Gaps between components are an extra risk to examine.
- Two-tier gap: users with database accounts can bypass application controls.
- Two-tier gap: a proxy account's database password may be stored insecurely.
- Auditor checks
  - Application controls cannot be bypassed.
  - Passwords are always encrypted.
  - Access to configuration or initialization files is minimal.
  - Access to those files is audited.

### Middleware

- Middleware
  - Definition: software gluing two distinct applications together.
  - Use: provides identification, authentication, authorization, directories and security.
  - Audit impact: spans environments, so data and program integrity can suffer.
- Common uses
  - Transaction processing (TP) monitors: monitor database transactions, mainly for load balancing.
  - Remote procedure call (RPC): client runs a program on a remote computer.
  - Object request broker (ORB): shared reusable objects, such as CORBA or COM/DCOM.
  - Messaging servers: asynchronously queue, prioritize and process messages.
- Risk: integrity loss when portability across platforms lacks control.
- Controls: testing and approval, authorized changes, version control.

### On-demand computing

- On-demand (utility) computing
  - Definition: resources allocated to users by current need.
  - Use: supply bandwidth, CPU, memory or applications when required.
  - Audit impact: confidentiality of data held by the third-party provider.
- Benefit: no payment for excess capacity.
- Exam traps
  - Direct database accounts in two-tier designs are the classic control bypass.
  - Three-tier fixes scalability and logic separation; it does not remove all risks.
  - Utility computing concern is provider confidentiality, not cost.

## 5.4.11 Network infrastructure security

### Controls over the communication network

- Network control
  - Definition: management of network devices through a control terminal and software.
  - Use: operate, monitor and maintain the network.
- Controls
  - Network control staff have adequate training and experience.
  - Separate network control functions and rotate duties where possible.
  - Software restricts operators, for example from amending operator logs.
  - Software keeps an audit trail of operator activity.
  - Operations management reviews audit trails periodically.
  - Document operation standards and review them periodically.
  - Monitor system engineers' network access closely.
  - Analyze workload for balance, fast response and efficiency.
  - Keep a terminal identification file to authenticate terminals.
  - Encrypt messages in transit where appropriate.
  - Restrict remote printing so sensitive documents are not exposed.
  - Harden devices: firmware upgrades, patching and updates.
  - Consolidate device logs with firewall and client-server OS logs.
  - Keep a dynamic device inventory to know who uses which computer.
  - Identify users at every step; watch predefined names such as SYSTEM.
  - Use ITIL practices for SLAs on network operations.
- ITIL
  - Definition: IT service management guidance framework.
  - Use: sets SLAs, incident handling and audit controls for network operations.
- Fibre Channel (FC) connections often manage large storage units.

### Internet security controls

- Internet security framework
  - Definition: policies and procedures setting rules for Internet use.
  - Use: basis from which technical controls are implemented.
- Policy rules
  - Reserve Internet privileges for those with a business need.
  - Define resources available to outside users.
  - Define trusted and untrusted networks.
- Classify sensitivity and criticality of information resources.
- Write OS hardening guidelines, including blocking exploitable Internet services.
- Supporting processes
  - Periodic risk assessments of Internet-based web applications.
  - Security awareness tailored to responsibility level.
  - Firewall standards and IDS standards.
  - Remote access control, including dial-up access.
  - Incident handling: detection, response, containment, recovery.
  - Configuration management to control the security baseline.
  - Encryption for assets passing over the Internet.
  - Common desktop environment to control what users see.
  - Monitor Internet activity; notify users through CERT bulletins.
- Exam traps
  - Policy and classification come first; technical controls follow from them.
  - Operators must not alter their own activity logs.
  - Separation and rotation of duties applies to network control too.

## 5.4.12 Firewalls 📓

- Firewall
  - Definition: device where network connections enter a site, applying traffic rules.
  - Use: perimeter control between trusted and untrusted networks, also internal zones.
  - Audit impact: protects only traffic that crosses it.
- General features
  - Block access to particular Internet sites.
  - Limit public-segment traffic to relevant addresses and ports.
  - Prevent certain users reaching certain servers.
  - Monitor and record communications for investigations.
  - Encrypt packets between sites via VPN.
  - Extended: protect against viruses and known OS exploits.
- Deny-all philosophy
  - Definition: access denied unless there is a specific business reason.
  - Audit impact: the preferred, widely adopted stance.
- Accept-all philosophy
  - Definition: access allowed unless there is a reason to deny.
  - Audit impact: rarely adopted; riskier.
- Three ORM categories: packet filtering, application firewall systems, stateful inspection.
- Firewalls are hardware and software built from routers, servers and software.

### Packet filtering firewall 📓

- Packet filtering firewall
  - Definition: first-generation firewall; a screening router checks packet headers against ACLs.
  - Use: filters by IP addresses and port numbers at the network layer.
  - Audit impact: verify ACL accuracy and filter configuration.
- Advantage: simple with stable performance.
- Weakness: improperly configured filters and attacks tunneled over permitted services.
- Weakness: direct packet exchange; exposure grows with permitted hosts and services.
- Weakness: one compromised router can expose every internal system.
- Weakness: large rule bases are hard to design and maintain.
- IP spoofing
  - Definition: attacker fakes a trusted or internal source address.
  - Audit impact: dropping internal-looking external packets helps; spoofed trusted external addresses defeat it.
- Source routing
  - Definition: attacker defines the packet's route to bypass the firewall.
  - Audit impact: drop packets with source routing enabled.
- Miniature fragment attack
  - Definition: tiny fragments hope only the first is inspected.
  - Audit impact: configure the firewall to drop fragmented packets.

### Stateless and stateful firewalls

- Stateless firewall 📓
  - Definition: filters using static header information only.
  - Audit impact: check rules for basic IP and port restrictions.
- Stateful inspection firewall 📓
  - Definition: tracks connection state, matching responses to outbound requests.
  - Use: records destination IPs of outbound packets, then checks incoming sources.
  - Audit impact: blocks attacks initiated from outside; more complex to administer.
- Stateful inspection works efficiently at the transport layer.
- It uses less CPU than full-time application proxies.

### Next Generation Firewall (NGFW) 📓

- NGFW
  - Definition: firewall combining packet filtering and stateful inspection with newer capabilities.
  - Use: filters by application and inspects packet contents.
  - Audit impact: validate that it performs stated functions before advising adoption.
- Features
  - Application awareness: rules by application rather than only port.
  - Deep packet inspection (DPI): examines packet contents, beyond the header.
  - Integrated IDS/IPS: signature-, policy- or anomaly-based monitoring and blocking.
  - High performance: avoids slowing traffic despite extra processing.
  - Threat intelligence: stays current on bad actors.
  - Antimalware: may add antivirus protection.
- NGFWs receive future updates through a built-in update path.
- Firewall as a Service (FWaaS)
  - Definition: cloud-based firewall maintained by the service provider.
  - Use: scales automatically; removes patching and sizing burden.
- Benefits: robust security, intelligent automation, streamlined architecture.
- ⚠ Check: manual text says NGFW reaches layer 7; its table says 3-4.

### Application firewall systems

- Application firewall system
  - Definition: gateway that passes information without direct packet exchange.
  - Use: acts as proxy between internal and external systems.
  - Audit impact: stronger than packet filtering; poorer performance and scalability.
- Application-level gateway: one proxy per service, such as HTTP or FTP.
- Circuit-level gateway: one general-purpose proxy validating TCP and UDP sessions; rare.
- Bastion host
  - Definition: heavily fortified host handling all incoming Internet requests.
  - Audit impact: a break-in compromises only the bastion, not the network.
- Proxy hides internal addresses and can log all traffic.
- NAT
  - Definition: maps private internal addresses to public addresses.
  - Use: hides the internal network.
- Mitigate performance limits with load balancing and a failover firewall.

### Web Application Firewall (WAF)

- WAF
  - Definition: firewall filtering HTTP traffic to and from web applications.
  - Use: works like a reverse proxy protecting web servers.
  - Audit impact: policies need regular updates; some use ML to self-update.
- Proxies mainly protect clients; WAFs protect servers.
- Inbound protection inspects application traffic for dangerous payloads.
- Outbound protection blocks sensitive data leakage.
- Blocklist WAF: negative model; blocks known threats, needs constant updating.
- Allowlist WAF: positive model; blocks all but pre-approved traffic.
- Most WAFs are hybrid.
- Network-based WAF: hardware, low latency, expensive, needs physical upkeep.
- Host-based WAF: co-located with the application, scalable, costly to maintain.
- Cloud-based WAF: minimal effort, regular updates, less threat visibility.
- WAF detects XSS, CSRF, L7 DDoS, injection and broken authentication.
- NGFW protects the network at layers 3-4; WAF protects web applications at layer 7.
- NGFW also covers DNS, FTP and SSH protocol attacks.
- Example: NGFW is the building entrance; WAF guards the manager's office.

### Firewall implementations

- Screened-host firewall
  - Definition: packet-filtering router plus a bastion host on the private network.
  - Audit impact: intruder must penetrate two systems.
- Dual-homed firewall
  - Definition: system with two or more interfaces on different networks.
  - Use: blocks or filters traffic passing between them.
- DMZ (screened-subnet) firewall
  - Definition: two packet-filtering routers plus a bastion host forming an isolated network.
  - Use: hosts public servers and bastion information servers.
  - Audit impact: most secure; intruder must penetrate three devices.
- DMZ benefits: private addresses stay hidden; internal systems lack direct Internet access.
- Outside router limits traffic to the DMZ; inside router controls DMZ-to-private access.

### Firewall issues and platforms

- False sense of security; most incidents come from insiders firewalls do not control.
- Modems can bypass firewalls; control or prohibit them.
- Misconfiguration may let dangerous services through.
- Screening router is sometimes mistaken for a full firewall.
- Monitoring and log review may be irregular.
- Policies may not be maintained.
- Network-layer firewalls miss SQL injection and buffer overflow; newer ones help.
- Hardware platform: faster, minimal overhead, less flexible and scalable.
- Software platform: flexible with extra services, slower, heavier overhead.
- Appliances are preferred: hardened OS, faster setup and recovery.
- Server-based firewalls risk compromise through vulnerable host OS.
- Firewall cannot identify repeated attacks; add an IDS inside the network behind it 📓⭐
- Exam traps
  - Repeated attack attempts missed: place IDS inside, behind the firewall.
  - Deny-all is the default philosophy; accept-all is the distractor.
  - Packet filtering checks headers only; it cannot see application content.
  - DMZ beats screened-host because it adds a third barrier.
  - Firewall alone cannot stop insiders, modems or application-layer attacks.

## 5.4.13 Unified Threat Management (UTM)

- UTM
  - Definition: multiple security features combined in a single device.
  - Use: one appliance covering many protections.
  - Audit impact: central point of control, but also a concentrated point of failure.
- Components
  - Antivirus: signature databases detect infected files, Trojans, worms and spyware.
  - Antimalware: detects and filters malware; heuristics analyze behavior to find novel threats.
  - Sandboxing: confines suspicious files so they cannot touch other programs.
  - Firewall: scans inbound and outbound traffic for attack attempts.
  - IDS/IPS: detects and prevents attacks together.
  - VPN: encrypted tunnel through a public network.
  - DLP: detects and blocks data breaches and exfiltration.
  - Web filtering: stops browsers loading specified sites.
  - Content filtering, email filtering and anti-spam protect users.
- Benefits
  - Security flexibility from a pool of tools.
  - Adaptability, with automatic updates.
  - Visibility: central view helps stop multi-module attacks.
  - Lower costs: fewer devices and fewer staff.
  - Greater risk awareness, enabling APT protection.
  - Faster security solutions through efficient use of resources.
  - Less complexity than separate products.
  - Simpler monitoring and compliance via identity-based least-privilege policies.
- Example: a small branch uses one UTM appliance instead of five separate devices.
- ⚠ Check: manual lists benefits only; single-point-of-failure remark is my addition, not manual-sourced.
- Exam traps
  - UTM means consolidation in one device, not a new technique.
  - Cost and complexity reduction are its main stated advantages.

## 5.4.14 Network segmentation

- Network segmentation
  - Definition: splitting a network into smaller zones with their own security requirements.
  - Use: improves performance and security; also called partitioning or isolation.
  - Audit impact: isolates infrastructure from the rest of the network.
- Segmentation: physical, hardware-based, controls north-south traffic.
- Micro-segmentation: virtual, software-based, controls east-west traffic.
- Micro-segmentation puts each device or application in its own segment.
- Treat both as related concepts with similar benefits and practices.
- North-south: traffic between clients and servers across the perimeter.
- East-west: traffic moving laterally within the network.
- Methods
  - VLAN segmentation: uses VLANs or subnets; complex, needs regular re-architecting.
  - Firewall segmentation: internal firewalls separate functional areas.
  - SDN segmentation: SDN overlay, typical for micro-segmentation; complex to manage.
  - Host-based segmentation: agents on endpoints report to a central dashboard.
- Benefits
  - Better performance through less congestion.
  - Limits attack damage by containing it in one zone.
  - Protects vulnerable devices from malicious traffic.
  - Improves compliance by shrinking in-scope systems.
  - Enhances security: defense in depth, hinders lateral movement.
  - Improves monitoring because smaller segments are easier to watch.
- Best practices
  - Avoid under- or over-segmentation.
  - Audit regularly, including vulnerability assessments and penetration tests.
  - Give each third party a separate portal.
  - Combine similar resources in the same zone.
  - Define access rights clearly.
  - Apply the principle of least privilege (POLP).
- Example: payment systems sit in their own zone, narrowing compliance scope.
- Exam traps
  - Segmentation limits lateral movement; it does not stop initial entry.
  - Compliance benefit is reduced scope, not removal of requirements.
  - Too many segments is also a flaw, not a strength.

## 5.4.15 Endpoint security

- Endpoint
  - Definition: any device enabling a user to connect to the network.
  - Use: ATMs, IoT devices, industrial machines, laptops, medical devices, phones, printers, servers.
  - Audit impact: endpoints are key entry points for attackers; audit them regularly.
- BYOD and IoT growth sharply increased the number of endpoints.
- Endpoint security: measures applied on each device connected to the network.
- Benefits
  - Keeps connected devices secure; endpoints become the perimeter.
  - Promotes data security, since data loss threatens the business.
  - Supports secure remote working when perimeter security is insufficient.

### Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)

- EDR
  - Definition: tools that continuously monitor endpoints and trigger automatic responses.
  - Use: collects data for threat analysis and attack prevention.
  - Audit impact: contains incidents at their source before they spread.
- Components
  - Data collection agents: gather processes, activity volume and connection status centrally.
  - Automated response: preset rules alert staff or log the user off.
  - Analysis and forensics: real-time analytics, threat hunting and post-mortem review.
- Advantages
  - Improved monitoring of devices at all times.
  - Improved database security: cloud threat databases reduce endpoint bloat.
  - Fast access to security data; can isolate the affected endpoint.
  - Centralized visibility through a management console.
  - Application control blocks unsafe or unauthorized applications; encryption limits data loss.
  - Malware detection supports containment and remediation.
- Deployment
  - On-location: locally hosted hub; legacy model that can create silos.
  - Cloud: central console in the cloud; removes silos and extends reach.
  - Hybrid: mixes both; common with remote work.
- Desired features: antimalware, ML for zero-day, web security, DLP, firewall, email gateway.
- Also desired: forensic capability and a centralized management console.

### Extended Detection and Response (XDR)

- XDR
  - Definition: broader than EDR; integrates endpoints, cloud, email, workloads, users and networks.
  - Use: correlates threat data with analytics and automation.
  - Audit impact: unified platform rather than a collection of endpoint tools.
- Parts
  - Data analysis: correlates alerts into a few high-priority alerts.
  - Detection: builds baselines, spots threats using software, ports and protocols.
  - Response: contains threats and updates policies; EDR responds only on endpoints and workloads.
- Benefits
  - Granular visibility, including entry point, victims, origin and spread.
  - Prioritization by grouping alerts across the MITRE ATT&CK framework.
  - Faster detection and response through automation.
  - Operational efficiency via centralized collection and response.
  - More sophisticated, system-tailored responses.
  - Better prevention using threat intelligence and adaptive ML.
  - Improved control and fewer false positives.
- Exam traps
  - EDR covers endpoints; XDR spans endpoints plus cloud, email and network.
  - XDR reduces alert overload by correlation, not by dropping alerts.
  - Endpoint security matters most when users work outside the corporate perimeter.

## Takeaway

- Deny-all is the preferred firewall philosophy; justify each allowed service.
- Place an IDS behind the firewall to catch repeated or missed attacks.
- VPN encrypts in transit but does not inspect traffic or stop malware.
- FTP is high risk: cleartext and complex ports.
- Segmentation limits lateral movement and shrinks compliance scope.
- EDR watches endpoints; XDR correlates across endpoints, cloud, email and network.
