## 5.15 Introduction: why procedure matters

- Evidence collection: gathering data from a crime scene for later legal use.
  - Definition: Gathering and keeping digital proof of an incident.
  - Use: Proves what happened, who did it, and how.
  - Audit impact: Bad handling can make evidence useless in court.
- Many computer crimes go unreported because they are never detected.
- Firms often hide incidents to avoid bad publicity and just fix the hole.
- Many laws protect physical property, so applying them to computer crime is hard.
- Even where laws are updated, investigators may lack tools or procedure knowledge.
- Without proper procedure, data may be damaged and evidence unusable for prosecution.
- Leave the environment and evidence unaltered after a computer crime. ⭐
- Call specialist law enforcement, or use a qualified in-house incident response team.
- Example: An admin reboots a hacked server; memory evidence is lost forever.
- Exam traps
  - First action is preserve and call specialists, not investigate or clean up.
  - Fixing the vulnerability first is wrong because it alters the evidence.

## 5.15.1 Types of investigations

- Investigation type: the kind of inquiry, set by who investigates and why.
  - Definition: A category of inquiry with its own goal and proof standard.
  - Use: Decides how evidence is gathered and handled.
  - Audit impact: Auditor must advise on handling that fits the type.
- Administrative investigation
  - Definition: Gives authorities facts to choose a course of action objectively.
  - Use: Typical in HR cases, such as a manager accused of impropriety.
- Criminal investigation ⭐
  - Definition: Inquiry when a crime is known or suspected.
  - Use: Organization works with law enforcement to convict the perpetrator.
  - Audit impact: Evidence may need sharing with the defense.
  - Audit impact: Evidence must be handled so a court can use it.
  - Proof standard: beyond a reasonable doubt.
- Civil investigation ⭐
  - Definition: One party sues another, such as for trademark or IP theft.
  - Use: Seeks monetary damages, not jail or a criminal record.
  - Proof standard: preponderance of evidence, a lower burden than criminal.
- Regulatory investigation
  - Definition: A regulator checks an organization for breaking specific rules.
  - Example: The US Securities and Exchange Commission investigating a listed company.
  - Audit impact: Organization must comply and supply all relevant evidence.
- Industry standards investigation
  - Definition: Checks whether an organization follows a specific industry standard.
  - Use: Firms follow standards voluntarily to cut risk and build reputation.
- Forensic investigation
  - Definition: Scientific gathering and preserving of potential criminal evidence from IS environments.
  - Use: Produces a structured investigation with a documented chain of evidence.
  - Audit impact: Evidence must be presentable in a court of law.
- Exam traps
  - Criminal means beyond reasonable doubt; civil means preponderance. Do not swap them.
  - Civil outcome is money; criminal outcome is conviction. Distractors mix these.
  - HR scenario points to administrative, not criminal, investigation.

## 5.15.2 Types of computer forensics

- Computer forensics
  - Definition: Methods to gather, process, interpret, and use digital evidence.
  - Use: Confirms an incident occurred and supports judicial proceedings.
  - Audit impact: Evidence is valid only if shown original and untampered.
- IS auditor may be asked to give expert opinion or check interpretation.
- Database forensics
  - Definition: Retrieving and examining database data and its metadata.
  - Audit impact: Investigator needs unrestricted access to the databases.
- Email forensics
  - Definition: Retrieving and analyzing messages, contacts, and schedules.
  - Audit impact: Needs access to threat actor and victim organization email.
- Mobile forensics
  - Definition: Examining phones for messages, photos, videos, and contacts.
  - Audit impact: The device may need seizing from the suspect.
- Memory forensics
  - Definition: Examining data in memory and cache, called data in process.
  - Use: Done through live analysis.
- Network forensics
  - Definition: Monitoring and recording network traffic and events.
  - Use: Finds the source and path of network attacks.
- Malware forensics
  - Definition: Examining program code and payloads of viruses, ransomware, and Trojans.
  - Use: Identifies abnormal behavior showing malware presence.
- Exam traps
  - Memory forensics pairs with live analysis, since RAM data vanishes at shutdown.

## 5.15.3 Phases of computer forensics

- Phases are followed regardless of forensics type; auditor verifies they are followed. ⭐
- 1. First response
  - Definition: Team actions right after an incident is reported.
  - Use: Actions depend on the nature of the incident.
- 2. Search and seizure
  - Definition: Searching devices involved and taking them into custody.
  - Use: Collects evidence and stops suspects continuing attacks.
- 3. Evidence collection
  - Definition: Gathering data using specified forensic handling methods.
  - Use: Builds the body of evidence for the incident.
- 4. Evidence security
  - Definition: Storing evidence safely to reduce contamination.
  - Audit impact: Secure storage lets evidence be authenticated as accurate.
- 5. Data acquisition
  - Definition: Retrieving electronically stored information (ESI) from seized devices.
  - Audit impact: Wrong procedure alters data and harms integrity.
- 6. Data analysis
  - Definition: Examining authenticated ESI using extraction, processing, modeling, and interpretation.
  - Use: Turns raw data into evidence suitable for court.
- 7. Evidence assessment
  - Definition: Relating gathered evidence directly to the reported incident.
- 8. Documentation and reporting
  - Definition: Post-investigation recording per applicable legal requirements.
- 9. Expert witness testimony
  - Definition: A related-field professional affirms the data is valid evidence.
- Exam traps
  - Order matters: secure and acquire before analysis; analysis never comes first.
  - Security of evidence precedes acquisition in the manual's list.

## 5.15.4 Audit considerations

### Data protection
- Definition: Measures stopping sought-after information being changed or destroyed.
- Use: Protocols tell parties electronic evidence will be sought.
- Audit impact: Incident handling infrastructure must exist before an incident.

### Data acquisition
- Definition: Moving all needed data and media to a controlled location.
- Use: Covers fixed disks and removable media.
- Write-blocker
  - Definition: Device giving read-only access to media under examination.
  - Use: Confirms each device is write-protected before copying.
  - Audit impact: No write-blocker means original data may be altered.
- Recorded statements from witnesses are also a source of information.
- Volatile data
  - Definition: Data lost at shutdown: open ports, files, processes, logons, RAM.
  - Use: Shows what is currently happening on the system.
  - Audit impact: Capture it first; powering off destroys it.

### Imaging
- Definition: Bit-for-bit, sector-by-sector copy of a disk.
- Use: Lets multiple analyses run without damaging the original.
- Captures residual data: deleted files and their fragments.
- Tools can sometimes recover data even after reformatting.
- Exam traps
  - A file copy is not an image; only bit-for-bit captures deleted fragments.

### Extraction
- Definition: Identifying and selecting data from the imaged set.
- Use: Pulls system, firewall, and IDS logs, audit trails, network information.
- Audit impact: Must meet standards of quality, integrity, and reliability.

### Interrogation
- Definition: Mining extracted data for prior indicators and relationships.
- Use: Finds phone numbers, IP addresses, and names of people.

### Ingestion / normalization
- Definition: Converting extracted data into a form investigators can read.
- Use: Turns hexadecimal or binary into readable characters.
- Use: Fusion, correlation, graphing, mapping, and time lining build the hypothesis.

### Reporting
- Two reports: technical for IT, nontechnical for management. ⭐
- Capture dates, times, and pertinent details; keep the company updated.
- Report states why reviewed, how reviewed, and conclusions reached.
- Goals: accurate, understandable, defensible, unambiguous, easy to reference.
- Goals: complete enough to explain conclusions; valid; timely.
- Report also notes organization, circulation restrictions, and auditor reservations.
- Exam traps
  - One technical report for everyone is wrong; audiences differ.
  - Report must include conclusions and reservations, not only raw findings.

## 5.15.5 Computer forensic techniques

- Investigators work on copies and also check hidden files and unallocated space.
- Findings are documented and verified against original device information.
- Deleted file recovery (file carving, data carving)
  - Definition: Searching for fragments of deleted files and rebuilding them.
  - Use: Recovers files deleted by insiders, attackers, or malware.
- Reverse steganography
  - Definition: Comparing hashes of file contents to spot hidden changes.
  - Use: Finds data hidden inside images or other files.
  - Audit impact: A changed hash shows the file was manipulated.
- Cross-drive analysis
  - Definition: Correlating and cross-referencing data across multiple drives.
  - Use: Detects anomalies and gives investigation context.
  - Also called anomaly detection.
- Live analysis
  - Definition: Analyzing volatile data (RAM, cache) while the system runs.
  - Use: Identifies causes of abnormal system traffic.
  - Audit impact: Lab extraction is often preferred to maintain chain of custody.
- Stochastic forensics
  - Definition: Reconstructing past activity without relying on digital artifacts.
  - Use: Suits insider breaches, as insiders rarely leave artifacts.
  - Example: Changed file attributes hinting at data theft.
- Exam traps
  - Insider case with no artifacts points to stochastic forensics.
  - Hash comparison points to reverse steganography, not encryption analysis.
  - Deleted file recovery is the same thing as file or data carving.

## 5.15.6 Computer forensics tools

- Acquisition tools
  - Definition: Tools that copy evidence from storage devices.
  - Use: Create mirror-image, bit-stream backup files of drives.
  - Audit impact: Must be trusted tools, since evidence may reach court.
  - ⚠ Check: Manual names SafeBank as example; outline omits it. ➕ Verify tool name.
- Digital evidence bags (DEBs)
  - Definition: Container for digital evidence that preserves chain of custody.
  - Use: Reliable storage for digital evidence until needed.
  - Tag file: metadata about the investigation and chain of custody.
  - Index file: data source, format, and capture device.
  - Bag file: the actual evidence, as bit streams or logical files.
  - ⚠ Check: Manual calls a DEB a plastic bag, odd for digital data.
- Analysis tools
  - Definition: Tools that examine a bit-stream image for clues.
  - Use: Start with basic file listing and documentation packages.
  - Suspects use TrueCrypt (encryption) and Hide and Seek (steganography).
  - Audit impact: Expect hidden, encrypted, or deleted data.
- File recovery tools
  - Definition: Tools that restore deleted files from free space.
  - Use: Review file traits, last viewed time, and deletion time.
  - Use: Then a tool such as EnCase opens suspicious files.
  - Audit impact: Deleted files remain in free space until overwritten.
- Exam traps
  - Evidence tools must be trusted and proven, not merely convenient or fast.

## 5.15.7 Chain of custody

- Chain of custody ⭐📓
  - Definition: Detailed record of evidence handling, ownership, transfer, and modification.
  - Use: Shows evidence integrity to meet legal requirements.
  - Audit impact: Gaps in records can make evidence inadmissible.
- Rebooting or opening files can lose, corrupt, or overwrite evidence.
- First step: copy one or more images of the attacked system.
- Dump memory to a file before rebooting.
- Analyze images and copies, never the original system. ⭐
- Chain records who had access, in chronological order.
- Chain records procedures followed, such as disk duplication or memory dump.
- Chain records proof copies match original, using checksums, timestamps, or documents.
- Ad hoc incident handling destroys the value of evidence.
- Use industry-specified good practices, proven tools, and due diligence.
- Example: Booting a suspect PC writes to the drive; the auditor cannot deny it.
- Specialist tools make a true copy, which is then used for investigation.
- Exam traps
  - Image first, then analyze the copy; never boot the suspect system.
  - Chain of custody tracks people and handling, not just file contents.
  - Checksums prove copies are identical; passwords or encryption do not.

## 5.15.8 Best practices to secure digital evidence

- Prevent contamination
  - Definition: Keep crime scene and data unmodified.
  - Use: Others can repeat analysis and reach same conclusions.
  - Use: Image memory and storage; work only on the bit-for-bit copy.
  - Audit impact: Working on originals deletes or contaminates metadata.
- Have a clear chain of custody
  - Use: Audit logs show who accessed evidence, when, and what they did.
  - Audit impact: Verify transfers between every person and agency.
- Implement tamper detection
  - Definition: Hash values taken at imaging act as an integrity fingerprint.
  - Use: Any change produces a new, non-matching hash.
- Secure storage devices
  - Use: Logon passwords and password-protected screen savers guard the device.
  - Use: Consider offsite storage for long-term evidence management.
- Keep mobile devices digitally isolated
  - Use: Isolation chamber, no Wi-Fi or wired links, airplane mode.
  - Use: Faraday bag blocks signals; the manual lists antistatic paper packaging. ➕
  - Audit impact: Prevents remote wipes or changes to phone data.
  - ⚠ Check: Manual says turn phone off, yet also says keep power state unchanged.
- Encrypt the hard drive
  - Use: Extra protection if passwords seem insufficient.
  - ⚠ Check: Manual says full disk encryption is not always the best option.
- Involve forensic experts
  - Use: Staff know when to stop and let experts take over.
- Avoid changing device power status
  - Use: Leave devices on or off as found.
  - Use: Remove batteries from powered-off devices to stop auto-restart timers.
- Monitor evidence transactions
  - Use: Record each sign-out of evidence for reporting or legal consultation.
  - Audit impact: IS security staff may need to manage evidence.
- Install write-blocking software
  - Use: Gives read-only access so examined media cannot change.
  - ⚠ Check: Acquisition section says write-blocker is a device; here it is software.
- Incorporate other evidence
  - Use: Email headers, chat, websites, and message timestamps add context.
  - Audit impact: Evidence extends beyond the seized hardware.
- Exam traps
  - Hash mismatch signals tampering; matching hashes support integrity claims.
  - Leave devices in their current power state; do not power-cycle for convenience.

## Case study (stem summary)

- Company: Spectertainment, a jazz video-clip producer and distributor.
- Staff use laptops and tablets and work remotely, up to two days weekly.
- Remote work raised productivity and morale.
- Staff trained on security procedures; written procedures also exist.
- Access uses logon ID and password to the application server over VPN.
- Security administrator assigns initial passwords; first logon forces a change.
- Management wants better remote access protection; the IS auditor reviews the new VPN.
- Auditor finds many employees using personal devices on the VPN.
- Remote access policy exists but sets no personal-device requirements.
- Questions cover VPN concern, forgotten passwords, personal-device policy, and benefits of allowing them.

## Takeaway

- Preserve first: leave evidence unaltered and involve specialists early.
- Image bit-for-bit, analyze copies, and prove integrity with hashes.
- Chain of custody records who, what, when; gaps destroy admissibility.
- Match proof standard to investigation type: criminal versus civil.
- Capture volatile data before shutdown; use write-blockers on all media.
