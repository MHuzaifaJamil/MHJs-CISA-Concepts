## 5.2.0 Basics and ownership

- Physical controls: barriers that stop people reaching assets.
- Environmental controls: protect equipment from heat, humidity, power problems, water and fire.
- Facility management often designs and runs these controls.
- IT and the security manager are often not the owners.
- Audit impact: auditor must still assess them and give assurance.
- Typical environmental needs: temperature, humidity and power.
- IPF (information processing facility): the room or building housing IT equipment.
- Audit impact: IPF is the main target of every check below.

## 5.2.1 Environmental exposures and controls

### Equipment issues and exposures

- Definition: environmental exposure is damage to IT from nature or surroundings.
- Causes: lightning, earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, hurricanes, tornados, extreme weather.
- Audit impact: these threaten availability first.

#### Power failure categories

- Four categories, based on duration and severity.
- Total failure (blackout): complete loss of electrical power.
- Blackout may hit one building or a whole region.
- Blackout causes: storms, earthquakes, or utility unable to meet demand.
- Severely reduced voltage (brownout): utility supplies power outside acceptable range.
- Manual cites 108-125 volts AC for the United States.
- ⚠ Check: that voltage range is US-specific; do not memorize it as universal.
- Brownout strains equipment and can shorten life or cause permanent damage.
- Sag: short, rapid voltage drop.
- Spike or surge: short, rapid voltage rise.
- Sags, spikes and surges cause data loss, corruption, network errors and hardware damage.
- EMI (electromagnetic interference): noise from storms or electrical equipment.
- EMI sources: motors, fluorescent lighting, radio transmitters.
- EMI can hang or crash systems, with damage like sags and surges.
- Example: a nearby motor causes random server crashes; shielding or relocation fixes it.

#### Matching interruption length to control

- Short-term interruptions (millionths to thousandths of a second): surge protectors.
- Intermediate-term (seconds to 30 minutes): UPS (uninterruptible power supply).
- Long-term (hours to days): alternative generators.
- Generators may be portable or built into the building.
- Generator fuels: diesel, gasoline, propane.
- Audit impact: check the control matches the expected outage length.
- Example: UPS alone cannot cover a two-day outage; a generator is needed.

#### Water and other causes

- Water damage and flooding occur even on upper floors.
- Common cause: broken water pipes.
- Other causes: terrorist threats, vandalism, electrical shock, equipment failure.

#### Auditor questions on the environment

- Is power within manufacturer specifications?
- Are air conditioning, humidity and ventilation adequate?
- Is equipment protected from static (antistatic rug or spray)?
- Is equipment kept free of dust, smoke and particles?
- Are food, drink and tobacco prohibited near equipment?
- Are backup media protected from temperature extremes, magnetism and water?
- Is equipment stored safely to prevent theft?
- Is maintenance scheduled and followed?
- Are hardware failures addressed promptly?

### Controls for environmental exposures

- Environmental exposures deserve the same protection level as physical and logical ones.
- Co-located, outsourced and cloud datacenters: auditor evaluates all hosted-environment exposures.
- Audit impact: you cannot assume the provider covers environmental risk.

#### Alarm control panels

- Definition: central unit that receives and manages fire and related alarms.
- Keep it separate from burglar or security systems.
- Fire department must reach it at all times.
- Place it in a weatherproof box.
- Place it in a controlled room, blocking unauthorized access.
- Give it a dedicated, separate power circuit.
- It should control or disable separate zones.
- Manual also says it should follow manufacturer temperature limits and local regulations.

#### Water detectors

- Definition: sensors that alarm when water is present.
- Place under raised floors and near drain holes.
- Needed even on high floors, because of leaks.
- Unattended equipment storage also needs them.
- Alarm must be audible to security and control staff.
- Mark detector locations on the raised floor.
- Responders should be named; other staff warned about electric shock risk.

#### Smoke detectors

- Definition: sensors that alarm on smoke, giving early fire warning.
- Install above and below ceiling tiles, and below raised floor.
- Alarm must be audible and linked to a monitored station.
- Marked locations help quick access.
- Smoke detectors supplement, never replace, fire suppression.
- Auditor visually verifies detector presence and power sufficiency, especially battery units.

#### Extinguishers and manual alarms

- Handheld fire extinguishers: strategic locations, tagged, inspected at least annually.
- Manual fire alarms: near exit doors, protecting personnel safety.
- Manual alarm links to a monitored guard station.

#### Fire suppression systems

- Definition: automatic systems that release an agent when heat is detected.
- Use: put out fire before staff or the fire department arrive.
- They sound an audible alarm and link to a monitored guard station.
- Inspect and test annually; follow industry and insurance standards.
- Ideal triggers: close fire doors, notify fire department, close ducts, cut nonessential power.
- Segment systems so one zone does not discharge everywhere.
- Total flooding: agent fills an enclosed three-dimensional space to target concentration.
- Local application: agent hits the fire directly, usually a two-dimensional area.
- Key difference: local application has no enclosing barriers around the fire space.
- Water-based sprinkler: water always sits in pipes.
- Audit impact: wet pipes can leak and damage equipment.
- Dry-pipe sprinkler: water flows only after the alarm activates a pump.
- Dry-pipe lowers accidental leak risk in the computer room.
- FM-200 (HFC-227ea): gas agent often considered the preferred option.
- Novec 1230: very clean agent, mostly an FM-200 alternative.
- Auditor may review test documentation, not trigger the system.
- Reason: testing is expensive; confirm inspection within the last year.
- ⚠ Check: manual does not name halon; do not assume it appears here.
- Exam traps:
- Smoke detectors do not replace suppression; they only give warning.
- Dry-pipe beats wet-pipe where water leaks would damage equipment.
- Auditor reviews test records; discharging the system is not expected.

#### Computer room location

- Avoid basement and top floor, to reduce flooding risk.
- Middle floors are best (for example third to sixth).
- Avoid adjacent water or gas pipes, except fire suppression piping.
- Avoid neighbors such as paper storage.
- Avoid sites near airports or chemical plants with explosive gases.
- If already in a basement, a plastic sheet can divert water.
- That is a cheaper alternative to costly relocation.

#### Fire department and emergency power-off

- Fire department inspects fire detection systems annually, for building-code compliance.
- Tell the fire department where the computer room is.
- Reason: it can bring equipment suited to electrical fires.
- Auditor asks whether an inspector or insurer visited, and obtains the report.
- Emergency power-off switch: instantly cuts power to computers and peripherals.
- Use two switches: one inside the room, one near it outside.
- Labels clear, easily accessible, but secured from unauthorized people.
- Shield against accidental activation.
- Consider video surveillance as a dissuasive control against malicious insiders.

#### Building and power protections

- Fireproof walls, floors and ceilings: true floor to true ceiling.
- Minimum two-hour fire resistance rating.
- Auditor locates wall fire-rating documents with building management.
- Electrical surge protectors reduce damage from power spikes.
- Voltage regulators keep current consistent.
- Both are typically built into the UPS.
- Auditor visually observes surge protectors on expensive equipment.
- UPS: battery or gasoline-powered generator between utility power and computer.
- UPS cleanses power so voltage stays consistent.
- UPS bridges outages for orderly shutdown, from minutes to days depending on design.
- Power leads from two substations give redundant power lines.
- Reason: the utility lines face water, fire, lightning and digging damage.
- This risk is mostly beyond the organization's control, so redundancy is the answer.
- Wiring belongs in fire-resistant panels and conduit.
- Conduit generally runs under the fire-resistant raised floor.

#### Housekeeping and people controls

- Food, drink and tobacco are banned from the IPF.
- Reason: fire, contaminants and liquid damage.
- Ban should be overt, such as an entrance sign.
- Office materials in the IPF must be fire-resistant.
- Cleaning fluids must be nonflammable.
- Emergency evacuation plans must be documented and tested.
- Plans put human safety first.
- Plans must not leave the IPF physically insecure.
- Controlled shutdown procedure is used if time permits.
- Auditor interviews a sample of IS staff on plan awareness.
- Plans should be posted throughout the facility.
- Humidity and temperature control: visit the IPF regularly to confirm adequacy.
- Exam traps:
- Evacuation: human life wins over protecting equipment.
- Redundant lines from two substations answer external power-line exposure.

## 5.2.2 Physical access exposures and controls

### Physical access exposures

- Definition: harm from accidental or intentional breach of physical access paths.
- Impact: financial loss, legal repercussions, lost credibility, lost competitive edge.
- Exposures: unauthorized entry.
- Exposures: vandalism, damage or theft of equipment or documents.
- Exposures: copying or viewing sensitive or copyrighted information.
- Exposures: alteration of sensitive equipment and information.
- Exposures: public disclosure of sensitive information.
- Exposures: abuse of data processing resources.
- Exposures: blackmail and embezzlement.
- Exposures: wiretapping or eavesdropping.

#### Possible perpetrators

- Insiders: disgruntled, on strike, facing discipline or dismissal, or notified of termination.
- Insiders with addiction, financial problems or emotional problems.
- Former employees.
- Outsiders: competitors, thieves, organized crime, hackers.
- Accidental, non-malicious actors who violate unknowingly.
- Most likely source: the uninformed, accidental person.
- Greatest impact: those with malicious or fraudulent intent.
- Exam traps:
- Likelihood and impact point to different actors; read which one is asked.

#### Facilities to protect

- Computer rooms, tape libraries, storage rooms and supplies.
- Offsite backup storage and communications closets.
- Operator consoles, programming areas, input/output control rooms.
- Portable equipment, printers (onsite and remote), LANs.
- Power sources and disposal sites.
- Safeguards must reach every vulnerable access point, including boundaries with external organizations.
- That includes remote, rented, leased and shared facilities.
- Auditor may need assurance that service providers and third parties have similar controls.
- Other questions: forced-entry protection, key control, terminals locked, equipment passes required.
- Other questions: are co-locations and external data centers audited regularly?

### Physical access controls

- Definition: measures limiting entry to people management specifically authorized.
- Authorization may be explicit (key issued) or implicit (job description).
- Hidden cameras are detective; visible cameras and signs deter. 📓
- Example: concealed cameras with no notices record intruders but prevent nothing.

#### Locks

- Bolting lock: needs a traditional metal key.
- Stamp key "do not duplicate"; store and issue under strict control.
- Combination (cipher) lock: numeric keypad or dial.
- Change combination regularly, and on transfer, firing or discipline.
- Reason: former holders may still know the code.
- Electronic door lock: card, token or biometric read by a sensor.
- Advantage: card assignable to an identifiable individual.
- Advantage: restrict by particular door or hour of day.
- Advantage: hard to duplicate.
- Advantage: easy to deactivate when lost, stolen or on termination.
- Advantage: silent or audible alarm on unauthorized attempts.
- Biometric door lock: uses a body trait; details are in section 5.7.16.
- Audit impact: electronic locks give individual accountability that shared keys cannot.

#### Logging, badges and guards

- Manual logging: visitor signs name, company, reason, person seen, entry and exit times.
- Verify identification, such as a driver's license or vendor tag.
- Log at front reception and the computer room entrance.
- Electronic logging: record all access, highlighting unsuccessful attempts.
- Identification badges: worn and displayed by all personnel.
- Visitor badges differ in color from employee badges.
- Photo IDs can double as electronic card keys.
- Issuing and retrieving badges is a tightly controlled administrative process.
- Video cameras: placed at strategic points and monitored by guards.
- Retain recordings for playback.
- Resolution must allow enlarging images to identify an intruder.
- Security guards: valuable with cameras and locked doors.
- Guards from external agencies should be bonded.
- Controlled visitor access: escort all visitors with a responsible employee.
- Visitors include friends, maintenance staff, vendors, consultants and external auditors.
- Lighting: criminals favor dark areas, so light premises well.
- Good lighting also improves video surveillance.

#### Safety and bonding

- Human safety takes precedence in all information security management.
- Auditor validates emergency procedures, disaster preparedness and evacuation procedures.
- Bonded service personnel (cleaners, offsite storage) limit financial exposure only.
- Bonding does not improve physical security.
- Exam traps:
- Bonding is financial protection, not a preventive physical control.
- When safety conflicts with security, safety wins.

#### Entry design

- Mantrap or airlock (access control vestibule): two doors, one person inside.
- Second door opens only after the first closes and locks.
- Use: stop piggybacking (following an authorized person through a door).
- Full-height turnstile can achieve a similar effect.
- Deadman doors: outer door must close before inner opens.
- Use: delivery and dispatch areas.
- Computer workstation locks: secure device to desk or block power or keyboard.
- Key or card locks suit high-security workstations, such as payroll.
- Use a controlled single entry point with a receptionist.
- Multiple entry points raise unauthorized entry risk.
- Eliminate unnecessary doors, such as smoking-area exits.
- Emergency exits can use alarmed panic bars.
- Alarm inactive entry points, motion detectors and reverse flow of one-way doors.
- Guards must be able to hear the alarm.
- Secured document and mail carts: covered, locked, never unattended.
- Computer rooms should not be visible or identifiable from outside.
- No windows or directional signs; directory shows only a general location.
- Any windows: reinforced glass, plus bars if on the ground floor.
- Exam traps:
- Mantrap answers piggybacking; badges alone do not.
- Computer room labeled with signs is a finding, not good practice.

### Auditing physical access

- Tour the computer site and offsite storage first.
- Tour gives overall perception and starts review of restrictions.
- Third-party sites may need a contractual right to audit.
- Much testing is visual observation.
- Documents: evacuation procedures, inspection tags, suppression test results, key lock logs.
- Test beyond the computer room: operator consoles, printer rooms, storage rooms.
- Also test UPS and generator, communications equipment, media storage, offsite backup.
- Look above ceiling panels and below raised floor.
- Check smoke and water detectors and general cleanliness.
- Check walls reach the real ceiling, not just the suspended one.
- Ground-floor rooms: walk outside, view windows and examine emergency doors for routine use.
- Evidence of misuse: cigarette stubs or litter at emergency doors.
- Also check air conditioning units and nearby flammable or dangerous storage.
- Entry paths to evaluate: entry doors, emergency doors, glass windows and walls.
- Entry paths: movable walls and modular cubicles.
- Entry paths: above suspended ceilings, beneath raised floors, ventilation systems.
- Entry paths: behind curtains or fake walls.
- Example: intruder crawls over a partition that stops at the false ceiling.

## 5.2.3 Industrial Control Systems (ICS) security

- Definition: ICSs manage infrastructure functions such as water, power and transportation.
- Use: run physical processes automatically and remotely.
- Audit impact: failures can cause physical harm, not only data loss.
- SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) systems: central monitoring and control of remote sites.
- PLC (programmable logic controller): controls a machine or process locally.
- RTU (remote terminal unit): remote field device reporting back to SCADA.
- IoT and smart devices add new risk factors to ICS.

### ICS risk

- Poor security posture: planning focuses on physical access only.
- Less thought goes to components, applications, networking equipment and telecom lines.
- Improper input validation: bad input can give attackers unintended functions or privileges.
- Improper authentication: most remote ICS elements do not authenticate issued commands.
- Result: illegitimate commands get executed.
- Third-party remote access needs trust, but the third party may be lax.
- Poor configuration and maintenance: weak patching, flaws, misconfiguration.
- Embedded software: Internet-connected controllers can become attack vectors.
- Network design weaknesses: little defense in depth, no zones, little port security.
- Technological depreciation cycle: aging devices with limited processing and memory.
- Impact: they cannot run strong cryptographic modules.
- Incompatibility with information security standards: office-designed standards, such as ISO/IEC 27001, may not fit.
- Weak protocols: designed in proprietary era and often leave message content unprotected.
- Weak cryptography libraries and inadequate encryption strength are common.
- Dependencies on ICT systems: ICT upgrades or restarts can destabilize ICS.
- Poorly maintained emergency generators and batteries often fail when needed.
- Third parties on site: engineers and integrators often go unmonitored.
- Impact: uncontrolled modifications to the ICS environment.
- Compliance risk: noncompliance with laws can bring breaches, penalties and fines.
- ⚠ Check: manual calls ISO 27001 a threat only where it misfits ICS.
- Example: a vendor laptop plugged into an engineering station changes control logic unnoticed.

### ICS security best practices

- Conduct an ICS asset inventory, then classify assets by criticality.
- Reason: you cannot protect assets you cannot see.
- Monitor ICS network baselines and investigate deviations.
- Segment ICS networks using firewalls that understand ICS protocols.
- Manual says air gaps used to protect ICS, but that practice was abandoned.
- Segmentation stops general IS attacks reaching ICS.
- Apply least privilege (POLP), even where ICSs lack access controls.
- Benefit: damage stays limited if access is gained.
- Deploy IDS/IPS to alert on threats and respond to malware.
- Secure remote access, using encryption.
- Secure physical access: guards and badges.
- Implement redundancy.
- Harden systems and networks: block unused ports and patch.
- Develop an incident response plan (IRP).
- Supply chain security: assess vendors for vulnerabilities and malicious code.
- Exam traps:
- ICS risk is typically unauthenticated commands and flat networks, not just physical theft.
- Segmentation, not air gaps, is the current recommended approach.

## Takeaway

- Match power control to outage length: surge protector, UPS, generator.
- Smoke detectors warn; suppression systems extinguish; both are needed.
- Mantrap stops piggybacking; bonding limits only financial loss.
- Human safety always outranks equipment protection and security.
- Auditors observe, tour and review test records rather than trigger systems.
- ICS risks: weak authentication, flat networks, aging devices; fix with segmentation.
