## 5.9 Overview

- Portable devices operate where physical controls are weak or absent.
- Definition: Mobile device means any device moved during normal use.
- Use: Gives staff flexibility to work anywhere, anytime.
- Audit impact: Mobility makes logical and physical access controls harder to implement.
- Mobile devices need encryption and strong authentication.
- Some data is too sensitive to store on mobile devices.
- Audit impact: Auditor should confirm data classification limits what may be stored.
- Devices and media can also be used to steal data and programs.

## 5.9.1 Mobile computing

- Definition: Mobile computing means using portable devices that travel outside enterprise boundaries.
- Examples: tablets, smartphones, laptops, USB storage, digital cameras.
- Use: Enables work away from the office network.
- Audit impact: Each device is an endpoint outside normal perimeter protection.

### Vulnerabilities and risk (Figure 5.48)

- Wireless networks are less secure, so information can be intercepted.
- Mobility crosses enterprise boundaries, so malware can propagate back inside.
- Bluetooth left on and discoverable invites unwanted connections.
- Stored information is often unencrypted.
- Lost or stolen devices may have no backup.
- Some devices have no authentication requirement at all.
- Unmanaged devices and no mobile strategy leave gaps in control.
- Unsigned third-party applications may carry malicious code.
- Example: A salesperson loses an unencrypted laptop holding customer lists.
- Exam traps:
  - Mobility itself is the root cause; unsupported controls are the symptom.
  - Missing mobile strategy is a governance gap, not a technical flaw.

## 5.9.2 Mobile device threats

- Definition: Threat means an event that exploits a mobile weakness.
- Information interception: data sniffed over the air.
- Malware propagation: infected device spreads malware to the enterprise network.
- Lost business: device loss or outage stops staff from working.
- Data leakage: lost or stolen device exposes stored data.
- Open public Wi-Fi: untrusted networks enable eavesdropping and man-in-the-middle attacks.
- Outdated devices: unpatched hardware and software hold known vulnerabilities.
- Identity theft: attackers harvest personal data from devices.
- Drive-by downloads: malware installs silently when users visit hostile sites.
- Audit impact: Map each threat to a specific preventive or detective control.
- Exam traps:
  - Identify the attack method first, then choose the matching control.

## 5.9.3 Mobile device controls

- Definition: Controls are safeguards that reduce mobile risk.
- Use: Many can be enforced centrally through MDM (see 5.9.4).
- Audit impact: Test each control for design and operation.

### Technical and physical controls

- Device registration: only authorized, registered devices access company resources.
- Physical security: cable locks, and covered cameras in sensitive areas.
- Bluetooth disabled when unused; airplane mode may not disable it.
- Wi-Fi: avoid connecting to public networks.
- Software updates keep operating systems and apps patched.
- Location services disabled when not needed.
- Strong lock-screen passwords and auto-lock after inactivity.
- Install minimal applications, only from official stores.
- Store minimal data on the device and back up regularly.
- Virus detection and control, with antivirus kept updated.
- Compliance with enterprise standards, including multifactor authentication (MFA).
- Secure transmission over VPN to the enterprise network.
- Network authentication, authorization and accounting tie devices to user identity and role.
- Audit impact: Role-based access lets IT track and monitor how devices are used.

### Administrative controls

- Approval: mobile device use must be formally authorized.
- Acceptable use policy defines permitted information, devices and services.
- Due care: staff protect devices, especially while traveling.
- Report any loss or theft immediately as a security breach.
- Awareness training covers policy, unknown links, attachments and trusted accessories.
- Example: Employee reports a lost phone within the hour, enabling remote wipe.
- Exam traps:
  - Lost device treated as a security breach, not a minor hardware loss.
  - Policy and training are preventive; neither replaces technical enforcement.

## 5.9.4 Mobile Device Management (MDM)

- Definition: MDM is a tool suite securing and managing devices, apps, data, access.
- Use: Manages the many device types employees use for company resources.
- Objectives: security, monitoring, remote management, troubleshooting.
- Audit impact: Centralized MDM coverage shows devices are managed, not rogue.

### Basic MDM features

- Encryption protects stored data (see 5.6).
- Remote wiping deletes all device data over phone service or Internet.
- ⚠ Check: Remote wipe does not permanently remove data; recovery tools may restore it.
- Audit impact: Never accept remote wipe alone as proof of data protection.
- Device lockout disables a device after repeated failed credential attempts.
- Sophisticated lockout is persistent and needs a different account or master code.
- Assets management uses GPS to track devices and personnel movements.
- GPS tracking needs Internet or phone service to report location.
- Application control limits installation to approved, work-related apps.
- Use: Reduces exposure to malicious apps from unknown sources.
- Device troubleshooting lets staff fix problems remotely from the console.
- Credential management centrally stores credentials behind a master credential, preferably MFA.
- Secure remote support lets help desks configure, chat, transfer files and control devices.
- Keep remote support logs behind the enterprise firewall.
- Standard mobile device applications: only approved, licensed, IT-installed software is authorized.

### Best practices for MDM

- Craft the implementation policy before deploying the MDM solution.
- Simplify device enrollment so all devices actually get enrolled.
- Establish self-service: remote wipe, password reset, lost-device tracking.
- Enforce updated MDM versions so configuration pushes and patches work.
- Protect end-user privacy; collect minimal data for business purposes only.
- Deploy containers to separate corporate apps and data from personal use.
- Perform data backup before deployment and throughout use.
- Train employees regularly, including at onboarding and after MDM changes.
- Exam traps:
  - Policy comes before the tool; deploying MDM first is wrong.
  - Containers let MDM rules apply only to work activity.

## 5.9.5 Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) 📓⭐

- Definition: BYOD lets employees use personal devices for work and network access.
- Use: Delivers productivity, innovation, better morale and cost savings.
- Audit impact: BYOD risk mirrors mobile risk, plus ownership and commingling issues.

### Security and control issues

- Protect sensitive data and intellectual property.
- Protect the networks that BYOD devices connect to.
- Define responsibility and accountability for device and stored information.
- Remove organizational data on termination of employment or device loss.
- Provide malware protection on personal devices.

### BYOD-specific risk

- Access controls and device security controls are harder to enforce.
- Eliminating enterprise data on exit or loss is difficult.
- Supporting many device types, operating systems and applications strains IT.
- Employee-owned devices must be backed up reliably.

### BYOD agreement and approval

- Require a BYOD agreement or acceptable use agreement (AUA) before use.
- Agreement may allow device seizure for legal matters.
- AUA makes security a shared duty of user and IT.
- Executive management must approve BYOD, with oversight and monitoring.

### BYOD best practices

- Establish data ownership, since personal and business data are commingled.
- Example: Remote wipe after theft may also erase the owner's personal photos.
- Obtain user acceptance after training, and state consequences of non-compliance.
- Enforce adherence: personal devices follow security policies on and off premises.
- Support device ownership: define what support the organization provides.
- Define patch management: who installs updates, and whether the organization tests them.
- Manage antivirus: state required products and settings.
- Consider infrastructure: more wireless devices need capacity planning.
- Have an exit BYOD strategy to prevent backdoor access by departing staff.

### Threat defines the control 📓

- Questions target one vulnerability at a time. 📓
- Identify the attacker's method before choosing the defense. 📓
- Physical loss or theft maps to full disk encryption (data at rest). 📓
- Casual access to an unattended device maps to passwords, screen lock, biometrics. 📓
- Personal malware near corporate mail maps to containerization or MDM. 📓
- Public Wi-Fi interception maps to VPN or data-in-transit encryption. 📓
- Exam traps:
  - Passwords alone fail against theft because attackers can extract the drive.
  - Encryption does not stop a coworker using an unlocked, unattended device.
  - VPN does not protect stored data on a stolen device.
  - Containerization separates data; it does not encrypt traffic on public Wi-Fi.
  - Weakest BYOD point is usually missing data-ownership and exit rules.

## 5.9.6 Internet access on mobile devices

- Definition: Mobile devices reach the Internet through WLANs or mobile networks.
- 4G is an IP packet-switched network with higher speed and video capability.
- 5G offers enhanced coverage, low latency and ultra-high data rates.
- Use: Browser-based applications now deliver much mobile content.

### Exposures

- Interception: information travels through the air and may be captured.
- Loss or theft: small devices are easy to lose.
- Loss of data on devices: weak or absent encryption leaves only a PIN.
- Misuse of devices to gather or intercept information.
- Distractions, for example use while driving, can cause accidents.
- OS vulnerabilities allow access, and enable jailbreaking.
- Definition: Jailbreaking intentionally removes vendor restrictions from a device.
- Audit impact: Jailbroken devices are more vulnerable and may install unsafe apps.
- Application vulnerabilities or malicious code expose data and device.
- Weak wireless user authentication at the device level.
- Weak file security: mobile platforms lack mature file access controls.
- Exam traps:
  - Jailbreaking raises risk because apps may not come from secure sources.

## 5.9.7 Audit procedures for mobile devices

- Mobile devices are endpoints, so procedures resemble endpoint and BYOD audits.
- Use: Regular audits catch vulnerabilities before they spread to other systems.

### Audit areas

- Governance: policy exists and protective features are enabled.
- Governance: policy bans carrying devices into sensitive areas.
- Governance: risk assessments and periodic audits have been performed.
- Patch management: device software, antivirus and applications are current.
- Patch management: patches are tested and approved before rollout.
- Configuration and change management: gateways run the latest approved software.
- Configuration and change management: Bluetooth disabled when unused, OS patched.
- Device management: no connection to unknown removable media.
- Device management: no unmanaged devices on the network.
- Device management: remote wipe or lock enabled; lifecycle controls exist.
- Device management: cameras covered; location services off when unneeded.
- BYOD: policy exists, controls manage personal devices, app installation procedures exist.
- Security monitoring: asset management, log reviews, download rules, central MDM.
- DR/BC: processes restore devices and continue operations, tested regularly.
- Awareness training: users know not to open unknown links or attachments.
- Encryption: data secure at rest and in transit, using TLS or VPN.
- Exam traps:
  - Best first evidence is an approved policy plus an inventory of devices.
  - Auditor verifies controls operate; auditor does not configure devices.

## 5.9.8 Mobile payment systems

- Definition: Mobile payment is payment made through a mobile device.
- Use: Links to credit cards and bank accounts.
- Audit impact: Security features need continual enhancement as threats evolve.

### Types of mobile payment systems

- Digital wallets (e-wallets) store value, debit, credit and personal information.
- Mobile wallets replace cards; examples are Apple Pay and Samsung Pay.
- Mobile wallets are tied to specific device hardware and software combinations.
- Digital currency wallets (cryptowallets) store private keys proving ownership of currency.
- Audit impact: Losing a private key means losing control of the asset.
- Contactless payment uses RFID or near field communication (NFC) chips.

### Mobile payment threats

- Phishing exploits mixed business and social data to trick users.
- Mobile malware arrives via social engineering, insecure hotspots or download attacks.
- Attackers can also infect point-of-sale servers and contactless terminals.
- Spoofing: a fake access point imitates a legitimate network name.
- Unauthorized access: stolen device bypasses PIN or fingerprint, then intercepts transactions.
- Tampering: attacker alters an app, adds a backdoor, and republishes it.
- Application vulnerabilities, especially weak authentication, expose stored data.
- Payment fraud: attacker adds another device to a victim's profile.
- Token data compromise: risk rises with weak issuer token services.
- Identity theft: stolen details open accounts in the victim's name.
- Cloned applications look genuine but have poor security.

### Best practices

- Tokenization replaces card data with random strings that cannot be reversed.
- Example: Intercepted token is worthless to a thief without the original device.
- Device-specific cryptograms prove payment came from the cardholder's own device.
- 3D Secure authenticates cardholders; merchants, networks and banks share transaction data.
- Strong customer authentication (SCA) requires two or more factors.
- Continuous fraud monitoring applies risk-based rules to accept or reject transactions.
- Regular audits find vulnerabilities and drive control recommendations.
- User awareness training counters social engineering.
- Exam traps:
  - Tokenization protects data in transit and storage; it does not prove device identity.
  - Cryptogram binds a payment to the device; token alone cannot do that.

## 5.9.9 Wireless networks

- Definition: Wireless uses radio frequency signals through free space instead of cables.
- Infrared and some Bluetooth-type devices need direct line of sight.
- Use: Supports flexible connectivity and e-business growth.
- Audit impact: Wireless risk equals wired risk plus weaknesses in wireless protocols.
- Other issues: narrow bandwidth, immature standards, unresolved security and privacy concerns.

### Four categories

- WWAN: wireless wide area network.
- WLAN: wireless local area network.
- WPAN: wireless personal area network.
- Ad hoc: wireless networks with shifting topology.

### Wireless wide area networks (WWAN)

- Definition: WWAN links networks across large areas using radio, satellite and mobile phone.
- Common technologies: 4G LTE, growing 5G; GSM third-generation still available.
- Use: Helps rural sites where cabling is too expensive.
- Audit impact: Planning, surveying and total cost of ownership need review.

### Wireless local area networks (WLAN)

- Definition: WLAN connects devices through an access point (AP) instead of wires.
- AP connects to the wired Ethernet LAN through an RJ-45 port.
- AP range is up to about 300 feet (100 meters).
- Coverage area is called a cell; cells can be linked for roaming.

### WEP and Wi-Fi Protected Access

- WEP: static keys, RC4, 40-bit key, CRC-32 integrity; weak.
- Key distribution is hard, so keys stay unchanged and get broken.
- ⚠ Check: Manual calls WEP a "last option"; best answer is no WEP.
- WPA: temporary fix for WEP, uses TKIP with RC4 and MIC.
- WPA-Personal suits home; WPA-Enterprise needs a RADIUS server.
- WPA2: ratified 2004, uses CCMP with AES, integrity via CBC-MAC.
- WPA2 has a key vulnerability, more serious in enterprise networks.
- WPA3: newest; uses SAE for stronger authentication and dictionary-attack resistance.
- WPA3 automatically encrypts connections on public networks without credentials.
- WPA3 mandates protected management frames, countering replay-type attacks.
- WPA and WPA2 use a 4-way handshake; WPA3 uses SAE instead.
- ⚠ Check: Manual table lists WPA3 integrity as SHA, and 198-bit enterprise key.
- ➕ The 198-bit figure is likely OCR error for 192-bit; verify before memorizing.
- Exam traps:
  - Best answer for WLAN encryption: WPA2 with AES at minimum, WPA3 preferred.
  - WPA with TKIP is an interim fix, not a final answer.
  - Minimum acceptable encryption strength is generally 128 bits.

### Wireless personal area networks (WPAN)

- Definition: WPAN is a short-range network linking devices to one another.
- Bluetooth: up to 49 feet (15 meters), frequency hopping, 1 to 2 Mbps.
- Bluetooth is peer-to-peer and sets up background connections automatically.
- Infrared: oldest WPAN method, needs line of sight.
- ZigBee: 250 Kbps, cheaper and uses far less energy than Bluetooth.

### Ad hoc networks

- Definition: Ad hoc networks connect devices dynamically with shifting topologies.
- Use: Mobile routers on wireless links forward data between devices.
- Audit impact: No fixed infrastructure means fewer central controls to audit.
- Bluetooth networks can behave as ad hoc networks.

### Wireless security threats and risk mitigation

- Threat groups: errors and omissions; fraud and theft; employee sabotage.
- Also: loss of physical and infrastructure support; malicious hackers.
- Also: industrial espionage, foreign government espionage, malicious code, privacy threats.
- Security requirements: authenticity, nonrepudiation, accountability, availability.
- Authenticity: third party can verify content was not changed in transit.
- Nonrepudiation: origin or receipt of a message is verifiable by third parties.
- Accountability: actions are uniquely traceable to one entity.
- Bluetooth sniffing tools such as Red Fang and Bluesniff passively capture traffic.
- Non-discoverable mode is not safe if device addresses are known.
- Strong directional antennas extend sniffing range beyond normal Bluetooth range.
- Rogue and unauthorized APs let attackers bypass firewalls.
- Identity theft and masquerading let attackers impersonate legitimate users.
- Attackers can track users' physical movements.
- Example: An employee plugs a cheap AP into a wall jack, bypassing the firewall.
- Exam traps:
  - Rogue AP is an unauthorized device; fix is detection, not stronger encryption.
  - Disabling SSID broadcast is weak security; it only hides, not protects.

### Wireless secure encryption protocols

- 802.1X/EAP: port-based access control; clients communicate only after authentication.
- 802.1X integrates RADIUS, TACACS, certificates, smart cards, tokens for mutual and MFA.
- EAP: framework supporting new authentication methods; LEAP, EAP-TLS, EAP-TTLS.
- ⚠ Check: Not every EAP method is safe; EAP-MD5 has been broken.
- PEAP: wraps EAP inside a TLS tunnel because EAP is typically unencrypted.
- LEAP: insecure and attacked; avoid, or use strong passwords if unavoidable.
- MAC filter: list of authorized client MAC addresses.
- Audit impact: MAC filtering is hard to manage and attackers spoof addresses.
- TKIP: RC4 with key mixing, sequence counter against replay, strong integrity check.
- Audit impact: TKIP and WPA were replaced by WPA2; attacks make them unreliable.
- CCMP: AES with 128-bit key; preferred protocol for 802.11i.
- Exam traps:
  - EAP-TLS and PEAP are acceptable; LEAP and EAP-MD5 are not.
  - MAC filter alone is not authentication; it is only access filtering.

### Auditing procedures for wireless networks

- Governance: approved current wireless policy, regular risk assessments, periodic AP configuration audits.
- Architecture: site survey done before AP deployment.
- Architecture: find and remove rogue APs and unauthorized wireless installations.
- Architecture: APs run latest approved software, physically secured, segmented from LAN.
- Architecture: wireless network not connected to public Wi-Fi.
- Configuration: change management in place; all default AP settings changed.
- Configuration: wireless IDS sensors placed properly.
- Encryption: strongest available, 128 bits minimum, WPA2 with AES, no WEP.
- Encryption: authentication should be mutual and centralized.
- Credential: default passwords changed, unused management interfaces disabled.
- Credential: out-of-band or separate VLAN management network for APs.
- SSID: must not reveal organization name, address or phone number.
- SSID: not broadcast.
- Monitoring: real-time monitoring, wireless IDS/IPS placement, session timeout.
- Monitoring: detect rogue devices and hidden wireless networks.
- Monitoring: replace broken authentication schemes promptly.
- Exam traps:
  - Auditor first checks policy and inventory, then tests technical settings.
  - Default settings left unchanged is a common high-risk finding.

## 5.9.10 Internet of Things (IoT)

- Definition: IoT is physical devices with sensors and software that exchange data.
- Use: Delivers business value and competitiveness through connected devices.
- Audit impact: IoT needs tailored audit procedures; controls resemble mobile device controls.
- Definition: Embedded system is a computing function built into a larger system.
- Examples: network printers, smart TVs, HVAC controls, thermostats, medical devices.

### IoT risk categories

- Business risk: health and safety, compliance, privacy, unexpected costs.
- Operational risk: inappropriate access to functions, shadow use, performance.
- Technical risk: device vulnerabilities, updates, management.

### Major IoT risks

- Low software quality: developers and users overlook firmware security issues.
- Lack of encryption exposes credentials crossing the network.
- Low processing power limits built-in security features.
- Outdated hardware and software enable botnets and eavesdropping.
- Backdoor accounts often masquerade as service accounts.
- Poor device management: little visibility into connected IoT devices.
- Interconnectedness: one compromised device can spread to the whole network.
- Absence of inbuilt security makes devices easy targets.
- Disconnect IoT devices when not in use to reduce attack surface.
- Example: A compromised smart thermostat becomes a pivot into the corporate network.

### IoT security controls

- Physical security: IMEI lock and eSIM for cellular IoT devices.
- Use: SIM stores critical information; lock it to the specific device.
- Remote access security: lock SIM to device; remotely disable on physical breach.
- Avoid public networks; use private networks for sensitive communication.
- Encryption protects data in transit (see 5.6).
- Network isolation separates connectivity from core functions; disable unneeded functions.
- Abnormality prevention and detection: alert on unusual activity; firewalls block outside traffic.
- Exam traps:
  - Isolation and segmentation are the strongest answer for unpatchable devices.
  - Default and backdoor accounts are a typical IoT finding.

### Security event management (SEM)

- ⚠ Check: SEM belongs to Part B in the manual, not 5.9 itself.
- Definition: SEM identifies, collects, monitors, responds to and reports security events.
- Use: Records and evaluates events across the information systems infrastructure.
- Audit impact: Helps auditors assess security architecture, policies and procedures.

## Takeaway

- Threat defines control: theft means encryption, public Wi-Fi means VPN.
- Remote wipe alone never guarantees data is permanently gone.
- Policy first, then MDM, then containers for personal and business separation.
- Use WPA2 with AES minimum, prefer WPA3; WEP never acceptable.
- Rogue APs, default settings and unmanaged devices are classic audit findings.
- Isolate IoT devices and remove default or backdoor accounts.
