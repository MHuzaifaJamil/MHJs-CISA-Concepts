## 5.1.0 Basics first: the documentation chain

- Information asset: any data, system or resource with value to the organization.
- Why documents matter: they say how assets are protected and used.
- Order, top to bottom: policy, standard, procedure, guideline.
- Policy is the "why" and "what", set at the top.
- Standard is mandatory rules that support the policy.
- Procedure is the "how", step by step.
- Guideline is optional advice that helps apply standards.
- Example: policy says encrypt sensitive email; standard names the approved algorithm; procedure shows setup.

## 5.1.1 Policies, procedures and guidelines

### Information security policy

- Definition: high-level rules and directives protecting information assets, written by senior management and board.
- Use: states the role of information security in the organization.
- Use: sets the tone and assigns responsibility for a secure environment.
- Audit impact: auditor reviews policies to confirm they set tone and assign responsibility.
- Audit impact: check the date of last update to confirm currency.
- ⭐ Exam traps: a policy owned only by IT, or never reviewed, is a finding.
- Exam traps: management approval and periodic review are the core expectations.
- 📓 Executive sponsorship gives authority and funding to security programs.

### Key elements of a policy

- Scope
  - Definition: boundary of the policy, set at a high level.
  - Use: says where information lives and who can access it.
  - Use: also covers where, how and when information may be stored.
  - Audit impact: unclear scope leaves assets uncovered and unowned.
- Policy statement
  - Definition: the organization's stated approach to security.
  - Use: sets the operating environment and the laws and regulations to follow.
  - Use: describes the types of information the organization handles.
  - Audit impact: confirm named laws match the real regulatory obligations.
- Objectives
  - Definition: goals the program is measured against.
  - Use: should be SMART.
  - SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, time-bound.
  - Audit impact: vague, unmeasurable objectives cannot show whether the program works.
- Typical objectives, each with a short meaning:
  - Confidentiality: only authorized people see information.
  - Integrity: information stays accurate, reliable and unaltered without consent.
  - Availability: authorized people can reach information when needed.
  - Availability is supported by routine maintenance keeping systems running.
  - Security guidance: a general, organization-wide approach to security.
  - Monitoring: documents protective measures and helps show value from IT systems.
  - Governance: structured decisions, clear roles, culture of continuous improvement.
  - Compliance: systems built to meet application compliance requirements.
  - Privacy: guidance protecting customer and other critical information.
- Example: "Restore payroll within four hours" is SMART; "keep systems safe" is not.

### Characteristics of a good policy

- Executive support
  - Definition: management buy-in with budget and resources.
  - Audit impact: without it, the policy is unfunded and ignored.
- Business-driven
  - Definition: security supports and extends business objectives.
  - Audit impact: auditor regularly checks alignment with business objectives.
- Simple and user-friendly
  - Definition: easy to read, usable as a reference by everyone.
  - Audit impact: complex policies are poorly understood and poorly followed.
  - 📓 User comprehension is vital for policy effectiveness.
- Integrated
  - Definition: covers all business functions and processes.
  - Use: contains or links issue-specific policies.
- Accessible
  - Definition: available to everyone in the organization.
  - Example: posting the policy on the intranet.
- Strategic
  - Definition: long-term focus, intended for several years.
- Professional
  - Definition: well presented while stressing adherence.
- Penalties
  - Definition: stated consequences for noncompliance.
  - Audit impact: a policy without penalties is hard to enforce.
- Flexible
  - Definition: adaptable to growth and new challenges.
- Regularly updated
  - Definition: revised with industry and business changes.
  - Audit impact: revisions should record last modification date and responsible department.
- ⭐ Exam traps: "penalties" and "accessible" are policy characteristics, often tested as missing elements.
- Exam traps: a rigid, never-updated policy fails flexibility and update tests.

### Types of policies

- Organizational (master) policy
  - Definition: primary, generic blueprint for the whole organization.
  - Use: parent of all other security policies.
- System-specific policy (SysSP)
  - Definition: derived from the master policy for one system, application or network.
  - Use: often enforced through access controls.
  - Example: payroll system security policy.
- Issue-specific policy (ISSP)
  - Definition: builds on the master policy to address specific threats.
  - Use: gives guidance on one topic or activity.
- Exam traps: SysSP covers one system; ISSP covers one issue across the organization.

### Issue-specific policy examples

- Acceptable use policy
  - Definition: constraints an employee accepts to use systems and networks.
- Change control policy
  - Definition: guidelines for formal changes to IT software and security processes.
- Access control policy
  - Definition: rules for access to data and systems, and steps to get access.
  - Audit impact: auditor confirms it enforces segregation of incompatible duties.
  - 📓 Goal: no one person executes conflicting functions leading to fraud or error.
  - ⭐ Exam traps: primary reason to review an access policy is segregation of duties.
- Incident response policy
  - Definition: organized approach to manage, remediate and recover from incidents.
- Identity and access management (IAM) policy
  - Definition: authorization of systems and applications to the right people.
  - Use: also covers authentication of employees, contractors and third parties.
- Remote access policy
  - Definition: acceptable, secure methods of connecting to internal networks remotely.
- Bring your own device (BYOD) policy
  - Definition: rules allowing personal devices to access organizational infrastructure.
  - Use: lists steps that reduce exposure from employee-owned assets.
  - 📓 BYOD introduces several distinct vulnerabilities at once.

### Information security procedures

- Definition: step-by-step tasks performed to reach an objective.
- Use: apply to users, IT staff, operations staff and security members.
- Use: cover OS install, security setup, new accounts, privileges, audit, destruction, incident reporting.
- Lowest level in the documentation chain, closest to systems and users.
- Audit impact: should be detailed enough for a wide range of employees.
- Audit impact: missing procedures lead to inconsistent, person-dependent practice.
- Example: a documented checklist for creating a new user account.

### Information security guidelines

- Definition: a suggested way of doing something, less prescriptive than a procedure.
- Use: recommendations when no specific standard applies.
- Use: help implement standards and streamline processes using best practice.
- General and open to interpretation; need not be strictly followed.
- Audit impact: noncompliance with a guideline alone is not a violation.
- ⭐ Exam traps: standards are mandatory, guidelines are advisory; mixing them is a common distractor.
- Exam traps: procedures are specific steps; guidelines allow leeway.

## 5.1.2 Information security frameworks and standards

### Standards

- Definition: mandatory activities, actions or rules supporting policies and their enforcement.
- Use: make technologies, parameters and procedures uniform across the organization.
- Example: policy requires approved encryption; standard names the approved method.
- Advantage: repeatable security, giving consistent management.
- Advantage: based on compliance and best practice, enabling objective decisions.
- Advantage: shared knowledge and common terms and definitions.
- Advantage: consistent products and installations matching organizational needs.
- Advantage: better product functionality and compatibility.

### Frameworks

- Definition: set of processes defining policies and procedures for a security program.
- Use: define and prioritize security tasks and manage risk through controls.
- Use: often the basis for internal policies, procedures and guidelines.
- Frameworks differ in complexity and scale.
- Organizations customize frameworks for industry or regulatory needs.
- Frameworks often overlap; mapping reduces duplicated effort and finds gaps.
- Framework choice depends on location, industry and expected compliance.
- Audit impact: choice must support operational, compliance and audit requirements.
- Audit impact: auditor must understand the frameworks applied in the organization.

### Common frameworks and standards

- ISO/IEC 27001 ➕
  - ⚠ Check: outline marks this ➕, but the manual does list ISO/IEC 27001.
  - Definition: international standard specifying an information security management system (ISMS).
  - Use: sets procedures to develop and maintain the ISMS.
  - Use: covers organizational, people, physical and technological controls.
  - ⚠ Check: ISO/IEC 27002 appears in the manual only via CCM mapping.
  - ➕ 27002 as the control guidance companion to 27001 is unverified here.
- NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) ➕
  - ⚠ Check: outline marks this ➕, but the manual lists NIST CSF.
  - Definition: standards, guidelines and best practices for managing cybersecurity risk.
  - Use: flexible, integrates with existing processes, usable in any sector.
- COBIT 📓
  - Definition: IT governance framework of best approaches for governing enterprise IT.
  - Use: minimizes IT risk while ensuring efficient use of resources.
  - ⭐ Exam traps: COBIT is governance; it is not a security control catalog.
- Zachman Framework
  - Definition: model for classifying and developing enterprise architectures, by John Zachman.
  - Use: models functions, processes and elements; used in managing change.
- TOGAF
  - Definition: enterprise architecture framework from The Open Group.
  - Use: defines business objectives and aligns them with architecture objectives.
  - Use: promotes consistent standards and methods among architects.
- SABSA
  - Definition: model for risk-driven, opportunity-focused security enterprise architecture.
  - Use: lifecycle of strategy, design, implementation, then managing and measuring.
- ITIL
  - Definition: framework standardizing IT service management.
  - Use: standardizes selecting, planning, delivering and maintaining the IT lifecycle.
- CIS Critical Security Controls (CSC)
  - Definition: technical and operational controls applicable to any environment.
  - Use: control areas include inventory, data protection, log management, malware and penetration testing.
  - Audit impact: does not address risk management; pair with a risk framework.
- PCI DSS
  - Definition: payment card standard developed by major card companies.
  - Use: applies where cardholder data is stored, processed or transmitted.
  - Audit impact: scope also covers environments that can affect cardholder data security.
  - Audit impact: extends to organizations outsourcing payment operations to third parties.
- CSA STAR (Security, Trust, Assurance and Risk)
  - Definition: Cloud Security Alliance assurance framework for cloud service providers.
  - Use: combines transparency, rigorous auditing and standards harmonization.
  - Use: voluntary self-assessments, attestations and certifications validate cloud posture.
- Cloud Control Matrix (CCM)
  - Definition: CSA control set of 197 objectives in 17 domains.
  - Use: assesses cloud implementations; guides customers and providers on needed controls.
  - Use: mapped to ISO 27001/27002, PCI DSS and NIST CSF.
- Exam traps: match the framework to the need.
  - Governance: COBIT. Service management: ITIL. Enterprise architecture: Zachman, TOGAF, SABSA.
  - ISMS certification: ISO 27001. Cloud assurance: CSA STAR. Card data: PCI DSS.

### Reasons to use a framework

- Improved security management through continuous implementation of measures and controls.
- Effective risk management; frameworks build a culture of risk management.
- Effective, efficient incident response when a breach occurs.
- Increased market reach; certification can qualify firms for certain contracts.
- Increased reliability; understanding needs ensures correct controls and procedures.
- Audit impact: auditor reviews framework use and relevance, and whether it meets its purpose.

### Auditing frameworks

- Review logical access, security testing techniques and investigation techniques.
- Review the framework against its basic elements.
- Also review risk management and risk assessment results.
- Also review control design and incident management.
- Exam traps: framework adoption alone is not assurance; check actual implementation and results.

## 5.1.3 Information security baselines

- Definition: minimum security level a system, network or device must meet.
- Use: products, configurations and mechanisms usually mapped to industry standards.
- Example: all systems meeting a minimum C2 trusted computer system evaluation standard.
- ⚠ Check: manual text is garbled around "C2"; C2 expansion unverified here ➕.
- A baseline plan should be followed by a full security evaluation and plan.
- Audit impact: baseline is also the comparison point when integrity is questioned.

### Baseline topics (Figure 5.3)

- Environment/inventory
  - Use: maintain inventory of networked computers with registered addresses.
  - Use: record the OS and owner.
- Malware
  - Use: antivirus with automatic updates, no less than weekly.
- Passwords
  - Use: strong passwords only; department provides guidance.
  - Use: departmental accounts for workgroups avoid password sharing.
- Patching
  - Use: automatic OS and basic software patching.
  - Use: less manual work means less chance of compromise.
  - Use: automate workstations so admins can focus on servers.
- Minimizing services
  - Use: offer only needed services on workstations.
  - Audit impact: default-enabled services widen the attack surface.
- Addressing vulnerabilities
  - Use: remove default accounts and delete unused accounts.
  - Use: organization-wide scans identify vulnerabilities and give a comparison baseline.
- Backups
  - Use: enable easy recovery from user mistakes and hardware failure.
  - Use: store backups offsite for added security.

### Baseline evaluation checklist (Figure 5.4)

- Environment/inventory checks
  - Data types, forms, and legal or regulatory handling requirements.
  - Which machines hold confidential data, and how it is protected.
  - OS types, subnets, workstation and server counts, locations.
  - Current documented hardware and software inventory; licensing proof.
- Malware checks
  - Is there an antimalware policy; are signatures current?
  - Are updates automatic; does it defend against zero-day attacks?
  - Do staff know whom to contact on infection?
- Password checks
  - Strong password policy enforced by software; password caching disabled.
  - Defined password change frequency; staff know accounts are not shared.
  - Admin has written authorization to test for weak passwords.
  - MFA enabled for all users of cloud-based systems.
- Patching checks
  - Automatic where possible; documented process and patch alert sources.
  - Patches tested before applying; machine backed up first.
- Minimizing services checks
  - Services identified, dependencies known, unneeded default services removed.
- Vulnerability and audit checks
  - Scan findings resolved; independent scan done by IT staff.
  - Firewalls and intrusion detection deployed or considered.
- Backup, recovery and BCP checks
  - Regular backups, offsite storage and periodic restore tests.
  - Media sanitized before disposal; redundant hardware exists.
  - Immutable backups and a broker service implemented.
- IT staff checks
  - Current job descriptions that include security duties.
  - Documentation to ease staff transitions.
- Privacy checks
  - Privacy policy exists; staff aware of privacy considerations.
  - Confidentiality or nondisclosure agreements cover privileged information.
- Exam traps: unrestored backups and untested patches are higher risk than missing paperwork.

### Access standards

- Definition: rules for who may access what, set at three levels.
- Level 1: generic, such as all passwords at least eight characters.
- Level 2: specific machines, such as Unix systems enforcing password changes.
- Level 3: specific applications, such as clerks entering but not authorizing payments.
- Audit impact: auditor confirms they separate duties, prevent fraud or error, meet policy.
- Audit impact: also confirm they minimize unauthorized access risk.

## Takeaway

- Policy states intent; standards are mandatory; guidelines are advisory; procedures are steps.
- Management approves policy; auditors check currency, scope, objectives and enforceability.
- Match framework to purpose: COBIT governance, ITIL services, ISO 27001 ISMS.
- Baselines are minimums; auditors test them with inventory, patching, backup and access checks.
- Access standards exist to enforce segregation of duties.
