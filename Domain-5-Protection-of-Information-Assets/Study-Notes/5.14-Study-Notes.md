# 5.14 Security Incident Response Management - Study Notes

[← Domain 5](../README.md)

Scope: Outline file 5.14-Security-Incident-Response-Management.md (CRM 28th Ed., sections 5.14.1 to 5.14.4).

## 5.14.0 Basics: what an incident is

- Incident: an event that may damage the organization.
  - Definition: an event that threatens assets, operations or information.
  - Use: triggers formal response, escalation and recovery work.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks the organization defines "incident" in writing.
- Not all incidents are computer-related.
- Example: an office burglary is also an incident.
- Incident response capability: a formal ability to respond, recover and learn.
  - Definition: people, plans and tools ready before an incident occurs.
  - Use: limits damage and speeds recovery.
  - Audit impact: absence of a formal capability is a significant finding.
- Exam traps
  - Physical events count; do not restrict incidents to IT events.
  - A written definition separates incidents from ordinary events and security problems.

## 5.14.1 Incident response process

- The manual's process follows NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2.
- Four phases: preparation; detection and analysis; containment, eradication and recovery; postincident activity.

### Phase 1: Preparation

- Preparation: building readiness and preventing incidents before they happen.
  - Definition: setting up policy, people, procedures and protections in advance.
  - Use: lets the team respond fast and consistently.
  - Audit impact: weak preparation raises damage, cost and recovery time.
- Prevention is cheaper and more effective than reacting.
- Prevention: patching, hardening, and configuring the network perimeter.
  - Definition: reducing the chance an incident occurs at all.
  - Use: patch management, system hardening, firewall rule sets.
  - Audit impact: auditor tests patch status and hardening baselines.
- Define an incident.
  - Definition: the organization's own statement of what counts as an incident.
  - Use: standardizes handling and reduces false-positive alerts.
  - Audit impact: no definition means inconsistent reporting.
- Create an incident response policy.
  - Definition: top-level document setting incident scope and authority.
  - Use: defines incidents, structure, roles, responsibilities and reporting requirements.
  - Audit impact: auditor confirms the policy is approved and current.
- Develop procedures (SOPs).
  - Definition: standard operating procedures: detailed technical steps, checklists and forms.
  - Use: turns policy into repeatable team actions.
  - Audit impact: SOPs must reflect organizational priorities.
- Establish communication guidelines.
  - Definition: predetermined rules on what is shared with whom.
  - Use: covers other response teams, law enforcement, media, vendors, external victims.
  - Audit impact: prevents leaking wrong information to wrong parties.
- Define incident response activities.
  - Definition: the services the team provides beyond responding.
  - Use: advisories, vulnerability assessment, intrusion detection, education and awareness.
  - Audit impact: unclear scope leaves coverage gaps.
- Select the incident response team.
  - Definition: choosing team structure and staffing model.
  - Use: factors are size, geography, 24/7 need, cost, expertise.
  - Audit impact: members need proper training and development.
- Establish notification mechanisms.
  - Definition: documented on-hour and off-hour contact methods.
  - Use: reach CIO, CISO, IT support, business continuity, outside responders.
  - Audit impact: mechanisms must be maintained and exercised, not just written.
- Exam traps
  - Prevention belongs to preparation, not to recovery.
  - Policy sets roles and reporting; SOPs hold the technical detail.

### Phase 2: Detection and analysis

- Detection: noticing that something may be an incident.
  - Definition: finding and assessing possible incidents.
  - Use: quick detection stops damage growing over time.
  - Audit impact: auditor reviews monitoring coverage and alert handling.
- This is the hardest phase.
- Hardest part: accurately detecting and assessing possible incidents.
- Determining type, extent and magnitude is difficult.
- Automated sources: IDS/IPS, antivirus, log analyzers.
- Manual source: user reports.
- Physical sensors count: cameras, motion detectors, smoke alarms.
- Example: an IDS alert plus a user complaint confirm a malware outbreak.
- Exam traps
  - The challenge is accurate assessment, not collecting more alerts.
  - Containment cannot be chosen well until scope and type are analyzed.

### Phase 3: Containment, eradication and recovery

- Containment: stopping the incident from spreading.
  - Definition: decisions that limit damage and resource exhaustion.
  - Use: shut down a system, disconnect it, disable functions.
  - Audit impact: strategies should be documented for quick decisions.
- Most incidents require containment.
- Use a separate containment strategy per major incident type.
- Eradication: removing the cause of the incident.
  - Definition: eliminating incident components after containment.
  - Use: delete malicious code, disable breached accounts.
  - Audit impact: some incidents need no eradication, or do it during recovery.
- Recovery: returning systems to normal operation.
  - Definition: restoring service and hardening against repeats.
  - Use: restore from clean backups, rebuild, replace files with clean versions.
  - Audit impact: confirm backups were clean before restoring.
- Other recovery actions: install patches and change passwords.
- Recovery also tightens perimeter security, such as firewall rule sets.
- ⚠ Check: the manual calls eradication "after containment" but allows it inside recovery.
- Example: ransomware host is isolated, wiped, rebuilt, patched, then credentials are reset.
- Exam traps
  - Order is contain, then eradicate, then recover; do not wipe before containing.
  - Restoring from a possibly infected backup reintroduces the incident.

### Phase 4: Postincident activity

- Lessons-learned review: a meeting reviewing how the incident was handled.
  - Definition: structured review after the incident closes.
  - Use: judges effectiveness of the handling process.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks reviews occur and actions are tracked.
- Accumulated incident data reveals systemic weaknesses.
- Weaknesses may sit in controls, policies or procedures.
- Results guide control improvements and future incident handling.
- Results also feed training and awareness programs.
- ⚠ Check: outline marks training feed as ➕, but the manual does state it.
- 📓 Incident resolution meantime is the best indicator of incident program effectiveness.
- Exam traps
  - Number of incidents reported is a weak effectiveness measure.
  - Resolution time reflects process efficiency; alert time alone does not.

## 5.14.2 Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT)

- CSIRT: the team that responds to security incidents.
  - Definition: an established team with clear reporting lines.
  - Use: handles incidents and issues for its constituency.
  - Audit impact: auditor confirms its mandate, reporting lines and standby support.
- Standby support must also be established.
- Primary responsibilities
  - Determine amount and scope of damage.
  - Determine whether information was compromised.
  - Implement recovery procedures to restore security.
  - Supervise additional security measures to prevent repeat incidents.
- CSIRT acts as a detective control.
- CSIRT acts as a corrective control.
- Awareness programs, exercises and workshops make it a preventive control.
- ⚠ Check: preventive role arises only through awareness work, not core duties.
- CSIRT is the single point of contact for security incidents.
- It also answers abuse reports about its constituency's network.
- It disseminates alerts, guidelines and updates to users.
- Auditor checks a formal documented plan covering identification, reporting and response procedures.
- Real-time IDS notification and a severity-rating process should exist.
- Common issues the plan must cover
  - Virus outbreak.
  - Web defacement.
  - Abuse notification.
  - Unauthorized access.
  - IDS/IPS security alerts.
  - Hardware or software theft.
  - System root compromise.
  - Physical security breach.
  - Malware.
  - Defamatory media information.
  - Forensic investigations.
- Exam traps
  - The CSIRT should be centralized contact, not scattered across departments.
  - Auditor prefers a documented plan over informal expertise.

## 5.14.3 Incident response plan

- Incident response plan (IRP): a documented plan for handling incidents.
  - Definition: the guide for dealing with actual and potential security incidents.
  - Use: allows cordoning off the scene and finding the attack's extent.
  - Audit impact: auditor may need to assure and advise on its effectiveness.
- Its greatest benefit is preserving evidence and starting remediation quickly.
- Best practices
  - Automate repetitive tasks.
    - Frees staff for strategic work and reduces labor cost.
  - Leverage templates and playbooks.
    - Playbooks assign roles, responsibilities and deadlines.
  - Centralize IR initiatives.
    - One accessible pool of data from all security tools.
  - Test the plan.
    - Exercises check ability to discover and contain incidents.
    - Test results calibrate the plan for real incidents.
  - Invest in IR training.
    - Closes talent gaps as technology changes.
  - Standardize incident response.
    - Reduces training needed; new recruits learn faster.
  - Obtain executive buy-in.
    - Executives approve the plan and supply resources.
    - Executives are ultimately responsible for effective operation.
  - Confirm roles and responsibilities.
    - Everyone must accept roles; reserves back up critical people.
    - Auditor tests this by interviewing staff.
  - Document critical assets.
    - Rank by criticality to set speed and depth of response.
    - Factors: compliance requirements, sensitivity, value.
  - Establish a crisis communications plan.
    - Appoint a spokesperson.
    - Pre-approve core messages and keep them instantly retrievable.
- Example: a draft breach statement already approved avoids mid-incident sign-off delays.
- Exam traps
  - Untested plans are the common gap; testing validates the plan.
  - Executive approval, not IT approval, is the key requirement.
  - Treating all assets equally slows response to critical ones.

## 5.14.4 Security Orchestration, Automation and Response (SOAR)

- SOAR: a platform that gathers security data and automates responses.
  - Definition: technologies combining orchestration, automation and response in one console.
  - Use: top-to-bottom threat management from identification to response strategy.
  - Audit impact: auditor advises on automation for operational efficiency.
- Orchestration: connects security tools.
  - Definition: integrates disparate, interdependent security systems into one layer.
  - Use: makes manual and automated tools work in harmony.
  - Audit impact: unintegrated tools leave visibility gaps.
- Automation: removes manual tasks.
  - Definition: tool-driven handling of repeatable security work.
  - Use: user access management, log queries, threat triage, containment.
  - Audit impact: the component that sets SOAR apart.
- Response: coordinates reaction to threats.
  - Definition: managing, planning and coordinating responses to identified threats.
  - Use: built on orchestration and automation.
  - Audit impact: response playbooks need review and testing.
- ⚠ Check: the manual says automation "eliminates" human error; reduces is safer wording.

### Benefits of SOAR

- Promotes security visibility.
  - Dashboards, metrics and reporting cover the whole infrastructure.
- Reduces security costs.
  - Less manual work and fewer specialists needed.
- Improves security efficiency.
  - Staff spend less time on each event.
- Improves security effectiveness.
  - Accurate real-time interventions mean fewer errors.
- Promotes security flexibility.
  - Fits existing setups without costly system redesign.
- Enhances collaboration.
  - Teams from different areas work together on one platform.
- Streamlines security operations.
  - Plugin libraries and prebuilt workflows for common use cases.

### SOAR vs SIEM

- SIEM: collects logs and events, then alerts analysts.
  - Definition: security information and event management.
  - Use: aggregation, correlation and alerting.
  - Audit impact: alerts alone still need human action.
- Both detect issues, collect data and handle notifications.
- SIEM only alerts; SOAR adds automation and response.
- SOAR usually absorbs SIEM data.
- SOAR also ingests endpoint, cloud and IoT alerts.
- ⚠ Check: manual claims SOAR can "predict" threats; unsupported ➕.
- Exam traps
  - If a question needs automated response, choose SOAR, not SIEM.
  - SOAR extends SIEM; it does not simply replace log collection.

## Takeaway

- Phases: preparation, detection, containment/eradication/recovery, postincident review.
- Detection and accurate assessment is the hardest phase.
- Contain first, then eradicate, then recover from clean backups.
- CSIRT is the single contact; plan must be tested and approved.
- SOAR adds automated response beyond SIEM alerting.
