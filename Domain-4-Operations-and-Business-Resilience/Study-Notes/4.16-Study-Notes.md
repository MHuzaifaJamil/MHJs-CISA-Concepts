## 4.16 Overview: what DR planning is

### Basics
- Disaster recovery (DR) planning
  - Definition: planning to restore critical IT services after a disruption.
  - Use: manages availability as an internal control.
  - Audit impact: auditors judge whether plans match business needs and are tested.
- DR planning is a continuous process, not a one-time project.
- Its importance comes from the business impact analysis (BIA), including RPO and RTO.
- Two outputs result from DR planning.
  - IT DR strategies: infrastructure, process and organization changes over three to five years.
  - DRPs: range from department procedures to modular, multitiered plans.
- Goal: respond to incidents affecting people, operations and compliance.
- Human safety comes first; evacuate people before restoring service.
- Third-party compliance obligations still apply during a disaster.
- Third-party disaster: the customer may have to wait for the vendor's DRP.
  - Audit impact: review vendor DR commitments and testing evidence in contracts.
- Example: a cloud provider outage leaves customers waiting on the provider's recovery.

### Exam traps
- People safety outranks system restoration in any recovery sequence.
- BIA drives recovery priorities; technology choices follow, never lead.

## 4.16.1 RPO, RTO and MTTR ⭐

### Recovery point objective (RPO) 📓⭐
- Definition: maximum acceptable data loss, measured as time before the disaster.
- Use: sets how often backups or replication must run.
- Audit impact: backup frequency longer than RPO means a control gap.
- Example: RPO of four hours requires a backup at least every four hours.
- Catch-up data
  - Definition: transactions since the recovery point that must be re-entered.
  - Audit impact: manual re-entry is slow and error-prone.
- Orphan data
  - Definition: data still lost even after re-entry.
  - Audit impact: complete recovery is almost impossible; expect residual loss.
- RPO mainly drives data protection technology.
- RPO in minutes calls for mirroring or real-time replication.
- Not all critical data retained means RPO was defined wrongly. 📓

### Recovery time objective (RTO) 📓
- Definition: maximum acceptable downtime; when operations must resume.
- Use: selects availability technology such as warm site, hot site or clusters.
- Audit impact: tested recovery time exceeding RTO means the plan fails.
- RTO in minutes calls for a hot site, dedicated spares or clustering.
- Disaster tolerance
  - Definition: time gap the business accepts without critical IT services.
  - Audit impact: lower RTO means lower tolerance and higher cost.

### Technologies by objective
- Both objectives are time-based; nearer zero means higher cost.

| Objective | Technologies, slower to faster |
|---|---|
| RPO | Tape, log shipping, disk backup, snapshots |
| RPO (tighter) | Delayed replication, mirroring, real-time replication |
| RTO | Cold standby, hot standby |
| RTO (tighter) | Active-passive clustering, active-active clustering |

### Mean time to repair (MTTR)
- Definition: average time to repair a failed system or device.
- Formula: MTTR = total repair time / number of repairs.
- Use: measures how quickly support teams restore failed components.
- Audit impact: high MTTR means lost productivity and revenue.
- Factors raising MTTR
  - System complexity.
  - Scarce spare parts.
  - Low technician skill or too few technicians.
  - Weak maintenance procedures.

### Other recovery parameters
- Interruption window
  - Definition: longest wait from failure to restored critical services.
  - Audit impact: beyond it, progressive losses become unaffordable.
- Service delivery objective (SDO)
  - Definition: service level reached while running in alternate mode.
  - Audit impact: must match business needs, not just IT capability.
- Maximum tolerable outage (MTO)
  - Definition: longest time the business can run in alternate mode.
  - Audit impact: lower alternate SDO and growing backlog make long stays unmanageable.
- ⚠ Check: manual says "maximum tolerable outages (MTOs)"; do not confuse with MTD.

### Worked example 1: RPO and catch-up data
- Given: last backup 02:00; disaster 05:30; restore finishes 09:30.
- Given: transaction rate 40 per hour; RPO four hours.
- Step 1: data lost at disaster = 05:30 - 02:00 = 3.5 hours.
- Step 2: check RPO: 3.5 hours is at most 4 hours, so RPO met.
- Step 3: outage duration = 09:30 - 05:30 = 4 hours.
- Step 4: catch-up window = 02:00 to 09:30 = 7.5 hours.
- Step 5: catch-up transactions = 7.5 x 40 = 300.
- Verification: lost 3.5 x 40 = 140, outage 4 x 40 = 160, sum 300; matches.
- Verification: worst-case loss equals backup interval, so interval must not exceed RPO.

### Worked example 2: RTO check
- Given: disaster 05:30; RTO four hours.
- Given: declaration took 30 minutes; failover 3 hours 15 minutes; validation 45 minutes.
- Formula: recovery time = declaration + failover + validation.
- Step 1: 30 + 195 + 45 = 270 minutes.
- Step 2: 270 minutes = 4.5 hours.
- Step 3: 4.5 hours exceeds the 4-hour RTO by 30 minutes; RTO missed.
- Verification: 05:30 + 4:30 = 10:00; clock elapsed 10:00 - 05:30 = 4.5 hours.
- Verification: RTO deadline 09:30; actual 10:00; shortfall 30 minutes confirmed.

### Worked example 3: MTTR
- Given: three repairs took 2 hours, 5 hours and 2 hours.
- Step 1: total repair time = 2 + 5 + 2 = 9 hours.
- Step 2: MTTR = 9 / 3 = 3 hours.
- Verification: 3 repairs x 3 hours = 9 hours, equals the total.
- Verification: deviations from 3 are -1, +2, -1; they sum to zero.

### Exam traps
- RPO concerns data; RTO concerns time. Do not swap them.
- Tight RPO needs replication; tight RTO needs standby capacity.
- Lowering either objective raises cost; reject answers claiming otherwise.
- MTTR is repair time, not recovery-site activation time.

## 4.16.2 Recovery strategies

- Definition: the chosen way to recover systems after an interruption.
- Use: guides development of detailed recovery procedures.
- Audit impact: each critical platform needs a strategy.
- Management reviews all alternatives, selects one and accepts residual risk.
- Selection factors
  - Criticality of process and applications.
  - Cost.
  - Time to recover.
  - Security.
- Recovery costs
  - Fixed: redundant computers, alternate routing, testing.
  - Variable: costs incurred during actual use.
  - Fixed costs are insurable; variable costs generally are not.
  - A suitable plan can lower insurance premiums.

### Exam traps
- Management selects strategy and owns residual risk; auditors only advise.
- Cheapest option is wrong if it cannot meet RTO.

## 4.16.3 Recovery alternatives

### Site types
- Cold site
  - Definition: space and basic infrastructure with no IT equipment or data.
  - Use: cheapest option for tolerant, low-criticality processes.
  - Audit impact: plan must cover acquiring and installing equipment.
- Mobile site
  - Definition: modular processing facility on transportable vehicles.
  - Use: delivered to a location specified on activation.
  - Audit impact: plan needs locations, right of access and utilities.
- Warm site
  - Definition: partly configured site with network connections and peripherals.
  - Use: faster than cold; load programs and data on activation.
  - Audit impact: equipment may be less capable than production.
- Hot site
  - Definition: fully equipped site with installed software and near-real-time data.
  - Use: fast recovery; staff move from the primary site.
  - Audit impact: storage capacity must match the primary site. 📓
  - Audit impact: differing server specifications are a key concern. 📓
  - Audit impact: shared, never-expiring accounts are a key concern. 📓
- Mirrored site
  - Definition: fully redundant site with real-time replication, equipped and staffed.
  - Use: users perceive no interruption.
  - Audit impact: highest cost; justify through BIA.
- Reciprocal agreement
  - Definition: similar companies agree to share IT facilities.
  - Audit impact: weak on compatibility, privacy compliance and enforceability.
- Reciprocal agreement for unique equipment
  - Definition: organizations with unique equipment or applications assist each other.
  - Audit impact: used less often than traditional reciprocal agreements.

### Selecting and contracting
- Fastest recovery costs most; compare BIA loss to option cost.
- Alternate site should lie outside the area hit by the disruption.
- Plan needs redundant network communication to the alternate site.
- Facility may be owned or third-party; subscribers may compete for it.
- Contract provisions to verify
  - Configurations adequate, with updates and tests over time.
  - Disaster definition broad enough.
  - Access exclusive or shared, guaranteed, with conflict resolution.
  - Priority that precludes preemption by governmental entities.
  - Availability and speed of availability.
  - Subscribers per site and per area.
  - Preference in regional disasters; backup for backup facilities.
  - Insurance for employees at the site.
  - Use period and technical support.
  - Communications adequacy.
  - Warranties and liability limits.
  - Right to audit logical, physical and environmental security.
  - Testing rights; possible premium reduction.
  - Reliability: UPS, limited subscribers, technical management, compatibility guarantees.
  - Security consistent with company policy.

### Procuring alternative hardware
- Cold and warm sites acquire hardware and software on activation.
- Standard systems are easy to buy; unique or outdated ones are not.
- Pre-acquire hard-to-get components to meet the RTO.
- Telecommunications recovery
  - Network disaster prevention and alternative routing.
  - Diverse routing: split or duplicate cable facilities.
  - Long-haul diversity and local loop protection.
  - Voice recovery, circuit and bandwidth availability, server DRPs.

### Exam traps
- Hot site audit concerns: capacity, specification mismatch and account hygiene outweigh cosmetic gaps.
- Reciprocal agreements fail on enforceability; do not accept them for critical systems.
- Site beside the primary is wrong; one disaster could hit both.

## 4.16.4 Development of DR plans

- Strategy follows the BIA and risk assessment.
- Implementation may change systems, networks, sites, organization and processes.
- Write the plan in simple language.

### IT DRP contents
- Procedures for declaring a disaster, including escalation.
- Activation criteria and covered scenarios.
- Linkage to emergency response, crisis management and BCPs.
- Persons responsible for each function.
- Recovery teams and their responsibilities.
- Contact and notification lists.
- Step-by-step recovery: where, when, what and order.
- Recovery procedures per IT system or component.
- Vendor and supplier contacts.
- Resources required for recovery.

### IT DRP scenarios
- Loss of network connectivity.
- Loss of a key IT system.
- Loss of the processing site or server room.
- Loss of critical data.
- Loss of an office.
- Loss of a key service provider, such as cloud.
- Plan for worst-case timing, such as sales peaks or period end.
- Recovery procedures give a high-level sequence per scenario, linking to detailed steps.

### Organization and responsibilities
- Use a matrix linking teams to disruption levels.
- Team names are not tested by name. ➕
- Incident response team: receives reports; gives postmortem input. ➕
- Emergency action team: first responders and fire wardens; evacuation. ➕
- Information security team: maintains security at the alternate site. ➕
- Damage assessment team: estimates damage and recovery time. ➕
- Emergency management team: coordinates teams; activates BCP; finance, legal, PR. ➕
- Offsite storage team: ships media and records to recovery site. ➕
- Software team: restores system packs and loads operating system software. ➕
- Applications team: restores user programs; monitors database integrity. ➕
- Emergency operations team: shift operators at the recovery site. ➕
- Network recovery team: reroutes WAN voice and data traffic. ➕
- Communications team: installs communications hardware; works with carriers. ➕
- Transportation team: finds recovery site; moves employees. ➕
- User hardware team: terminals, printers and photocopiers. ➕
- Data preparation and records team: updates databases; salvages records. ➕
- Administrative support team: clerical work, message center, payroll. ➕
- Supplies team: logistics for office and computer supplies. ➕
- Salvage team: relocation advice, reconstruction versus relocation, insurance claims. ➕
- Relocation team: moves operations from hot site to restored location. ➕
- Coordination team: links offices and offshored providers. ➕
- Legal affairs team: handles legal issues from incidents. ➕
- Recovery test team: tests plans and analyzes results. ➕
- Training team: BC and DR training for users. ➕

### Exam traps
- A plan lacking escalation and activation criteria cannot be invoked reliably.
- Plan must cover worst-case timing, not average operations.

## 4.16.5 DR testing methods

### Principles
- Base test schedule on risk assessment and BIA.
- Untested plans are likely to fail.
- Set objectives that give measurable benefit.
- An independent party, such as the IS auditor, monitors and evaluates.
- Testing steps
  - Develop test objectives.
  - Execute the test.
  - Evaluate the test.
  - Develop recommendations.
  - Follow up on implementation.
- No recommendations means the test was not challenging enough.

### Test types, least to most rigorous
- Checklist review
  - Definition: team members confirm distributed checklists are current.
  - Audit impact: preliminary only; proves nothing about real recovery.
- Structured walk-through
  - Definition: team steps through the plan on paper.
  - Audit impact: finds gaps and constraints cheaply.
- Simulation test
  - Definition: role-play a scenario without processing at the recovery site.
  - Audit impact: tests people and coordination, not technology.
- Parallel test
  - Definition: recovery site made ready while primary operations continue.
  - Audit impact: proves recovery site capability without business risk.
- Full interruption test
  - Definition: operations shift fully to the recovery site.
  - Audit impact: most rigorous, costly and disruptive.
- Start simple and increase gradually; run full interruption yearly after individual tests.
- Surprise tests are realistic but disruptive.
- Schedule at low-impact times, such as long weekends, with key members present.

### What testing evaluates
- Plan completeness and precision.
- Personnel performance.
- Awareness of non-team staff.
- Coordination with vendors and suppliers.
- Backup site capacity.
- Vital records retrieval.
- Relocated equipment and supplies.
- Overall IS processing performance.
- Phases: pretest, test (applications fail over), posttest.
- Posttest: restore to primary and delete data from third-party systems.
- Diaries record observations, problems and resolutions.

### Test results metrics
- Time: was RTO met.
- Data: was RPO met, with aligned recovery points across interconnected applications.
- Amount: throughput of the backup site.
- Percentage or number of critical systems recovered.
- Accuracy: data entry and processing compared with normal.

### Exam traps
- Greatest concern in a DR test: defective or failing backup systems. 📓⭐
- Reason: that defeats recovery itself; schedule or participant issues are secondary.
- Parallel test does not stop production; full interruption does.
- Simulation involves no processing at the recovery site.

## 4.16.6 Invoking DR plans

- BCP and DRP are closely aligned.
- Notify the designated individual first; follow escalation protocol.
- Escalation covers spokesperson, top management and regulators.
- Mobilize teams after matching the incident to a tested scenario.
- Evaluate response options by ability to meet RPO and RTO.
- Follow documented procedures; reassess options at each step.
  - Reason: testing may not have covered every step or scenario.
- Document all decisions.
- Update procedures after normal service resumes.

### Exam traps
- No tested scenario matches exactly; judgment plus documentation is expected.

## Case study (Domain 4 stub): Pinkwater Bank

- Stem only: community bank with 16 branches plans a new branch automation system.
- System handles deposits, loans and confidential customer data.
- Branch director proposes microwave radio links for low cost and privacy.
- Backup proposed: direct cable-TV coaxial Internet link per branch.
- Branches would also get wireless access points and locally administered servers.
- IT manager says the cable provider will encrypt coaxial traffic.
- Later stem: BCPs and DRPs unchanged for over eight years.
- Organization grew over 300 percent; headquarters has about 750 employees.
- Over 60 servers sit in the data center; branches link by frame relay.
- Critical applications have an RTO of three to five days.
- Branches are 30 to 50 miles apart and over 25 miles from headquarters.
- Data center backups sit at a third-party facility 35 miles away.
- Branch backups are stored at nearby branches under reciprocal agreements.
- Hot site contract: 25 servers, 100 desktops, plus branch shipment of servers.
- Contract term is three years; upgrades happen at renewal.
- ⚠ Check: the outline lists answers (no encryption, quarterly reviews); stem excludes them.

## Takeaway

- RPO governs data loss and backups; RTO governs downtime and standby technology.
- Nearer-zero objectives cost more; management weighs BIA loss against option cost.
- Hot site audits focus on capacity, specifications and account control.
- Plans must be tested; failing backup systems are the greatest test concern.
- Safety first, then invoke plans, document decisions and update afterward.
