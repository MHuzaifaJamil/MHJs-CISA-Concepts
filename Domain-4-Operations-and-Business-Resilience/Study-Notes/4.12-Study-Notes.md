## 4.12.0 Resilience lifecycle 📓

### Basics: the four-step chain
- Order: Risk Assessment, then BIA, then BCP, then DRP. 📓
- Each step feeds the next with its output.

### Risk Assessment (RA)
- Definition: process finding threats, vulnerabilities, and likely impact. 📓
- Use: supplies likelihood and impact inputs to the BIA.
- Audit impact: weak RA means the BIA starts from poor data.
- Example: comparing building weaknesses against earthquake likelihood.

### Business Impact Analysis (BIA) ⭐
- Definition: study of which functions matter and how long they can stop. 📓
- Use: sets downtime tolerance and recovery targets (MTD, RTO, RPO). 📓
- Audit impact: wrong BIA means wrong recovery strategy and wasted spend.
- Example: finding how long a bank survives with offline transactions.
- ⚠ Check: MTD is not defined in the manual's 4.12 text. ➕
- ⚠ Check: manual names interruption window and maximum tolerable outage (MTO) in 4.16.
- Treat MTD as the same idea as those terms, but confirm. ➕

### Business Continuity Plan (BCP)
- Definition: plan to keep the business running during a crisis. 📓
- Use: covers people, manual workarounds, and alternate sites.
- Audit impact: BCP must trace back to BIA priorities.

### Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)
- Definition: technical part of the BCP restoring IT systems. 📓
- Use: gives exact steps to rebuild servers and data.
- Audit impact: DRP targets must match BIA recovery times.
- Example: restoring databases from cloud backup servers.

## 4.12 BIA fundamentals

### Purpose and place in strategy
- BIA is a critical step in building continuity strategy.
- Its main output feeds the BCP and risk countermeasures.
- Primary objective: critical functions, recovery priorities, and RTO/RPO targets. 📓⭐
- It evaluates processes, supporting IT, time frames, priorities, resources, interdependencies.
- Audit impact: confirm each BIA result links to a business process.

### Relationship to risk assessment
- Even with prior risk assessment, double-check criticality inputs.
- BIA often uncovers vital but less visible supporting components.
- Hidden components include gateways and transport servers.
- Exam traps: BIA is not a threat-identification exercise; that is risk assessment.
- Exam traps: BIA is not an insurance valuation or failover speed test.

### Sponsorship and approval
- Needs strong senior management sponsorship.
- Needs heavy involvement of IT and end-user personnel.
- Senior management must approve criticality of information resources.
- Audit impact: IT alone deciding criticality is a finding.
- Exam traps: process owners and management own criticality, not the IT department.

### Resource scope
- Include all resource types: applications, data, networks, system software, facilities.
- Look beyond traditional resources such as database servers.
- Visible components are easy; hidden ones come from process owners.
- Audit impact: missing dependencies break recovery even when servers are restored.

### Outsourcing
- Consider contractual commitments when IT is outsourced.
- Audit impact: check provider contracts support BIA recovery times.
- Example: provider SLA promises 72-hour restore but BIA allows 24 hours.

### Impact bands and time estimates
- Definition: impact bands rate downtime effect as high, medium, or low.
- Use: each process impact is estimated in hours, days, or weeks.
- Same approach estimates impact of data loss.
- Financial value may be assigned to each band if needed.
- Audit impact: bands must be consistent across all business units.

### Vital resource supply times
- Collect how long the business runs if a supply breaks.
- Collect when a replacement resource would arrive.
- Example: bank running out of blank chip cards for personalization.
- Example: workstations shipped in after a disaster.

### BIA data-gathering approaches
- Questionnaire: sent to key users in IT and end-user areas.
- Questionnaire answers are tabulated, then follow-up contact fills gaps.
- Interviews: held with groups of key users, then tabulated.
- Joint workshop: IT staff and process owners meet together.
- Workshop is used after data collection to agree downtime and vital resources.
- Analyze past transaction volume to support interview answers.
- Audit impact: volume data checks users' claims against evidence.
- Exam traps: workshop comes after data collection, not as the first step.

### The three BIA questions
- Question 1: what are the different business processes?
- Question 2: which critical information resources relate to those processes?
- Question 3: what is the critical recovery period before unacceptable losses?
- Resource disruption is a disaster only if tied to a critical process.

#### Q1 Criticality indicators
- Supports health and safety, such as hospital records or air traffic control.
- Disruption causes lost income or exceptional unacceptable costs.
- Needed to meet legal or statutory requirements.
- Affects many users or business segments.
- Criticality varies by time and mode of operation.
- Example: ATM operations versus business-hours processing.

#### Q2 Critical business processes
- Examples: receiving payments, producing goods, paying employees.
- Examples: advertising, dispatching finished goods, legal and regulatory compliance.

#### Q3 Critical recovery period
- Definition: time processing must resume before significant losses occur.
- Banks and brokerages have shorter periods than manufacturers.
- Day of week and time of year change the window.
- Example: Saturday midnight outage gives a bank more time than Monday midnight.
- Exam traps: critical recovery period depends on the business, not on technology.

### Two independent cost factors
- Definition: downtime cost and recovery cost move in opposite directions.
- Downtime cost rises with time, then plateaus when business cannot function.
- Downtime parts: idle resources, lost sales, financial costs, delays.
- Indirect downtime parts: market share, image, goodwill.
- Recovery cost falls as the target recovery time lengthens.
- Recovery parts: BCP preparation and testing, offsite premises, insurance, alternate sites.
- Each strategy has a fixed-base cost paid even without a disaster.
- Shorter target recovery time means higher fixed cost.
- Variable costs appear after a disaster: warm site daily fee, overtime, logistics.
- Total cost is the sum of both curves, forming a U shape.
- Choose the strategy at the lowest point of the total curve.
- Audit impact: cheapest strategy is wrong if downtime losses spiral.
- Cost calculations are not tested on the CISA exam.
- Exam traps: minimum recovery cost alone is not the goal; minimum total cost is.

### Worked example A: total cost minimum (illustrative)
- Assumed downtime cost: US$10,000 per hour of outage.
- Strategy 1 recovers in 4 hours; fixed cost US$90,000.
- Strategy 2 recovers in 12 hours; fixed cost US$60,000.
- Strategy 3 recovers in 24 hours; fixed cost US$40,000.
- Formula: total cost = fixed recovery cost + (downtime hours x hourly cost).
- Strategy 1: 90,000 + (4 x 10,000) = 90,000 + 40,000 = 130,000.
- Strategy 2: 60,000 + (12 x 10,000) = 60,000 + 120,000 = 180,000.
- Strategy 3: 40,000 + (24 x 10,000) = 40,000 + 240,000 = 280,000.
- Lowest total is Strategy 1 at US$130,000.
- Verification: recomputed downtime parts 40,000; 120,000; 240,000 sum with fixed to match; ordering 130k < 180k < 280k confirmed.
- ⚠ Check: hourly cost is assumed and flat; real downtime cost plateaus.

## 4.12.1 Classification of operations and criticality analysis

### Risk ranking
- Definition: ranking systems by impact over critical recovery period times likelihood.
- Use: prioritizes which systems get recovery strategies first.
- Done together with IS processing and end-user personnel.
- Audit impact: ranking without end users gives distorted priorities.

### System classification
- Critical: replaceable only by identical capability; no manual option.
- Critical: very low interruption tolerance and very high interruption cost.
- Vital: manual operation possible only briefly.
- Vital: restore usually within about five days or less.
- Sensitive: manual operation at tolerable cost for extended period.
- Sensitive: manual work is difficult and needs extra staff.
- Nonsensitive: interruption tolerated long, with little or no cost.
- Nonsensitive: little or no catching up after restoration.
- Example: payment switch is critical; internal newsletter is nonsensitive.
- Exam traps: critical means no manual substitute, not merely important.
- Exam traps: sensitive systems can run manually; critical systems cannot.
- Exam traps: do not rank by system size or data volume alone.

### Annual loss expectancy (ALE) example
- Definition: expected loss from likelihood times impact, capping reasonable preparedness spend.
- Use: justifies a reasonable cost of being prepared.
- Manual example: 0.1 percent risk (1 in 1,000) over five years.
- Impact assumed: US$10 million.
- Formula: maximum reasonable spend = impact x probability.
- Step 1: convert 0.1 percent to 0.001.
- Step 2: 10,000,000 x 0.001 = 10,000.
- Result: US$10,000 over five years.
- Verification: 1 in 1,000 of 10,000,000 is 10,000; 10,000 / 10,000,000 = 0.001 confirmed.
- ⚠ Check: manual calls this ALE, yet the figure spans five years.
- Annual equivalent would be 10,000 / 5 = US$2,000 per year.
- Verification: 2,000 x 5 = 10,000 matches the five-year figure.
- Exam traps: probability here covers five years, so do not treat result as annual.

### Next phase: recovery strategy metrics
- Next phase selects recovery strategies using RPO, RTO, and MTTR.
- Definition: RTO is the target time to restore a service.
- Definition: RPO is the maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time. 📓
- Definition: MTTR is average time needed to repair a failed item.
- Use: RPO drives backup or replication frequency. 📓
- Audit impact: compare actual backup frequency against stated RPO.
- Detail on RPO, RTO, and MTTR is in section 4.16.

### MTD, RTO, RPO relationships ➕
- Relationship: RTO must be shorter than or equal to MTD. ➕
- Reason: recovery must finish before outage becomes unaffordable.
- RPO is independent of RTO; it measures data, not time offline.
- Lower RTO and RPO values mean higher cost.
- Exam traps: RTO equal to or above MTD means unacceptable losses.
- Exam traps: a short RTO does not guarantee a short RPO.

### Worked example B: RTO against MTD ➕
- Assumed MTD for order system: 48 hours.
- Proposed RTO: 36 hours.
- Test: is RTO ≤ MTD?
- Step 1: 36 ≤ 48, so the target is acceptable.
- Step 2: slack = 48 - 36 = 12 hours.
- A proposed RTO of 60 hours fails: 60 > 48.
- Verification: 36 + 12 = 48 confirms slack; 60 - 48 = 12 hours over MTD confirmed.

### Worked example C: RPO against backup interval
- Backups run every 4 hours; order volume is 500 per hour.
- Formula: worst-case data loss = backup interval (time since last good copy).
- Worst-case loss: 4 hours x 500 orders = 2,000 orders.
- Stated RPO: 2 hours, equal to 1,000 orders.
- Result: 4 hours exceeds 2 hours, so backups fail the RPO.
- Required interval: 2 hours or less.
- Verification: 2,000 / 4 hours = 500 per hour matches; 2 x 500 = 1,000 at compliant interval.
- Exam traps: backup interval sets achievable RPO, not the other way around.

## Takeaway
- BIA sets critical functions, priorities, and RTO/RPO targets. 📓⭐
- Management approves criticality; IT does not decide alone.
- Three questions: processes, supporting resources, critical recovery period.
- Minimize total cost: downtime plus recovery forms a U curve.
- Critical systems have no manual substitute; sensitive systems do.
- RTO must stay within MTD; backup interval must meet RPO. ➕
