## 4.1.0 Overview: what IT operations covers

### Applications and software
- Definition: software that runs the business, including OSs, applications, databases and middleware.
- Use: operations teams select, build, implement and maintain this software.
- Audit impact: auditor reviews change management, testing, documentation and authorization.
- Definition: middleware is software that lets separate applications exchange data.
- Audit impact: interfaces between systems are a common point of data loss.
- ➕ Application controls: user access reviews, interface controls, SoD, backup, encryption.
- ⚠ Check: that exact control list is not stated in the manual intro; verify.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: the auditor assesses whether controls exist and work, not run them.
  - Wrong: auditor approving changes personally. That breaks independence.

### System monitoring and management
- Definition: tools and logs that show system health, events and performance.
- Use: detect issues early, before users or the business feel the impact.
- Audit impact: auditor assesses monitoring effectiveness in detecting and responding to problems.
- Audit impact: auditor also reviews capacity planning and optimization practices.
- Example: a disk fills slowly; an alert fires at 80 percent, preventing an outage.

### Backup and disaster recovery
- Definition: backups copy data; a DRP restores IT after a disruption.
- Definition: data replication keeps a live copy of data at another place.
- Use: protect data integrity and availability; minimize downtime after failure or disaster.
- Audit impact: auditor evaluates incident management effectiveness in detect, report and respond.
- Audit impact: auditor assesses BCP and DRP so critical operations restore promptly.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: a backup is only proven when a restore test succeeds.
  - Wrong: counting backup job completion alone as evidence of recoverability.

### Virtualization and cloud computing
- Definition: virtualization runs many virtual machines (VMs) on one physical host.
- Definition: a hypervisor is software that creates and manages those VMs.
- Definition: IaaS rents infrastructure, PaaS rents a platform, SaaS rents finished software.
- Use: flexibility, scalability and lower cost through shared, resizable resources.
- Audit impact: auditor gets assurance by direct testing or third-party SOC reports.
- Audit impact: cloud customer still owns its data and accountability.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: when direct testing is impossible, rely on independent SOC reports.
  - Wrong: assuming the provider's controls automatically satisfy the customer's obligations.

### Documentation and knowledge management
- Definition: written procedures, troubleshooting guides, process narratives and end-to-end flowcharts.
- Use: smooth transitions, efficient operations and team collaboration.
- Audit impact: documentation must be complete, current and accessible.
- Exam traps
  - Wrong: documentation that exists but is outdated counts as adequate. It does not.

### Regulations and exam scope
- Auditor must know applicable regulations, such as GDPR, HIPAA and industry rules.
- Vendor-specific terminology is not examined unless it is globally generalized.

## 4.1.1 Networking

### Reference model purpose
- Definition: network architecture standards give a reference model for intercomputer communication.
- Use: let different hardware and software communicate despite differing architectures.
- Audit impact: auditor uses the model to locate where a control or device operates.

### Local Area Network (LAN)
- Definition: a LAN covers a small area, from one room to a few buildings.
- Use: connect nearby devices at low cost with high speed.
- Setting up a LAN requires assessing cost, speed, flexibility and reliability.
- Planners also assess physical media and access methods.
- Traffic monitors track volumes, error rates and response times on critical links.
- Audit impact: media type affects capacity, reliability and security.

#### LAN physical media
- Copper twisted-pair
  - Definition: two insulated wires twisted together to cut cross talk.
  - Audit impact: unshielded pairs are vulnerable to electromagnetic interference (EMI).
  - Control: run unshielded cable in dedicated conduits, away from fluorescent lights.
  - Definition: cross talk is signal leakage between adjacent cables.
  - Avoid long parallel cable runs to limit cross talk.
- Fiber-optic
  - Definition: glass fibers carry data as light flashes.
  - Use: preferred for high-volume, long-distance runs.
  - Example: linking floor switches to enterprise data switches, or servers to SANs.
  - Benefits: low transmission loss, immune to EMI, low wiretap risk.
  - Drawback: more fragile; suited where changes are infrequent.
- Radio systems (wireless)
  - Definition: low-powered devices broadcast and receive electromagnetic signals carrying data.

#### LAN topologies and media access
- Definition: topology is the physical layout; protocols define how data is interpreted.
- Bus, ring and star exist; only star is common in new builds.
- Topology is now driven by ease of construction, reliability and practicality.
- Media access technologies give shared access and stop one device monopolizing.

#### LAN components
- Repeater
  - Definition: physical layer device that amplifies or regenerates weakened signals.
  - Use: extend range or join segments; fixes attenuation.
- Hub ➕
  - Definition: shared-media device that repeats traffic to all ports.
  - ⚠ Check: outline omits hubs from LAN components; manual includes them.
  - Audit impact: hubs cannot enforce port security; switches are preferred.
- Bridge
  - Definition: data link device that forwards frames by MAC address.
  - Use: split a segment to reduce collision domains.
  - Can filter frames from predefined MAC addresses.
  - Software-based, so less efficient than switches and rare today.
- Layer 2 switch
  - Definition: data link device forwarding by source and destination MAC address.
  - Uses hardware ASICs, giving low latency and many dedicated high-speed ports.
  - Use: reduces collision domains in Ethernet.
- Router
  - Definition: network layer device that examines IP addresses to direct packets.
  - Linked segments stay logically separate and can act as independent networks.
  - Blocks broadcasts and traffic to unknown addresses; filters by network or host.
  - Audit impact: software-based routers examine every packet and can bottleneck.
  - Control: place routers carefully, use switches and load balancing.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: layer 2 uses MAC addresses; layer 3 uses IP addresses.
  - Wrong: saying a router forwards broadcasts. It blocks them.

#### LAN risks
- Unauthorized changes harm data and program integrity.
- No version control weakens data protection.
- Poor user verification and remote connections expose the LAN externally.
- Virus and worm infection.
- General access instead of need-to-know causes improper disclosure.
- Masquerading as a legitimate LAN user.
- Internal sniffing: gathering addresses or details useful for later attacks.
- Internal spoofing: changing a network address to pose as another.
- No detailed automated logs (audit trails) enabled.
- Destruction of logging and auditing data.

#### LAN security capabilities
- Declare ownership of programs, files and storage.
- Least privilege (POLP) limits access to what the role needs.
- Record and file locking prevents simultaneous updates.
- User ID and password rules cover length, format and change frequency.
- Port security: use switches, not hubs, to block unknown MAC addresses.
- Encrypt local traffic with IPSec.

#### What the auditor documents about a LAN
- Users or groups holding privileged access rights.
- LAN topology and network design.
- LAN administrator or owner, and their functions.
- Distinct groups of LAN users.
- Applications used on the LAN.
- Procedures and standards: design, support, naming conventions and data security.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: first understand and document the environment, then test controls.

### Wide Area Network (WAN)
- Definition: a WAN links geographically dispersed LANs, such as plants, cities and nations.
- WAN characteristics apply to the physical and data link OSI layers.
- Data flow modes
  - Simplex: one direction only.
  - Half duplex: both directions, but one at a time.
  - Full duplex: both directions at once, with no turnaround delay.
- Lines can be switched or dedicated.

#### WAN implementation media
- Fiber-optic carries most high-capacity connections.
- Microwave radio
  - Line-of-sight, so antennae must "see" each other.
  - Rainfall and climate can degrade links.
  - New builds are digital; common on "light routes" where fiber is uneconomic.
  - Example: electrical utilities link SCADA systems by microwave.
- Satellite radio link
  - Uses transponders, each with 36 MHz bandwidth.
  - Weather affects signals.
  - Traditional round-trip delay is about 300 ms.
  - Low-earth orbit networks such as Starlink cut latency under 100 ms.

#### Transmission media comparison
- Twisted pair: cheap and simple; short range under 200 feet.
- Twisted pair weaknesses: easy to tap, cross talk, interference.
- Coaxial: carries data and video; thick, expensive, distance sensitive.
- Fiber: high bandwidth, secure, hard to tap, no cross talk.
- Fiber weaknesses: expensive, hard to splice and modify.
- Radio, microwave, satellite: easy to tap; subject to interference and noise.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: for confidentiality in transit, fiber is the hardest to tap.
  - Wrong: choosing wireless media for sensitive links without encryption.

#### WAN message transmission techniques
- Message switching: whole message stored, then forwarded when a path frees.
- Message switching cost depends on message length.
- Virtual circuit: logical circuit between two devices for reliable communication.
- Switched virtual circuit (SVC): set up on demand.
- Permanent virtual circuit (PVC): always-on connection.

### TCP/IP and its relation to the OSI reference model ⭐

#### OSI basics
- Definition: OSI is a seven-layer conceptual model of how data moves.
- Each layer is self-contained, so one can change without harming others.
- Each layer appears to talk directly to the same layer on the remote system.
- Audit impact: layer knowledge helps place firewalls, encryption and monitoring tools.

#### The seven layers (top to bottom)
- Application (7)
  - Definition: standard interface letting applications reach network resources.
  - Not the same as application software.
- Presentation (6)
  - Transforms data to a standard format.
  - Provides encryption, compression and reformatting.
- Session (5)
  - Establishes, manages and terminates dialogs between computers.
- Transport (4)
  - End-to-end reliable transfer, error recovery and flow control.
  - Acknowledges each packet; unacknowledged packets are resent.
- Network (3)
  - Handles IP addresses, routing and forwarding.
- Data link (2)
  - Builds frames and detects errors using a cyclic redundancy check (CRC).
  - CRC detects all single-bit and most multiple-bit errors.
  - Bad frames are discarded when CRC values do not match.
  - Uses unique MAC addresses assigned at manufacture.
  - MAC addresses can be overridden, but this is not recommended.
  - A promiscuous interface listens to all data, enabling sniffing.
- Physical (1)
  - Sends the bit stream as electrical, optical or radio signals.
  - Defines cables, connectors and cards.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: encryption as a service is presentation layer; IP routing is network layer.
  - Wrong: confusing the application layer with application software.
  - Wrong: assuming the data link layer corrects errors; it detects them.

#### Data traversal
- Data travels down the layers at the sender.
- Each layer adds headers or trailers, like an envelope.
- At the receiver, data moves up and the envelopes are removed.
- OSI concepts guide LANs, WANs, MANs and the Internet.

#### TCP/IP mapping
- TCP/IP predates OSI, so layers do not match one-to-one.
- Application protocols: HTTP, FTP, SMTP, TFTP, NFS, SNMP, Telnet, DNS, DHCP.
- Transport protocols: TCP and UDP.
- Network protocols: ICMP, ARP, RARP, IP.
- Data link protocols: Ethernet, FDDI, Token Ring, PPP.

#### Equipment and data unit per layer
| Layer | Equipment | Data unit |
|---|---|---|
| Application | Gateway | Data |
| Transport | Layer 4 switch | Segment |
| Network | Router, layer 3 switch | Packet |
| Data link | Layer 2 switch, bridge, wireless AP, NIC | Frame |
| Physical | Hub, repeater, NIC | Bits |

- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: match the unit to the layer: segment, packet, frame, bits.

### Network administration and control
- Duties: monitor usage and throughput, and balance loads.
- Duties: react to violations and failures, and save or restore data.
- Duties: scale the network as use grows.
- Management software must be accessible only to the network administrator.
- Audit impact: that software is the network OS on switches and routers.
- Network OS functions: local and remote terminal access.
- Network OS functions: shared file and print services, and links to hosts.
- Network OS user features: transparent access and authorization checks.
- Network OS user features: uniform logon and logging, and online documentation.

#### Network performance metrics
- Latency
  - Definition: delay from source to destination.
  - Caused mainly by device switching and routing, less by distance.
  - Measure easily with the ping command.
- Jitter
  - Definition: variation in latency, measured in milliseconds.
  - High jitter means large delay swings; low jitter means small swings.
- Throughput
  - Definition: useful work per unit time; bytes per second through a channel.
- Quality of service (QoS)
  - Definition: overall network performance from the user's viewpoint.
  - Includes latency, jitter, delay, availability and packet loss.
- Error counts and retransmissions also indicate performance problems.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: latency is delay; jitter is change in delay.
  - Wrong: treating throughput as the same thing as bandwidth capacity.

#### ISO/IEC 10040 network management tasks
- Fault management: detect devices with technical faults.
- Configuration management: view and change device settings remotely.
- Accounting management: record who uses which resources.
- Performance management: monitor usage and alarm on thresholds.
- Security management: detect suspicious traffic or users and alert.

#### Network management tools
- Response time reports
  - Show average, worst and best times over an interval.
  - If slow, investigate I/O bottlenecks, bandwidth and CPU capacity.
- Downtime reports
  - Track availability of lines and circuits.
  - Remedies: add or replace lines, use dedicated links, add backup power.
  - Remedies: improve access controls and monitor utilization for forecasting.
- Online monitors
  - Check transmission accuracy using echo checking and status checking.
  - Echo checking bounces received data back to the sender.
  - Status checking ensures messages are neither lost nor duplicated.
- Network monitors show real-time node status.
- Network (protocol) analyzers
  - Definition: diagnostic tools attached to a link to inspect passing packets.
  - Usually hardware-based; operate at data link or network level.
  - Output: protocols, packet types, traffic volume, errors and bandwidth use.
  - Audit impact: analyzers can expose sensitive data, so restrict their use.
- Simple Network Management Protocol (SNMP)
  - Definition: TCP/IP protocol to monitor and control network variables.
  - A master console polls devices that run SNMP agents.
  - Use: manage configurations and collect performance and security statistics.
  - Audit impact: every managed device needs an agent; weak access exposes configuration.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: excessive downtime points to line, power or capacity remedies first.

### Converged protocols
- Definition: protocols merging proprietary and standard protocols on shared infrastructure.
- Use: reuse existing TCP/IP networks for special services.
- Benefit: simpler security and significant cost savings.
- Risk: more protocols and devices to manage on one infrastructure.
- Fiber Channel (FC)
  - Definition: high-speed, lossless storage network over fiber.
  - Speeds reach 16 Gbps over up to 10 km.
  - Needs dedicated infrastructure, separate from client traffic.
  - Use: block storage and low-latency OLTP databases.
  - Generally more secure than IP-based storage.
- Fiber Channel over Ethernet (FCoE)
  - Definition: FC frames encapsulated over Ethernet.
  - Needs 10 Gbps Ethernet or faster.
  - Reliable and fault tolerant, but costly in switches and arrays.
  - ⚠ Check: manual calls FCoE Layer 3, replacing IP; Layer 2 is likelier.
- Multi-protocol label switching (MPLS)
  - Definition: forwards packets using short labels instead of network addresses.
  - Header is analyzed once; a label then indexes the forwarding table.
  - Label is a short, fixed 20-bit value.
  - Table holds class-of-service values to prioritize traffic.
  - Handles many protocols through encapsulation, not relying on TCP/IP.
- Voice over IP (VoIP)
  - Definition: tunnels voice, video and data over TCP/IP.
  - Cheaper than landline and cellphone calls; can be software-only.
  - Audit impact: voice traffic needs the same security as data traffic.
- iSCSI
  - Definition: SAN protocol sending SCSI commands over TCP/IP.
  - Gives block-level access to storage; low-cost alternative to FC.
  - Initiator: host software or hardware that sends data to storage.
  - Target: system on the storage device hosting the storage resources.
  - Benefits: lower cost, flexibility, efficient transfer, no distance limit.
  - Security: authentication, network isolation, integrity and confidentiality.
  - Easy to configure, so suited to disaster recovery.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: FC is dedicated and more secure; iSCSI is cheaper over IP.
  - Wrong: assuming convergence only reduces risk. It adds complexity too.

### Internet Protocol networking
- IP is connectionless.
- IPv4: 32-bit dotted decimal address; about 4.3 billion addresses.
- IPv6: much larger address space, simpler headers, better mobile fit.
- ⚠ Check: manual says IPv6 gives "infinite" addresses; treat as "vastly larger".
- ⚠ Check: manual's IPv4/IPv6 table columns are garbled; outline matches known facts.
- IPv4 vs IPv6 from a security view
  - Checksum: IPv4 uses header checksum; IPv6 has none.
  - Delivery: IPv4 broadcasts; IPv6 multicasts.
  - Security: IPv4 lacks built-in encryption and authentication; IPv6 provides them.
  - Configuration: IPv4 manual or DHCP; IPv6 adds autoconfiguration and renumbering.
  - IPv6 makes end-to-end integrity achievable.
  - IPv6 flow label field identifies packet flows.
- Transition strategies
  - Dual stacking: both versions run on the same device.
  - Tunneling: IPv6 traffic carried across the IPv4 routing system.
- Audit impact: dual-stacked devices expose two attack surfaces; filter both.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: IPv6 adds built-in security options, but must be configured.

### Network Address Translation (NAT)
- Definition: converts internal IP addresses to public addresses for Internet traffic.
- Use: private networks can reuse addresses without clashing with public hosts.
- Static NAT: permanent one-to-one internal to external mapping.
- Static NAT use: lets an internal web server be reachable from the Internet.
- Dynamic NAT: internal address mapped from a public pool per session.
- NAT with port address translation (NPAT)
  - Many private devices share one public IP.
  - Unique port numbers distinguish each device's traffic.
- Benefits
  - Cost reduction: one leased public IP serves a whole network.
  - Privacy: hides internal addresses and topology from outsiders.
  - Security: only internally initiated connections return.
  - Flexibility and scalability: no renumbering as networks change.
  - Boundary enforcement: traffic is forced through the firewall.
- Limitations
  - Time-consuming to implement; needs large connection tables.
  - Can interfere with IPSec.
  - Some applications stop working.
  - Adds switching path delay.
- Audit impact: NAT hides internal hosts but is not a replacement for a firewall.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: NAT conserves public IPs and hides topology; it is not encryption.
  - Wrong: claiming NAT works seamlessly with IPSec.

### Network devices 📓
- Router 📓
  - Directs traffic between networks using IP addresses.
  - Audit impact: review routing tables for correct segmentation.
- Switch 📓
  - Connects devices within one LAN.
  - ➕ Managed switches create VLANs for internal isolation.
  - ⚠ Check: VLANs are not in the 4.1 manual text; verify in chapter 5.
- Gateway 📓
  - Connects networks using different protocols; performs protocol conversion.
  - Audit impact: a gateway is a critical control point.
  - ⚠ Check: the manual only lists gateways as application-layer equipment.
- Hub, bridge, repeater ➕
  - Repeater: physical layer; regenerates attenuated signals.
  - Bridge: data link layer; filters frames by MAC address.
  - Hub: shared medium; cannot support port security.
- Layer 2 switch: ASIC-based, MAC forwarding, fewer collision domains.
- Layer 3 and layer 4 switches handle routing and segment-level switching.
- Wireless access point is data link layer equipment.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: choose the device by the layer and protocol difference to bridge.
  - Wrong: using a router where only protocol translation is needed; that is a gateway.

## 4.1.2 Computer hardware components and architectures

### Processing components
- Definition: components that process or store data, versus input/output (I/O).
- CPU variants
  - Microprocessor: CPU on a single chip.
  - Multi-processor: more than one CPU.
  - Multi-core: several CPUs on one chip.
- CPU parts
  - Arithmetic logic unit (ALU): performs mathematical and logical operations.
  - Control unit: circuits that direct all computer operations.
  - Internal memory (registers): used for processing transactions.
- Other components: motherboard, RAM, ROM, power supply, cooling and the OS.
- Wireless parts: Wi-Fi, cellular radio (LTE, 5G) and Bluetooth.
- Permanent storage
  - HDD: spinning disks with a moving read/write head.
  - SSD: flash memory, no moving parts, faster and more durable.
- GPU
  - Definition: processor that relieves the CPU of graphics-heavy work.
  - Use: machine learning, AI and high-performance computing on servers and cloud.

### Input and output components
- Input-only: keyboard and mouse.
- Input and output: touchscreen.
- Output-only: printer.

### Types of computers
- Supercomputers: highest speed; dedicated to specialized calculations.
- Mainframes
  - Shared by thousands of internal or external users.
  - Run batch and real-time programs; often a proprietary OS.
  - Long protected by early security and control tools.
- High-end and midrange servers
  - Partitioning and CPU management resemble mainframes at lower cost.
  - High-end often UNIX database servers; smaller often Windows file or print.
- Personal computers: single-user workstations, usually networked.
- Thin-client: minimal hardware; processing happens on the server.
- Laptops
  - Battery tolerates power failure.
  - Audit impact: theft risk, for stored data or LAN access.
- Next unit of computing (NUC): small-form desktop or edge device.
- Single-board computer (SBC)
  - Complete computer on one board; fixed RAM, no expansion slots.
  - Example: Raspberry Pi.
- Smartphones, tablets and handhelds can sync with PCs for backup or transfer.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: thin clients centralize processing, which simplifies control and data protection.

## 4.1.3 Common enterprise back-end devices
- Definition: servers and appliances delivering application services in a distributed environment.
- IoT growth brings data leakage and privacy risk; see chapter 5.
- Print servers
  - Use: consolidate printing across sites and domains for cost savings.
- File servers
  - Use: central repositories controlled with an access-control matrix.
  - Audit impact: review access rights against need-to-know.
- Application (program) servers
  - Host programs, process business logic and talk to databases.
  - Consolidating licenses enables central management and better security.
- Web servers
  - Serve web pages to customers and staff, reached through URLs.
- Database servers
  - Store data as a repository, not present it.
  - Application and web servers turn that data into usable information.
- Data loss prevention (DLP) gateway
  - Definition: device inspecting traffic leaving and entering the organization.
  - Use: identify sensitive data and block exfiltration attempts.
- Appliances
  - Definition: single-purpose devices, smaller, faster and more efficient than servers.
  - Examples: firewalls, IDS, IPS, switches, routers, VPNs, load balancers.
  - Audit impact: appliances cannot run other services, so patch and configure them carefully.

### Proxy servers
- Definition: a server acting on a user's behalf as an intermediate link.
- Forward proxy
  - Sits between client and Internet.
  - Use: caching, traffic filtering and anonymizing client IP.
- Reverse proxy
  - Sits between Internet and web server.
  - Use: load balancing, security, hiding server IPs and caching.
- Open proxy
  - Definition: proxy usable by anyone on the Internet.
  - Audit impact: avoid; abused for phishing and spam.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: forward proxy protects clients; reverse proxy protects servers.
  - Wrong: assuming a reverse proxy hides client addresses.

## 4.1.4 USB mass storage devices
- Definition: removable USB storage, such as flash drives, external HDDs and SD cards.
- Features: hot swapping, plug-and-play and bus power.
- Features: usually no vendor-specific drivers are needed.
- SD cards may need a card reader in a USB port.

### Risks
- Malware vector: viruses, spyware and keyloggers travel on transferred files.
- Data theft from unattended, unlocked PCs with USB ports.
- Data and media loss; unencrypted lost drives expose contents.
- Data corruption when removed without telling the computer first.
- Compatibility issues across USB versions can corrupt transfers.
- Physical damage from small size and portability.
- Loss of confidentiality; lost patient data may violate HIPAA.
- Example: an employee loses an unencrypted drive holding patient records.

### Security controls
- Encryption
  - Data is useless without the key.
  - Data stays exposed if unencrypted on the network or workstation.
- Granular control: centrally block mass storage yet allow mice and keyboards.
- Technology alone is not enough; policies, procedures and awareness are needed.
- Educate security personnel; drives are easy to conceal.
- Enforce lock-desktop policy in higher-risk environments.
- Antivirus must scan all attached drives and removable media.
- Allow only secure (encrypted) devices, enforced by central software.
- Return information file: omit company details; add a legal disclaimer.
- Read-only switch on drives used with untrusted machines blocks malware writes.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: strongest single control is central port control plus mandatory encryption.
  - Wrong: relying on encryption alone; workstation copies remain unprotected.

## 4.1.5 Wireless communication technologies
- Wi-Fi: radio-wave technology for high-speed Internet access.
- Bluetooth: short-distance device-to-device connection.
- RFID
  - Definition: tags on objects that readers identify and track by radio.
  - Use: asset tracking, inventory management and access control.
- Advantages: convenience, mobility and scalability.
- Audit impact: wireless is easier to intercept; see 5.9 for Wi-Fi and Bluetooth detail.
- Exam traps
  - Wrong: treating convenience benefits as reducing risk; wireless widens exposure.

## 4.1.6 Hardware maintenance program
- Definition: scheduled cleaning and servicing, documented, following vendor specifications.
- Scope includes environmental hardware: temperature, humidity, fire protection and power.
- Information maintained
  - Reputable service company for each resource.
  - Maintenance schedule and cost.
  - Performance history: planned versus unplanned, executed, exceptional.
- Management documents and justifies deviations from vendor specifications.

### Auditor steps
- Verify scope and objectives match business needs; note gaps.
- Remote sites may lack equal vendor coverage; assess that risk.
- Ensure a formal plan is management-approved and followed.
- Identify costs over budget; overages may signal non-adherence or coming change.

### Hardware monitoring reports
- Availability reports
  - Show periods the computer is available; focus on downtime.
  - Causes: weak hardware, OS maintenance, poor environment, inadequate training.
- Hardware error reports
  - Identify CPU, I/O, power and storage failures.
  - Check for intermittent or recurring errors; diagnosis is not always easy.
- Asset management reports: inventory of network-connected equipment.
- Utilization reports
  - Document use of machines and peripherals.
  - Mainframe-class utilization typically averages 85 to 95 percent.
  - Brief peaks to 100 percent and dips below 70 percent are tolerated.
  - Trends predict future resource needs.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: sustained low or maxed utilization signals capacity-planning problems.

## 4.1.7 Hardware reviews

### Hardware acquisition plan
- Aligned with business requirements, enterprise architecture and IS plans.
- Compared regularly with business plans to stay synchronized.
- Acquisition criteria developed.
- Environment adequate for current and new hardware.
- Specifications, installation needs and lead time documented.

### Acquisition of hardware
- Requests carry a cost-benefit analysis.
- Purchases go through purchasing, avoiding duplication and ensuring tender compliance.
- Purchases match the approved acquisition plan.
- Written policy statements are issued and communicated to users.
- Approval forms and procedures exist.

### IT asset management
- Hardware tagged, owner designated, location recorded.
- Contracts and SLAs retained.

### Capacity management and monitoring
- Monitoring plan uses historical data, trouble logs and job accounting reports.
- Continuous review of hardware and system software capacity.
- Equipment that auto-contacts the manufacturer on failure is monitored.

### Preventive maintenance schedule
- Vendor-recommended frequency observed.
- Maintenance in off-peak periods, not during critical processing.
- Backup hardware flexible enough to allow maintenance.

### Availability, utilization, problem logs
- Reviewed for availability meeting workload and user needs.
- Management reviews malfunctions, reruns, abnormal terminations and operator actions.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: acquisition should trace to approved plan and cost-benefit analysis.
  - Wrong: buying outside purchasing to speed delivery; it bypasses control.

## Takeaway
- Know which OSI layer each device and protocol operates at.
- Audit by documenting the environment first, then testing controls.
- Fiber is most secure medium; wireless and copper are easier to tap.
- USB risk needs central control, encryption, awareness and antivirus together.
- Hardware reviews trace acquisition, maintenance and capacity back to approved plans.
- Rely on SOC reports when direct cloud testing is not possible.
