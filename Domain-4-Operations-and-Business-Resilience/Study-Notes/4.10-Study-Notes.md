## 4.10 Introduction: ITSM basics

### IT service management (ITSM)
- Definition: managing IT as a set of linked processes that serve the business.
- Use: organizes IT work around services the business actually receives.
- Audit impact: auditor checks that processes exist, interlock and are measured.
- Processes are highly interdependent, so a weak one degrades the others.
- ITSM focuses on business deliverables, not on technology for its own sake.
- It covers infrastructure management for applications that deliver IT services.
- Goal: fine-tune services as business demand changes.
- Goal: show better service quality with lower cost over the long term.
- Service management metrics should be captured and analyzed to improve quality.
- Example: an email service managed as one service, not as separate servers.

### Service level agreement (SLA) and operational level agreement (OLA)
- Definition (SLA): agreement describing the services IT delivers to its customer.
- Use: narrows the gap between customer expectations and services offered.
- Audit impact: the SLA is the yardstick for judging service performance.
- Definition (OLA): internal agreement covering services that support IT's own delivery.
- Use: lets internal teams back up commitments made in the SLA.
- Audit impact: a missing OLA can leave SLA commitments with no internal support.
- Exam traps
  - SLA faces the customer; OLA is internal between IT teams.
  - An OLA is not a contract with an external vendor.

### Service flow: help desk to change to configuration
- Step 1: help desk classifies the complaint and stores it as an incident.
- Step 2: help desk searches the known error database (KEDB) for a solution.
- Definition (KEDB): repository of known problems and their documented workarounds.
- Use: speeds incident resolution by reusing proven fixes.
- Audit impact: auditor checks the KEDB is current and actually used.
- Step 3: repeated or major incidents escalate to problem management.
- Step 4: any needed change goes through change management.
- This applies whether the change comes from a problem, an enhancement or anything else.
- Change management reviews cost-benefit and feasibility before approval.
- Change risk is studied, and a fallback plan is prepared.
- Change management invokes configuration management.
- Definition (configuration management): tracks configuration items and their versions.
- A change may touch one configuration item or several.
- Versions can be kept at system, program or module level.
- Policy may require a new version for any system-level change.
- Example: a software suite has systems, programs and modules, each versioned separately.
- Audit impact: unapproved or untracked changes break the link between SLA and reality.
- Exam traps
  - All changes pass through change management, even problem fixes.
  - A fallback plan is required, not optional.

### Frameworks
- Definition (ITIL): detailed, hands-on framework for IT service management.
- Use: delivers operational service management based on business value.
- Audit impact: auditor benchmarks processes against ITIL practices where adopted.
- Definition (ISO 20000): international standard for ITSM.
- Use: lets in-house, outsourced or external IT show alignment with business needs.
- Audit impact: certification gives evidence, but scope must still be checked.
- Exam traps
  - ITIL is a framework of practices; ISO 20000 is a standard.

## 4.10.1 Service level agreements (SLA) 📓

### What an SLA is
- Parties: an IT organization and a customer, which is the business.
- The IT organization may be internal or an external provider.
- Example: internal IT supplies email and ERP; an external provider supplies hosting.
- An SLA describes services in nontechnical terms, from the customer's viewpoint.
- During the agreement period, it is the standard for measuring services.
- It is also used to adjust the services during that period.
- Audit impact: auditor confirms the SLA is understandable and measurable.

### Service-level management
- Definition: defining, agreeing, documenting and managing required, cost-justified service levels.
- Use: maintains and improves customer satisfaction and delivered service.
- Audit impact: levels must be cost-justified, not just maximal.
- It covers more than the SLAs themselves.
- Service catalog: produced and maintained as part of the process.
- Service review meetings: held to examine performance.
- Service improvement plans (SIPs): created for areas missing their SLAs.
- A clear service level lets IT design service and customers monitor it.
- Missed SLAs require improvement by the provider.
- Penalties: the provider may pay a fine or reduce billing.
- Exam traps
  - Service-level management includes catalogs, reviews and SIPs, not only SLA documents.
  - Penalties depend on contract terms, so check the contract wording.

### SLA characteristics
- Service characteristics defining an SLA: accuracy, completeness, timeliness and security.
- Audit impact: auditor checks all four are defined, not only timeliness.

### Monitoring tools: exception reports
- Definition: automated reports listing applications that failed or malfunctioned.
- Use: shows how well IT personnel deliver service.
- Audit impact: a high exception count signals deeper control weaknesses.
- Possible causes of excess exceptions follow.
- Poor understanding of business requirements.
- Poor application design, development or testing.
- Inadequate operating instructions.
- Inadequate operations support.
- Inadequate operator training or performance monitoring.
- Inadequate sequencing of tasks.
- Inadequate system configuration.
- Inadequate capacity management.

### Monitoring tools: system and application logs
- Definition: records of abnormal and normal events on systems and applications.
- Use: identifies application problems and activity performed on computers.
- Audit impact: logs are too large to review manually.
- Analysis software lets the auditor run tests over the logs.
- Test: only approved programs access sensitive data.
- Test: only authorized IT personnel access sensitive data.
- Test: data-altering utilities are used only for authorized purposes.
- Test: approved programs run only when scheduled; no unauthorized runs.
- Test: the correct data file generation is used for production.
- Test: data files are adequately protected.

### Monitoring tools: operator reports and schedules
- Definition (operator problem reports): manual logs of operations problems and their resolutions.
- Use: management reviews whether operator actions were appropriate.
- Audit impact: reveals training needs.
- Definition (operator work schedules): manual schedules supporting human resource planning.
- Use: ensures proper staffing, especially in critical or heavy-use periods.
- Audit impact: schedules should allow cross-training and emergency staffing.
- Exam traps
  - Exception reports are automated; operator problem reports are manual.

### Chargeback, outsourcing and measures
- SLAs are often tied to chargeback systems.
- Definition (chargeback): apportioning IT cost to the end-user department.
- Use: links service consumption to cost.
- Third-party delivery of IT functions requires an outsourcing SLA.
- Service levels often include hardware and software performance targets.
- Example: user response time and hardware availability.
- Measures may also cover financial performance, such as yearly cost reduction.
- Measures may cover human resources: planning, turnover, training.
- Measures may cover risk management, such as compliance with control objectives.
- Audit impact: measures must include risk, security and control.
- Audit impact: measures must also include efficiency and effectiveness.
- Exam traps
  - A performance-only SLA is incomplete without risk and control measures.
  - Outsourcing does not remove the need for an SLA; it creates it.

## 4.10.2 Monitoring of service levels

### Regular monitoring
- Appropriate management must monitor defined service levels regularly.
- Purpose: confirm IS operations objectives are achieved.
- Also review the impact on customers and other stakeholders.
- Example: a bank tracks ATM availability and customer satisfaction after outages.

### Availability calculation
- Formula: allowed downtime = (100% minus availability%) x total period.
- Step 1: hours per year = 365 x 24 = 8,760.
- Step 2: allowed unavailability at 99.9% = 100% - 99.9% = 0.1%.
- Step 3: downtime = 0.001 x 8,760 = 8.76 hours per year.
- Verification: 8.76 hours x 60 = 525.6 minutes, about 8.8 hours; 99.9% of 8,760 is 8,751.24, leaving 8.76 hours.
- Step 4: at 99%, downtime = 0.01 x 8,760 = 87.6 hours.
- Step 5: 87.6 / 24 = 3.65 days, about three and a half days.
- Verification: 87.6 hours is ten times 8.76, matching the tenfold gap between 99% and 99.9%.
- ⚠ Check: the manual says 99.9% allows about three and a half days yearly.
- ⚠ Check: that figure fits 99%; 99.9% allows about 8.8 hours.
- Exam traps
  - Each extra "nine" cuts allowed downtime tenfold.
  - Always compute from the percentage; do not trust remembered figures.

### Outsourced services
- Monitoring is essential when a third party serves the organization's customers directly.
- Failure affects the organization more than the third party.
- Example: fraud from a third-party control weakness damages the organization's reputation.
- Only responsibility for service provision is outsourced.
- Accountability stays with the organization.
- Audit impact: auditor determines how management gains assurance on third-party controls.
- Exam traps
  - Outsourcing transfers tasks, never accountability; "transferred risk" answers are wrong.
  - Reviewing SLA metrics alone does not prove third-party controls operate effectively. 📓

### Assurance techniques
- Questionnaires: management collects self-reported control information from the provider.
- Onsite visits: management inspects the third party's operations directly.
- Independent assurance reports: a third-party auditor's report on provider controls.
- Examples named: SSAE 18 SOC 1 report; AT-101 (SOC 2 and SOC 3).
- Audit impact: independent reports usually carry more weight than self-assessment.
- ⚠ Check: manual pairs SOC 2 and SOC 3 with AT-101; ➕ verify numbering.
- Exam traps
  - SOC 1 relates to financial reporting controls; SOC 2 and 3 cover other criteria. ➕

## 4.10.3 Service levels and enterprise architecture

### Enterprise architecture (EA)
- Definition: structured design of how business, data and technology fit together.
- Use: aligns service delivery across the organization.
- Audit impact: architecture should reflect operational and recovery requirements.
- Delivery channels: mobile apps, the Internet, service outlets, third parties, kiosks.
- Channels use different technologies.
- All channels are serviced by the same backend database.
- EA best aligns operational requirements with service delivery objectives.
- Unacceptable recovery time leads to fault-tolerant, high-availability architecture.
- Applies to critical service delivery channels.
- Example: a shared backend outage stops mobile, web and kiosk service together.
- Exam traps
  - Recovery time too long means upgrade the architecture, not relax the SLA.

## Takeaway
- ITSM runs IT as linked processes; SLAs bind them to business expectations.
- SLAs use customer language and cover accuracy, completeness, timeliness and security.
- Exceptions, logs, operator reports and schedules are the monitoring tools.
- Recompute availability: 99.9% allows about 8.8 hours yearly, not days.
- Outsourcing moves responsibility; accountability and assurance duties stay with the organization.
- Unacceptable recovery time points to fault-tolerant, high-availability architecture.
