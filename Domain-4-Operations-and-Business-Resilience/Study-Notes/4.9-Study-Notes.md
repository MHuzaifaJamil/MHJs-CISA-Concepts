## 4.9 Basics: What Operational Logs Are

- Log (audit trail)
  - Definition: A recorded history of activities, system events, and user interactions.
  - Use: Monitoring, investigating incidents, and proving accountability.
  - Audit impact: Without logs, the auditor cannot trace who did what.
- Operational log management
  - Definition: Collecting, monitoring, analyzing, and storing logs from systems, applications, and devices.
  - Use: Gives visibility into the IT environment and supports compliance.
  - Audit impact: Auditor evaluates whether this program exists and works.
- Log monitoring as a compensating control
  - Definition: Reviewing logs to offset a control that cannot be implemented.
  - Use: Small organizations cannot fully separate duties with limited staff.
  - Audit impact: Auditor confirms logs are actually reviewed for abuse of elevated access.
  - Example: A two-person IT team reviews admin activity logs weekly instead of segregating duties.
- Log record contents
  - Definition: Fields that make an event traceable.
  - Use: Establishes type, time, location, source, and outcome of the event.
  - Use: Also establishes identity of individuals, subjects, or objects involved.
  - Audit impact: Missing fields weaken accountability and forensic value.
- Exam traps
  - Monitoring logs is a detective or compensating control, not a preventive one.
  - Compensating means the primary control is impossible, not merely inconvenient.

## 4.9.1 Types of Logs

- Why many types exist
  - Nearly every network component can generate its own kind of log.
  - Auditor picks the log type that matches the control being tested.
- Event log
  - Definition: Records traffic, use, login attempts, failed passwords, and application events.
  - Use: Often a detective control after breaches occur.
- Server log
  - Definition: Text record of one server's activity during a period.
- System log
  - Definition: Records OS events: startup, changes, unexpected shutdowns, errors, warnings.
  - Example: Windows system log showing a USB removed without ejecting.
- Access log
  - Definition: Lists people or bots accessing applications or files.
  - Use: Compensating control when separation of duties is not feasible.
- Change log
  - Definition: Chronological list of changes to an application or file.
  - Use: Part of change management; shows authorized people made modifications.
  - Audit impact: Compare change log entries to approved change tickets.
- Availability log
  - Definition: Tracks performance, uptime, and availability.
  - Use: Determines whether SLAs were met.
- Resource log
  - Definition: Shows connectivity issues and capacity limits.
  - Use: Administrators check the environment is sized properly.
  - Audit impact: Can be an early indicator of a network breach.
- Threat log
  - Definition: Records traffic matching a predefined firewall security profile.
  - Use: Triggers alerts as an early warning for breaches.
- Database log
  - Definition: Tracks inserts, updates, and deletions of records.
  - Use: Supports integrity, troubleshooting, structure review, and security assessment.
- Error log
  - Definition: Produced when a system has a problem.
  - Use: Details help resolve the event.
- Firewall log
  - Definition: Records inbound and outbound traffic crossing the firewall.
  - Use: Identifies potential threats.
- Operating system log
  - Definition: Shows production data file versions, scheduled programs, and utility or service ID use.
  - Use: Confirms OS integrity was not harmed by improper parameter or library changes.
- Application log
  - Definition: Shows application efficiency and whether changes followed authorization.
  - Use: Detects unauthorized access and suspicious user behavior.
- Access control log
  - Definition: Evaluates controls over critical files, databases, programs, and security facilities.
  - Use: Covers communications systems, DBMSs, and applications.
- Protecting log integrity
  - Intruders often alter logs to hide their activity.
  - Secure logging preserves evidence authenticity for legal or court use.
  - Common solution: capture and centralize logs on a secure server using SIEM.
  - Audit impact: Logs kept only on the source host are unreliable evidence.
- Exam traps
  - Threat log means firewall profile matches; resource log hints at capacity or breach.
  - Availability log answers SLA questions; change log answers authorization-of-change questions.
  - Best protection from tampering is centralization, not stronger local admin passwords.
- ⚠ Check: Outline says event logs are "detective control"; manual says "often", not always.

## 4.9.2 Log Management

### Purpose and Cycle

- Purpose of log management
  - Definition: Ensuring availability, integrity, and confidentiality of log data.
  - Use: Supports IT operations, security, and compliance.
  - Audit impact: Logs must be actively monitored as a control for a specific risk.
- Six-step log management cycle
  - Definition: Manual presents a six-step cycle in a figure (4.19).
  - ⚠ Check: Figure text is illegible in the source; step names unverified. ➕
  - Steps discussed in the text: collection, alerts, storage, analysis, reporting, integration. ➕

### Three-Tier Infrastructure

- Tier 1: Log generation
  - Definition: Hosts that produce log data.
  - Use: They send logs over networks to tier-two servers.
  - Use: Some hosts let servers authenticate and retrieve log copies instead.
- Tier 2: Log analysis and storage
  - Definition: Log servers receiving logs or copies from many hosts.
  - Use: Transfer is real time or in scheduled batches.
  - Use: Servers receiving from many sources are called collectors or aggregators.
  - Use: Data may sit on log servers or separate database servers.
- Tier 3: Log monitoring
  - Definition: Consoles for reviewing log data and automated analysis results.
  - Use: Generating reports and managing log servers and clients.
  - Audit impact: Console privileges should follow least privilege (POLP).
- Retention
  - Definition: Keeping logs for a defined period.
  - Use: Supports follow-up investigations and attacks undetected for long periods.
  - Use: Complete logs show when, how, what was accessed, and exfiltration.
  - Audit impact: Retention too short destroys evidence of slow attacks.

### Data Collection

- Sources
  - Servers, network devices, databases, operating systems, and applications.
- Collection methods
  - Many systems have built-in logging.
  - Collection agents or centralized solutions cover systems lacking built-in logging.
- Minimum content
  - Timestamp: when the action occurred.
  - User: who took the action.
  - Event: what action was taken.
  - ⚠ Check: Outline marked this ➕; the manual does state this minimum.
- Exam traps
  - A log lacking user identity cannot support accountability, even with timestamps.

### Generating Alerts

- Alert
  - Definition: A notification sent to people when a log shows a possible problem.
  - Use: Lets people take immediate action.
- Alert routing
  - Different logs alert different people or teams.
  - Example: A syslog event may alert only the end user.
  - Example: A threat log alerts the IT security team about attempted breach.
- Auditor's checks
  - Alerts are generated using appropriate parameters.
  - Alerts are actually received.
  - Action is taken promptly, based on alert criticality.
- Exam traps
  - Alerts generated but never acted on is a control failure.

### Storing and Protecting Logs

- Protection goal
  - Prevent unauthorized access, modification, or deletion of log data.
- Centralized log management system
  - Definition: One system collecting and storing logs from many sources.
  - Use: Easier analysis, correlation, and monitoring.
- Retention policy
  - Based on regulatory requirements and business needs.
- Encrypted storage and offsite backups
  - Protect confidentiality and integrity of log information.
  - Offsite or cloud backups add redundancy and disaster recovery.
- Role-based access control
  - Only authorized personnel access logs, per role and responsibility.
- Automated backup schedule
  - Prevents log loss from hardware failure, crashes, or other incidents.
- Exam traps
  - Retention length is set by regulation and business need, not storage convenience.
  - System administrators of monitored hosts should not control their own logs.

### Analyzing Log Data

- Two analyses in log monitoring
  - First: immediate threat assessment, with real-time alerting on thresholds or patterns.
  - Second: deeper analysis across correlated logs for trends, anomalies, and breaches.
- Log parsing
  - Definition: Extracting relevant fields from log entries into a consistent format.
  - Use: Regular expressions and parsing libraries commonly do this.
- Normalization
  - Definition: Standardizing different log formats, timestamps, and data elements.
  - Use: Allows comparison across multiple sources.
- Event correlation
  - Definition: Finding relationships between events from different logs.
  - Use: Reveals patterns and dependencies indicating incidents or anomalies.
  - Use: SIEM systems provide this capability.
  - Example: Failed logins on many hosts, then one success, then large data transfer.
- Pattern matching
  - Definition: Searching logs for predefined patterns.
  - Use: Detects known attack signatures or error conditions.
- Statistical analysis
  - Definition: Applying math to find outliers, trends, and abnormal behavior.
  - Use: Mean, median, standard deviation, and time-series analysis.
  - Example: A user's nightly data volume far above their normal standard deviation.
- Exam traps
  - Correlation across sources needs normalized formats and synchronized timestamps.
  - Real-time alerting handles urgency; deep analysis finds slow, subtle patterns.

### Reporting Concerns

- Documented reporting and escalation procedures
  - Define who is notified, how to escalate, and which channels to use.
- Severity classification
  - Definition: Rating incidents by criticality using predefined criteria.
  - Use: Prioritizes response and allocates resources.
- Incident response plan
  - Includes roles, responsibilities, and communication protocols.
  - Includes containment, mitigation strategies, and post-incident analysis.
- Prompt reporting
  - Delays can cause further damage to systems and data.
- Report content
  - Source of concern, affected systems or assets, potential impact.
  - Actions taken or recommended, in clear language for technical and non-technical readers.
  - Recommended containment, mitigation, and remediation plans.
- Follow-up and stakeholder communication
  - Action plans include follow-up and ongoing status updates to stakeholders.
- Regulatory and legal requirements
  - Reporting must adhere to applicable requirements.
  - Document concerns, actions, and outcomes for audit and compliance.
- Exam traps
  - Report as soon as identified; waiting for full analysis is wrong.

### Timestamp Synchronization 📓⭐

- Definition: Keeping clocks on all log-producing systems aligned to one time source.
- Use: Lets events from different systems be ordered correctly.
- Audit impact: Unsynchronized clocks break forensic reconstruction and incident timeline analysis.
- Example: Servers differing by minutes make attack sequence across hosts unreliable.
- Exam traps
  - Primary impact is inaccurate forensic reconstruction, not storage overhead or indexing failure.
  - Out-of-sync clocks do not cause automatic deletion of events.
- ⚠ Check: Manual mentions timestamp normalization but not clock sync explicitly; user's note stands. 📓

### Integration with SIEM and IT Governance

- SIEM versus log management
  - Definition: SIEM adds multi-source integration, real-time monitoring, correlation, and threat detection.
  - Use: Log management stores and analyzes logs; SIEM builds on it.
  - Use: Log management can be standalone or a component of SIEM.
  - Audit impact: Logs are the foundation data source for SIEM.
- Governance frameworks
  - COBIT and ITIL: log management aligns with these best-practice frameworks.
- Incident management
  - Logs support detection, diagnosis, and resolution.
  - Logs reveal root cause and sequence of events before an incident.
- Problem management
  - Logs show recurring incidents and systemic patterns.
  - Supports root cause analysis and permanent fixes.
- Change management
  - Logs verify change success and unexpected consequences.
  - Review logs before, during, and after changes.
- Service monitoring
  - Logs produce uptime, response time, error rate, and SLA compliance reports.
- Configuration management
  - Logs give an audit trail of configuration changes and support reconciliation.
- Knowledge management
  - Log entries signal need for knowledge articles, troubleshooting guides, and FAQs.
- Operational log management as an IT control
  - Provides visibility, effective monitoring, prompt anomaly detection, and compliance.
- Exam traps
  - SIEM extends log management; it does not replace the need for good logs.

## Takeaway

- Logs establish who, what, when, where, and outcome of events.
- Centralize logs on a secure server to resist intruder tampering.
- Auditor verifies alerts fire, reach people, and get acted upon.
- Synchronized timestamps are essential for forensic timelines.
- Normalize, correlate, then report by severity under documented procedures.
- Log monitoring can compensate when segregation of duties is impossible.
