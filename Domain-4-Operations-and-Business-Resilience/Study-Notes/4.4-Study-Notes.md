## 4.4 System Interfaces (basics)

### Core terms
- System
  - Definition: components, hardware and software, working together to run computers.
  - Use: delivers the processing that business applications depend on.
  - Audit impact: interfaces link systems, so one weak link affects many.
- System interface
  - Definition: passes one application's output to another as input.
  - Use: moves data with little or no human interaction.
  - Audit impact: errors spread automatically because nobody reviews each transfer.
- User interface
  - Definition: the screen or tool where a human interacts with a system.
  - Use: lets people enter and view data.
  - Audit impact: interfaces involving humans are usually called user interfaces, not system interfaces.
- Interfaces work across different programming languages and developers.
  - Use: organizations choose best-fit applications yet still share data.
  - Audit impact: mixed technologies complicate testing, mapping and reconciliation.

### Transfer categories
- System-to-system
  - Definition: data moves between two systems, internal or external.
  - Use: moves financial data across business cycles and feeds analytics.
  - Audit impact: a defect repeats on every run, so errors become pervasive.
  - Example: general ledger exports journal data nightly to a business intelligence tool.
- Business intelligence and analytics feeds
  - Definition: data copied from a repository into an analysis tool.
  - Use: data mining, reporting and visualization for insight.
  - Audit impact: bad source or transfer data produces wrong management insight.
- Partner-to-partner
  - Definition: two or more partner organizations interface directly.
  - Use: partners exchange data continuously, for example in a supply chain.
  - Audit impact: confidential data and partner weaknesses reach your environment.
  - Example: several suppliers share design data to build one complex part.
- Person-to-person
  - Definition: humans exchange data by email attachment or shared drive.
  - Use: quick ad hoc sharing without special tooling.
  - Audit impact: hardest to observe, manage, secure and control.
  - Example: finance emails a payroll spreadsheet to an outside bureau.
- Exam traps
  - Person-to-person is the least controlled category, not the most automated.
  - Interface means little human interaction; do not confuse it with user interface.

## 4.4.1 Risk associated with system interfaces

### Risks by category
- System-to-system risk
  - Definition: interface faults that corrupt or drop data between systems.
  - Audit impact: pervasive errors if not corrected quickly.
- Partner-to-partner risk
  - Definition: exposure of data or cyber weakness through a partner.
  - Audit impact: confidential data may reach an unauthorized third party.
- Person-to-person risk
  - Definition: exposure from manual handling of data.
  - Audit impact: human error, security exposure and privacy issues may go uncaught.

### Data accuracy and compliance
- Accuracy and completeness of exchanged data must be reliable.
- Inaccurate reports harm research, financial, performance and competitive decisions.
- Even a small error can create compliance liability.
- Extracted data must equal data recorded in the recipient system.
  - Audit impact: any mismatch means integrity failure in transit.
- Organizations should track interfaces centrally and keep audit trails for regulations.

### Data security and availability
- Unauthorized access can occur by interception, malicious activity or error.
- Examples of access failures
  - Elevated shared-drive access granted to the wrong person.
  - Phishing attacks on staff handling transfers.
  - Access allowed through insecure third parties.
- System outages can leave interfaces without data.
  - Audit impact: incomplete reports may be produced, harming reliability.
- Exam traps
  - Data must be protected in transit, not only at rest in each system.
  - Outages affect accuracy and completeness, not only availability.

## 4.4.2 Controls associated with system interfaces

### Interface tracking program
- Interface management program
  - Definition: a managed process that tracks all interfaces and transfers.
  - Use: gives visibility of internal, external and ad hoc transfers.
  - Audit impact: auditor confirms it exists and matches business needs and goals.
- Managed file transfer (MFT) system
  - Definition: commercial or custom tool that controls file transfers.
  - Use: central point to schedule, secure and log transfers.
  - Audit impact: unlogged ad hoc transfers bypass the control.
- Application programming interface (API)
  - Definition: defined call that lets one program request data from another.
  - Use: modern alternative to file transfers between applications.
  - Audit impact: API calls also need logging, signing and authentication.
- Capabilities the auditor should expect
  - Manage multiple transfer mechanisms and protocols, or APIs.
  - Encrypt, decrypt and electronically sign files and API calls.
  - Compress and decompress files.
  - Connect to database servers and support email transfers.
  - Automate regular transfers and handle interruptions.
  - Analyze, track and report data attributes.
  - Support regulatory compliance.
  - Integrate with back-office applications.
  - Authenticate and authorize users.
  - Log required events.
- ⚠ Check: manual lists capabilities twice, once with and once without APIs and signing.

### Reconciliation of sent versus received data
- Reconciliation
  - Definition: comparing data sent with data received.
  - Use: proves completeness and accuracy across the interface.
  - Audit impact: without it, silent loss or alteration goes undetected.
- Methods
  - Automated controls are generally preferred.
  - Manual: compare a report of sent data to one of received data.
  - Cryptographic hash checksums can compare sender and recipient automatically.
  - Example: software generates control totals at extraction and reconciles after loading.
- Monitoring
  - A qualified person should review reconciliation results.
  - Audit impact: reviewer must be able to spot material differences.

### Encryption and nonrepudiation
- Encryption
  - Definition: scrambling data so only key holders can read it.
  - Use: protects data when interception risk is relatively high.
  - Audit impact: auditor checks encryption fits each use and risk.
  - Example: industrial espionage, identity theft or card data theft.
- Strong access and authentication controls may also be needed.
- Files may be password-protected.
- Nonrepudiation (as used in this section)
  - Definition: assurance that the intended recipient is the actual recipient.
  - Use: prevents data going to, or being denied by, the wrong party.
  - Audit impact: lack of it allows misdelivery and later disputes.
- ⚠ Check: nonrepudiation usually means proof of origin; this section stresses recipient.

### Audit trail and logs
- Audit trail
  - Definition: a record of who did what and when.
  - Use: supports investigation, accountability and regulatory evidence.
  - Audit impact: missing fields weaken evidence and incident reconstruction.
- Capture these items
  - Sender and send time.
  - Receive time.
  - Data format, for example xls, csv, txt or xml.
  - Transfer method.
  - Recipient.
- Assess automated server logs along the transfer path.
  - Audit impact: external transfers touch many internet hosts, raising exposure.

### External market data feeds 📓⭐
- External market data feeds need strict inbound authentication and verification. 📓⭐
  - Definition: third-party data streams, such as prices, flowing into your systems.
  - Audit impact: forged or tampered feed data could drive wrong decisions.
- Missing authentication is more critical than missing encryption or quality monitoring. 📓
- ⚠ Check: this ranking and the market-feed point are not in the manual section.
- Rationale: unauthenticated source cannot be trusted, so its data quality is moot. ➕
- Exam traps
  - Pick the control whose absence lets untrusted data enter, not the most visible one.
  - Encryption protects confidentiality; it does not prove the sender's identity.
  - Reconciliation detects mismatch; it does not replace authentication or logging.
  - A one-time transfer review does not cover ad hoc transfers.

## Takeaway
- Interfaces move data automatically, so one fault becomes pervasive.
- Person-to-person transfers are least controlled; track ad hoc ones too.
- Reconcile sent to received data; automate, and have a qualified reviewer.
- Encrypt where interception risk is high; log sender, times, format, method, recipient.
- Authenticate inbound external feeds first; this outranks encryption and quality checks. 📓
