# 4.5 End-User Computing and Shadow IT: Study Notes

## 4.5 Basics: EUC versus Shadow IT

- **End-user computing (EUC)**
  - Definition: Non-IT staff create and manage their own technical resources.
  - Use: Users do tasks without direct IT involvement.
  - Audit impact: IT normally governs and manages EUC through an official process.
- **Shadow IT**
  - Definition: Technology bought or installed outside the official process, unknown to IT.
  - Use: Departments get tools quickly, bypassing normal channels.
  - Audit impact: IT cannot protect or monitor what it does not know exists.
- Shadow IT arises when users go around the official EUC process.
- Example: A sales team buys a cloud tool with a credit card.
- Exam traps
  - Official EUC is governed by IT; shadow IT is not.
  - Shadow IT is defined by missing approval, not by the technology used.

## 4.5.1 End-User Computing

### Basic terms

- **End user**
  - Definition: Person who uses applications built, serviced and installed by others.
  - Use: Normal consumers of business applications.
  - Audit impact: Distinct from EUC creators, who build their own tools.
- **End-user computing (applied)**
  - Definition: Users, usually non-programmers, design their own applications with software products.
  - Use: Spreadsheets and small databases built by business staff.
  - Audit impact: Such applications often skip independent review.
- Example: A finance analyst builds a pricing model in Excel.
- **End-user support manager**
  - Definition: Role acting as liaison between IT department and end users.
  - Use: Channels user needs and standards between both sides.
  - Audit impact: Evidence that IT oversight of EUC actually exists.

### Benefits and inherent risk

- Benefit: users build and deploy applications quickly.
- Benefit: reduces pressure on the IT department.
- Benefit: organization responds faster to markets, regulations and consumer interests.
- Risk: applications may lack independent review.
- Risk: often built without a formal development methodology.
- ⚠ Check: Manual says most EUC applications pose no significant enterprise risk.
- Risk depends on criticality, so management must define criteria.

### Security risks

- **Missing authorization**
  - Definition: No mechanism controls who may access the application.
  - Use: Normally restricts access by role.
  - Audit impact: Anyone with file access may read or change data.
- **Missing authentication**
  - Definition: No mechanism verifies who the user is.
  - Use: Normally proves user identity.
  - Audit impact: Actions cannot be attributed to individuals.
- **Audit logging unavailable**
  - Definition: Standard tools, such as Excel and Access, may not record activity.
  - Use: Logs support accountability and investigation.
  - Audit impact: Changes are untraceable, weakening evidence and detective controls.
- ⚠ Check: Manual says logging "may not be available"; it is not absolute.
- **Unencrypted sensitive data**
  - Definition: Sensitive content stored without encryption or other protection.
  - Use: Encryption protects confidentiality.
  - Audit impact: Lost or shared files expose data directly.
- **Data loss from no backups**
  - Definition: Applications are not properly backed up.
  - Use: Backups enable recovery.
  - Audit impact: Business-critical calculations may be unrecoverable.
- **Compliance risk**
  - Definition: Applications may store sensitive or private information.
  - Use: Regulated data needs defined controls.
  - Audit impact: Uncontrolled storage may breach legal or contractual duties.
- **Monitoring challenges**
  - Definition: Applications missing from IT inventories.
  - Use: Inventory drives monitoring and oversight.
  - Audit impact: Unlisted applications are never monitored or audited.
- **Excluded from maintenance and patching**
  - Definition: Applications and databases omitted from IT maintenance schedules.
  - Use: Patching removes known vulnerabilities.
  - Audit impact: Known weaknesses remain open indefinitely.
- Example: An Access database holds customer data, no password, no backup.
- Exam traps
  - Root cause is lack of IT oversight, not the tool itself.
  - Best first fix is policy and inventory, not banning EUC.

### Controls

- Management defines risk criteria to determine application criticality.
- An EUC use policy should exist; the auditor verifies this.
- Apply data classification to EUC applications.
- Maintain an inventory of all EUC applications.
- Manual links this inventory to section 4.2, IT Asset Management.
- Critical EUC applications get the same controls as other applications.
- Audit impact: Unlisted critical applications are a top finding.
- Exam traps
  - Criticality, not who built it, decides the control level.
  - A critical spreadsheet needs full change, access and backup controls.
  - Policy alone is weak without inventory and classification.

## 4.5.2 Shadow IT

### Basics

- **Shadow IT (detailed)**
  - Definition: Unvetted, unapproved systems, services, hardware or software on enterprise infrastructure.
  - Use: Collaboration, software development, content sharing, data storage and processing.
  - Audit impact: Not reviewed, tested, approved or secured under written policies.
- Drivers: low-cost cloud software procurable within minutes.
- Drivers: powerful smartphones familiar to users.
- Drivers: work from home networks.
- Drivers: personal hardware.
- Example: Staff share files through a personal cloud storage account.
- Shadow IT can drive disruption and innovation.
- It can also expose the enterprise to significant risk.

### Managing benefits and risk

- Step: identify whether an IT upgrade or digitalization program is needed.
- Step: determine the need for controls.
- Step: apply appropriate controls.
- Step: reassess regularly; controls are not a one-time activity.
- Controls depend on enterprise type, size and risk appetite.
- Audit impact: Verify controls still exist and operate effectively.
- Exam traps
  - Banning all shadow IT is weaker than channeling needs through IT.
  - Shadow IT may signal unmet business need worth addressing.

### Typical controls

- **Shadow IT policy**
  - Definition: Policy aligned with business objectives and supporting security requirements.
  - Use: Sets rules for acquiring and using technology.
  - Audit impact: Verify it exists, is communicated and enforced.
- **IT as service-delivery organization**
  - Definition: Culture of supportive, consultative IT and business relationships.
  - Use: Reduces motive to bypass IT.
  - Audit impact: Cultural control; look for engagement and responsiveness evidence.
- **IT budgeting and procurement approval**
  - Definition: IT reviews and approves all IT-related purchases.
  - Use: Stops unapproved technology at the buying stage.
  - Audit impact: Compare purchases against the approved IT inventory.
- **IT system consolidation**
  - Definition: Fewer providers, networks, platforms, devices and media; consolidated applications and data centers.
  - Use: Shrinks technology footprint and eases data management.
  - Audit impact: Applied only if feasible.
- **Explicit user access and administrative rights**
  - Definition: Admin and data access rights are assigned explicitly.
  - Use: Unassigned users cannot freely install new applications.
  - Audit impact: Review local admin rights and software installation ability.
- **User education**
  - Definition: Formal program for personnel in all business units.
  - Use: Raises awareness of risks and approved channels.
  - Audit impact: Check coverage, content and attendance records.
- **User activity monitoring**
  - Definition: Recording and monitoring what users do.
  - Use: Detects unapproved tools and risky behavior.
  - Audit impact: Verify logs are reviewed and acted upon.
- **User data exchange controls**
  - Definition: Strong endpoint controls over how users move data.
  - Use: Limits data leaving through unapproved tools.
  - Audit impact: Test endpoint restrictions on external transfer.
- Exam traps
  - Preventive controls such as procurement approval beat after-the-fact detection.
  - Technical blocking alone fails without policy and education.
  - Controls must be reassessed, not set once.

## Takeaway

- EUC is IT-governed; shadow IT bypasses IT entirely.
- Main EUC risks come from missing IT oversight and inventory.
- Control EUC by criticality, policy, classification and inventory.
- Critical EUC applications deserve the same controls as other applications.
- Manage shadow IT through policy, procurement, access rights, education and monitoring.
- Reassess shadow IT controls regularly; they are never one-time.
