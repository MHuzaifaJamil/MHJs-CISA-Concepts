## 1.5 Overview: the audit as a project

### Audit process basics
- **Audit process**
  - Definition: the ordered work of gathering evidence and judging controls.
  - Use: turns an audit request into an objective report for management.
  - Audit impact: skipping steps weakens the conclusion and the auditor's credibility.
- Three core duties: gather evidence, evaluate control strengths and weaknesses, report objectively.
- Evaluation rests on evidence from audit tests, never on opinion alone.
- The report presents issues: control weaknesses plus remediation recommendations.
- Planning is the necessary first step of an effective IS audit.
- Auditors assess overall risk for the general and application areas first.
- Then they build an audit program: objectives plus procedures meeting those objectives.

### Audit management
- **Audit management**
  - Definition: the people who run the audit function and its resources.
  - Use: ensures staff, schedule and follow-up reviews exist.
  - Audit impact: no schedule or follow-up means findings never get fixed.
- Follow-up reviews check corrective actions, mainly for internal IS audit.
- ⚠ Check: Outline says follow-up generally. Manual ties follow-up scheduling to internal audit.

### Audit steps
- **Audit steps**
  - Definition: the fixed sequence of activities inside any audit.
  - Use: gives a repeatable path from scope to report.
  - Audit impact: an out-of-order step makes evidence or conclusions unreliable.
- Step 1: define the audit scope.
- Step 2: formulate audit objectives.
- Step 3: identify audit criteria.
- Step 4: perform audit procedures.
- Step 5: review and evaluate evidence.
- Step 6: form conclusions and opinions.
- Step 7: report to management after discussing with key process owners.
- **Audit criteria**
  - Definition: the standard the subject is measured against.
  - Use: lets the auditor call something a deviation.
  - Audit impact: without criteria, a finding is only an opinion.
  - Example: a password policy requiring twelve characters is the criterion for testing settings.

### Project management techniques
- **Project management techniques**
  - Definition: tools for planning and controlling the audit like a project.
  - Use: keeps the engagement inside time, budget and scope.
  - Audit impact: poor control causes missed scope or overrun budgets.
- Plan the engagement: plan the audit considering project-specific risk.
- Build the audit plan: chart tasks across a timeline, optimizing resources.
- Estimates must be realistic and account for auditee availability.
- Execute the plan: carry out tasks against the plan.
- Monitor activity: compare actual progress with planned audit steps.
- Monitoring lets challenges be managed early and scope finish on time.
- Example: an auditor sees testing slipping two weeks and reallocates a colleague.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: plan, schedule, execute, then monitor against plan.
  - Wrong: treating monitoring as an end-of-audit task.

## 1.5.1 Audit objectives

- **Audit objective**
  - Definition: the purpose of the audit, stating what must be concluded.
  - Use: drives scope, tests and the evidence needed. 📓
  - Audit impact: vague objectives lead to irrelevant tests and unsupported opinions.
- Objectives often confirm controls exist to minimize business risk.
- They also confirm controls function as expected.
- Objectives include legal and regulatory compliance.
- Objectives include confidentiality, integrity, reliability and availability of information and IT resources.
- ⚠ Check: Manual says "reliability" alongside the CIA terms. Outline shows only CIA.
- Management may hand the auditor a general control objective to evaluate.
- **Translating objectives**
  - Definition: converting broad business objectives into specific IS audit objectives.
  - Use: makes the audit testable in the IT environment.
  - Audit impact: determining objectives is a critical step in planning.
  - Example: finance wants correct ledger posting; IS objective is edit checks.
- **Control objective and key control points**
  - Definition: a control objective states the aim; key controls achieve it.
  - Use: initial review identifies key controls, then decides whether to compliance-test them.
  - Audit impact: testing non-key controls wastes effort and misses risk.
- Identify key control points after understanding and documenting business processes.
- Identify both key general controls and key application controls.
- **Substantive testing via CAATs**
  - Definition: substantive testing confirms data accuracy; CAATs are computer-assisted audit techniques.
  - Use: assess integrity of financial reporting data.
  - Audit impact: CAATs test whole datasets faster than manual review.
- ⚠ Check: Manual says auditors "may assist" with this. It is optional, not mandatory.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: objectives come first; scope, tests and evidence follow from them.
  - Wrong: choosing tools or sample sizes before the objective is set.
  - Wrong: assuming the objective is only about technical controls.

## 1.5.2 Audit phases

- **Audit phases**
  - Definition: major stages of an audit, each split into key steps.
  - Use: organizes work from planning to follow-up.
  - Audit impact: each phase leaves work papers that support the final report.
- ⚠ Check: Outline lists "plan, define, perform, report". Manual figure 1.11 shows three phases.
- The three figure phases: planning, fieldwork/documentation, reporting/follow-up.
- ➕ "Define" is a step inside planning, not a separate phase.

### Planning
- **Planning phase**
  - Definition: groundwork that fixes what, why, how far and with what.
  - Use: sets objectives, scope and methodology. 📓
  - Audit impact: poor planning misallocates resources and misses key risks.
- Risk assessment is the first step of planning. 📓⭐
- ⚠ Check: Manual figure lists subject, objective, scope, then pre-audit risk assessment.
- Manual calls risk assessment critical for setting final scope of risk-based audits.
- Manual calls it good practice, not required, for compliance audits.
- Identify control objectives after selecting the business process. 📓⭐
- ⚠ Check: Manual says identify key controls after documenting processes. Wording differs from outline.
- **Functional walk-through**
  - Definition: tracing one transaction through a process to see how it works.
  - Use: understand the business process and spot key controls. 📓⭐
  - Audit impact: it builds understanding; it does not prove control effectiveness.
  - Example: following a purchase order from request to payment.
- ⚠ Check: Manual 1.5 never defines the walk-through; support is my notes.
- Allocate resources once subject, objective and scope are known.
- Planning step 1: determine audit subject (function, system or location).
- Planning step 2: define audit objective, the purpose of the audit.
- Example: objective is whether source code changes happen in a controlled environment.
- Planning step 3: set audit scope.
- **Audit scope**
  - Definition: the boundary of what the audit will cover.
  - Use: limits to specific systems, function, unit or time period.
  - Audit impact: a clear scope defines testing points, skills and resources needed.
- Example: scope limited to one application and one quarter of program changes.
- Planning step 4: perform pre-audit planning.
- Run a risk assessment to set the final scope.
- Interview the auditee about areas of concern for inclusion.
- Identify regulatory compliance requirements.
- Define resources: technical skills, budget, locations, team roles, time frame.
- Identify information sources: flowcharts, policies, standards, procedures, prior work papers.
- Set administrative points of contact.
- Build a communication plan: whom to inform, when, how often, why.
- Planning step 5: determine audit procedures and data-gathering steps.
- Obtain departmental policies, standards and guidelines for review.
- List individuals to interview.
- Select methods and tools for evaluation.
- Develop test scripts.
- Set criteria for evaluating the test.
- Define how to verify test accuracy and repeatability.
- ⚠ Check: Regulatory identification appears in steps 4 and 5; manual does too.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: risk assessment drives resource allocation and final scope.
  - Wrong: starting with detailed testing before understanding the process.
  - Wrong: picking controls to test before documenting the process.
  - Wrong: treating the walk-through as a control effectiveness test.

### Fieldwork / documentation
- **Fieldwork phase**
  - Definition: where evidence is collected, controls tested and results recorded.
  - Use: produces the evidence base for findings.
  - Audit impact: weak documentation leaves findings indefensible.
- Step 1: acquire data.
- **Advance request list**
  - Definition: list of evidence and interviews requested before fieldwork.
  - Use: speeds collection and clarifies auditee responsibilities.
  - Audit impact: avoids delays that compress testing time.
- Collect evidence securely, for example through a fileshare.
- A GRC tool (governance, risk and compliance) can ease data collection.
- Step 2: test controls.
- Techniques: interviews, observations, inspections.
- Sampling may be needed to review a subset of a population.
- Example: sample servers and observe antimalware installed per policy.
- Step 3: discover and validate issues.
- **Issue**
  - Definition: a deviation from expected audit outcomes, such as policy.
  - Use: basis for recommendations to management.
  - Audit impact: unvalidated issues risk wrong or embarrassing findings.
- Seek auditee explanations before concluding. 📓
- Perform root cause analysis before reporting. 📓⭐
- Confirm material findings with additional evidence. 📓⭐
- ⚠ Check: These three come from my notes; manual 1.5 only says validate issues.
- Step 4: document results in the audit program and work papers.
- Documentation follows the organization's audit documentation standards.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: gather more assurance and find root cause before recommending.
  - Wrong: recommending process redesign or stopping operations on first observation.
  - Wrong: just documenting and presenting a finding unverified.

### Reporting / follow-up
- **Reporting phase**
  - Definition: communicating validated results and tracking remediation.
  - Use: delivers findings to management and oversight.
  - Audit impact: no follow-up means reported risks remain open.
- Discuss findings with auditee management to gain agreement. 📓⭐
- Then report formally (details in 1.9).
- Step 1: gather report requirements before drafting.
- Requirements come from internal audit standards or external reporting rules.
- Step 2: draft the report.
- IS audit leadership reviews the draft before the auditee does.
- Draft includes overall results, potential findings and recommendations.
- Auditee reviews and responds with planned remediation actions before finalization.
- Step 3: issue the report.
- Retain the final report per internal or external requirements.
- Present it to the oversight function, such as the audit committee.
- Step 4: follow up on management's remediation progress.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: validate and agree facts with management, then issue.
  - Wrong: sending a draft directly to the audit committee first.
  - Wrong: issuing before the auditee has responded with planned actions.

## 1.5.3 Audit programs

- **Audit program (work program)**
  - Definition: step-by-step audit procedures and instructions for one assignment.
  - Use: based on the scope and objective of that assignment.
  - Audit impact: identifies scope, objectives and procedures for sufficient, reliable evidence.
- Purpose: formally document procedures and their sequence.
- Purpose: make procedures repeatable for other internal or external auditors.
- Purpose: record testing type, compliance and/or substantive.
- Purpose: satisfy accepted audit standards on planning.
- **Evaluation perspectives**
  - Definition: lenses for judging IT functions and systems.
  - Use: security, quality, fiduciary, service and capacity.
  - Audit impact: choosing the wrong lens skews procedures.
- Security means confidentiality, integrity, availability.
- Quality means effectiveness and efficiency.
- Fiduciary means compliance and reliability.
- **General audit procedures** (typical order)
  - Obtain and record understanding of the audit area.
  - Create risk assessment, general audit plan and schedule.
  - Perform detailed planning with steps and a timeline.
  - Conduct a preliminary review of the audit area.
  - Evaluate the audit area.
  - Verify appropriateness of controls against control objectives.
  - Perform compliance testing.
  - Perform substantive testing.
  - Report results.
  - Follow up when relying on an internal audit function.
- **Compliance testing**
  - Definition: tests that controls are implemented and consistently applied.
  - Use: checks control operation.
  - Audit impact: failure shifts the auditor toward more substantive testing.
- **Substantive testing**
  - Definition: tests confirming information accuracy.
  - Use: verifies data and balances.
  - Audit impact: gives direct evidence of misstatement.
- ➕ Deeper treatment of compliance versus substantive belongs to 1.6.
- **Minimum skills to develop a program**
  - Know the enterprise and industry to categorize risk and threat.
  - Understand the IT space and affecting technologies.
  - Understand how business risk relates to IT risk.
  - Hold basic risk assessment knowledge.
  - Know IS control testing procedures and best method per control.
- **Testing methods**
  - GAS (generalized audit software) surveys data files such as logs and access lists.
  - Specialized software assesses operating systems, databases and parameter files.
  - Flowcharting documents business processes and automated controls.
  - Audit logs and reports evaluate parameters.
  - Documentation review checks written support.
  - Inquiry and observation gather understanding.
  - Walk-throughs trace a process end to end.
  - Reperformance means the auditor repeats the control independently.
  - Example: recalculating an interest accrual to confirm the system output.
- Procedures per control objective. ➕
- ⚠ Check: Not found in manual 1.5. Treat as practical advice, verify before relying.
- Manual cross-references ITAF 1204 and guideline 2204 (performance and supervision).
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: program matches scope and objective, documents testing type.
  - Wrong: one generic program for every audit regardless of risk.

## 1.5.4 Audit work papers

- **Work papers**
  - Definition: documented record of audit plans, programs, tests, findings and incidents.
  - Use: bridge between audit objectives and the final report.
  - Audit impact: support and traceability make findings defensible.
- Format and media may vary by department needs.
- Traceability must run from objectives to report and back.
- The audit report itself counts as a work paper.
- Protect the integrity of test evidence to preserve its value.
- Apply the same security requirements being assessed to the work papers.
- Reason: reports and papers may hold sensitive information useful to attackers.
- Set a retention and destruction process based on legal requirements per audit type.
- Work papers document evidence, tests and conclusions.
- Supervisory review of work papers. 📓
- ⚠ Check: Not stated in 1.5.4. Manual only references ITAF 1204 performance and supervision.
- Example: a manager signs off on tested samples before the report issues.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: papers link objective, procedure, evidence, conclusion; protect and retain legally.
  - Wrong: destroying papers after the report issues, ignoring legal retention.
  - Wrong: leaving papers unsecured because they are internal documents.

## 1.5.5 Fraud, irregularities and illegal acts

- **Fraud**
  - Definition: intentional deception for gain, often by misusing systems or controls.
  - Use: auditors must recognize its possibility and indicators.
  - Audit impact: missing fraud indicators breaches due professional care.
- Management is primarily responsible for controls that deter or detect fraud.
- Controls can fail through exploited vulnerabilities.
- Controls can fail through management-perpetrated weaknesses.
- **Collusion**
  - Definition: two or more people cooperating to defeat a control.
  - Use: explains why segregation of duties can still fail.
  - Audit impact: auditors cannot rely on controls alone.
- Controls reduce but never completely eliminate fraud.
- The auditor must know fraud indicators and apply due professional care.
- Auditors watch for exploitation of vulnerabilities and control override in IT systems.
- Auditors may come across fraud indicators during regular assurance work.
- After careful evaluation, the auditor communicates the need for detailed investigation to appropriate authorities.
- Suspected fraud: collect sufficient evidence first. 📓⭐
- ⚠ Check: Manual says "careful evaluation", not "collect evidence"; notes supply that logic.
- Then report to appropriate management.
- Major fraud or high risk: audit management considers promptly informing the audit committee.
- Auditors must know legal requirements on fraud detection procedures and reporting.
- Manual references ITAF 1207 and guideline 2207 (irregularity and illegal acts).
- Example: unusual vendor payments appear; auditor evaluates, gathers support, informs audit management.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: evaluate and evidence first, then escalate through the proper line.
  - Wrong: reporting a bare suspicion directly to the audit committee.
  - Wrong: confronting the suspect or conducting a forensic investigation personally.
  - Wrong: assuming strong controls rule out fraud.

## 1.5.6 Agile auditing 📓

- **Agile auditing**
  - Definition: audit work in short, iterative cycles. 📓
  - Use: deliver faster, more efficient audits showing stakeholder value.
  - Audit impact: speed must not reduce objectivity or evidence quality.
- Planning, execution and review repeat in short cycles. 📓
- **Sprint**
  - Definition: time-boxed segment focused on specific risks. 📓
  - Use: delivers a small slice of the audit quickly.
  - Audit impact: scope is revisited every sprint.
- A continuous feedback loop runs with stakeholders. 📓
- Agile flexes in rapidly changing environments. 📓
- Traditional auditing is linear, structured and rigid. 📓
- ⚠ Check: Manual mentions two-week sprint cycles only as an example.
- Origin: Agile comes from software development, replacing the formal Waterfall model.

### Agile values
- Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.
- Working software over comprehensive documentation.
- Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.
- Responding to change over following a plan.
- Documentation is kept to the bare minimum required.

### Effect on planning and fieldwork
- Agile blurs or removes the strict gap between planning and fieldwork.
- Tasks run in parallel.
- Example: auditees prepare user lists while auditors finalize audit program steps.
- Example: auditors analyze collected data while waiting for planning meetings.
- This removes major bottlenecks.

### Benefits
- Reduced end-to-end planning: weeks or days instead of months.
- Streamlined engagements: planning, fieldwork and reporting combined.
- Direct collaboration: customers join the daily standup scrum.
- **Daily standup scrum**
  - Definition: short daily team meeting.
  - Use: gives auditees a seat at the table.
  - Audit impact: customer input guides engagements; independence must stay intact.
- Flexible scope: adjust as new information appears.
- Auditors still obtain audit management approval for scope changes.
- Real-time assurance: share findings as testing finds them.
- This replaces waiting for a draft report near the end.
- Frequent plan updates: revisit backlog and annual plan more often.
- Traditional plans review annually; Agile plans review quarterly or more often.

### Agile compared to ITAF standards
- 1002 Organizational Independence: direct communication reflects independence, with no conflicts.
- 1003 Objectivity: not compromised; skepticism and final decisions retained.
- 1005 Due Professional Care: backlog prioritized more often; management retains right to conclude.
- 1006 Proficiency: sprints and standups develop staff; collaboration teaches the business.
- 1402.3 Follow-up and risk acceptance: frequent communication ensures executives learn accepted risks.
- ITAF 1402.3 trigger: accepted risk exceeds risk appetite, discuss with senior management.
- Guideline 2001.2.6 QA: sprint retrospective reviews the last sprint's delivery.
- **Sprint retrospective**
  - Definition: team review of how the last sprint went.
  - Use: covers people, interactions, processes, tools and definition of "done".
  - Audit impact: supports quality assurance and continuous improvement.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: Agile adds speed and collaboration but never removes independence.
  - Wrong: claiming Agile skips documentation or evidence requirements.
  - Wrong: claiming findings wait for a final report in Agile.
  - Wrong: assuming scope changes need no approval.

## Takeaway

- Plan first: risk assessment sets resources and final scope.
- Validate findings with evidence and root cause before reporting.
- Agree facts with auditee management before the formal report.
- Work papers link objectives to report; protect and retain them.
- Suspected fraud: evaluate and gather evidence, then escalate properly.
- Agile speeds audits but keeps independence, objectivity and approvals.
