## 1.7.0 Evidence

### Evidence basics
- Definition: any information an auditor uses to judge whether criteria are met.
- Use: supports audit conclusions and findings.
- Audit impact: no evidence means no defensible conclusion or finding.
- Conclusions must rest on sufficient, relevant and competent evidence.
- Example: asking "is access removed on exit?" and testing real leaver records.
- Plan evidence type, purpose and reliability level during audit planning.

### Sufficient, reliable, relevant and useful
- Definition (sufficient): enough quantity to support the conclusion.
- Definition (relevant): linked to the audit objective being tested.
- Definition (reliable): trustworthy because of its source, nature and timing.
- Definition (useful): helps the auditor meet the audit objectives.
- Use: these four words are the quality checklist for any evidence item.
- Audit impact: weak on any one dimension weakens the whole conclusion.
- ⚠ Check: the manual names sufficient, relevant and competent, not "useful".
- ⚠ Check: the manual does not list "useful" as a separate evidence quality.
- Keep "useful" as an outline addition, but rely on the manual's three terms.

### Appropriate, sufficient and competent (IFAC terms)
- Definition (appropriate): the quality of evidence.
- Definition (sufficient): the quantity of evidence.
- Definition (competent): evidence that is both reliable and relevant.
- Use: auditors judge quality and quantity together before concluding.
- Audit impact: audit judgment decides when sufficiency is reached.
- Example: ten reliable samples may outweigh one hundred unreliable screenshots.
- Exam traps
  - Competent means reliable plus relevant, not merely "a lot".
  - Quantity alone never fixes poor quality.
  - Quality alone may still leave the sample too small.

### Types of evidence
- Auditor's observations, presented to management.
- Interview notes taken during inquiry.
- Results of independent and qualified third-party assessors.
- Correspondence, internal documents and contracts with external partners.
- Results of audit test procedures.
- Audit impact: all types help, but their reliability levels differ.

### Reliability hierarchy ⭐
- Definition: a ranking of evidence types from most to least trustworthy.
- Use: choose stronger evidence where the risk is higher.
- Audit impact: relying on weak evidence risks a wrong conclusion.
- Practical order: external, objective, original, recent beats internal, judgmental, copied.
- ⚠ Check: the manual gives determinants of reliability, not a formal ranked list.
- ⚠ Check: treat the ranking as a study aid derived from the four determinants.

### Determinants of reliability
- Four determinants: independence, qualifications, objectivity and timing.

#### Independence of the provider
- Definition: evidence from outside the audited organization is more reliable.
- Use: confirmation letters verify accounts receivable balances.
- Audit impact: internal providers may have bias or an interest in the result.
- Signed external contracts are reliable only if originals are available.
- Example: a bank's direct reply beats a copy handed over by the client.
- Exam traps
  - Photocopies from the auditee do not equal original external documents.
  - Management assurance is internal, so it ranks low.

#### Qualifications of the provider
- Definition: the skill and responsibilities of the person giving evidence.
- Use: weigh the answer by the speaker's competence and role.
- Audit impact: applies to insiders, outsiders and the auditor too.
- An auditor lacking technical understanding may produce unreliable test results.
- Example: a helpdesk clerk explaining database encryption settings is weak evidence.

#### Objectivity of the evidence
- Definition: objective evidence needs little judgment or interpretation.
- Use: prefer direct review over opinion.
- Audit impact: judgment-based evidence is easier to dispute.
- Example: directly reviewing a media inventory is objective evidence.
- Example: staff opinions on application efficiency are not objective evidence.

#### Timing of the evidence
- Definition: the period when evidence exists or can be retrieved.
- Use: shapes nature, timing and extent of compliance and substantive testing.
- Audit impact: evidence may vanish if not collected promptly.
- Example: uncontrolled spreadsheets change, and lack backups, so old states vanish.
- Exam traps
  - Dynamic data needs early capture; do not postpone collection.

### Focus on overall audit objectives
- Some evidence is relevant; some is only peripheral.
- Audit impact: stay focused on objectives, not on evidence volume.
- 📓 Suspected fraud: gather sufficient evidence before escalating.
- 📓 Confirm material findings with additional evidence before reporting.
- Exam traps
  - First step on suspicion is more evidence, not immediate accusation.
  - Escalating on weak evidence risks unfair or wrong conclusions.

## 1.7.1 Techniques

- Note: candidates must pick the best technique for a given scenario.
- Techniques differ in strength; re-performance is the strongest.

### Reviewing IS organization structures
- Definition: examining how IT roles and reporting lines are arranged.
- Use: judge whether the structure gives adequate segregation of duties (SoD).
- Audit impact: adequate SoD is a key general control.
- Distributed processing and end-user computing may change classic structures.
- Classic structure separates systems and operations functions.
- Example: developers who also deploy to production signal weak SoD.

### Reviewing IS policies and procedures
- Definition: checking written rules and the steps that implement them.
- Use: confirm they exist, are understood and are followed.
- Audit impact: unused policies provide no control assurance.
- Management owns formulating, documenting, promulgating and controlling policies.
- Periodic reviews check policies stay appropriate.
- Exam traps
  - Existence alone is not enough; test understanding and compliance too.
  - Auditors verify management's responsibility; they do not write the policies.

### Reviewing IS standards
- Definition: mandatory technical or process specifications the organization adopts.
- Use: first understand the standards already in place.
- Audit impact: standards form the yardstick for later compliance checks.
- ⚠ Check: the manual gives this item only one sentence.

### Reviewing IS documentation
- Definition: reading records describing how systems are built and run.
- Use: first understand the existing documentation, paper or electronic.
- Audit impact: electronic documents need integrity controls, which the auditor evaluates.
- The auditor looks for a minimum level of documentation.
- Typical documents
  - Feasibility studies and other initiating documents.
  - External application supplier documentation.
  - SLAs with IT providers.
  - Functional requirements and design specifications.
  - Test plans and reports.
  - Program and operations documents.
  - Program change logs and histories.
  - User and operations manuals.
  - Security plans and risk assessments.
  - Business continuity plans (BCPs).
  - Quality assurance (QA) reports.
  - Security metrics reports.
- ⚠ Check: the manual specifies SLAs with external IT providers, not any provider.
- Definition (CASE): computer-aided software engineering tools that build systems.
- Use (CASE, prototyping): they may automate documentation or remove traditional documents.
- Audit impact: still look for documentation standards and practices.

### Inquiry and interviewing
- Definition: asking appropriate people questions to gather evidence.
- Use: organize in advance, with clear objectives communicated.
- Use a fixed outline, with an interview form or checklist.
- Document the interview with notes.
- Never be accusatory; make people comfortable to share information.
- Verify note accuracy with the interviewee.
- Audit impact: interview evidence is weak alone; corroborate it.
- Exam traps
  - Interview answers rank below objective, independently obtained evidence.
  - Confrontational questioning reduces openness and evidence quality.

### Observation of personnel performing their duties
- Definition: watching people and processes in real operation.
- Use: confirm who actually performs a function and how.
- Audit impact: shows real practice versus documented practice.
- Be unobtrusive and document everything in sufficient detail.
- Interim report: issue one if the final report is not timely enough.
- Photographs can support evidence.
- Example: a photograph of a server room with doors fully open.
- What observation identifies
  - Actual functions: the authorized person is the person doing the job.
  - Actual processes and procedures: shows deviations, useful for physical controls.
  - Security awareness: shows practice of preventive and detective measures.
  - Reporting relationships and SoD: confirms assigned duties are really separated.
- Compare observation results with logical access rights.
- Drawback: the observer may interfere with the environment.
- Staff who know they are watched may change usual behavior.
- Exam traps
  - Observation proves practice only at one moment in time.
  - Pair it with log or access-right review for stronger assurance.

### Inspection
- Definition: examining records, documents or physical assets directly.
- Use: verify existence and condition of items or documents.
- Audit impact: more objective than asking people about them.
- ⚠ Check: the manual names inspection only in a list of techniques.
- ⚠ Check: the manual gives no separate definition or example for inspection.

### Re-performance and recalculation
- Definition: the auditor actually performs the control again, in real time.
- Use: applied when inquiry, observation and examination give insufficient assurance.
- Audit impact: generally better evidence than the other techniques.
- Example: re-running an interest calculation and comparing it with the system output.
- Exam traps
  - Strongest technique does not mean first choice; use it when lesser ones fall short.
  - Inquiry alone never proves a control operates effectively.
- ⚠ Check: the manual says "reperformance" and "generally" better; "recalculation" is the outline's wording.

### Walk-through 📓
- Definition: tracing one transaction through a process step by step.
- Use: confirm the auditor's understanding of controls.
- Helps the control owner and auditor share the same understanding.
- Helps identify evidence to collect for validating control effectiveness.
- Audit impact: a walk-through alone does not prove control effectiveness.
- Example: following one purchase order from request to payment.
- Exam traps
  - Walk-through confirms understanding; testing confirms operating effectiveness.
  - Choose walk-through early in planning, not as the final proof.

### Confirmation
- Definition: obtaining a direct written reply from an independent third party.
- Use: verify balances or facts, such as receivables.
- Audit impact: external source makes it highly reliable.
- Example: a customer confirms its outstanding invoice balance to the auditor.
- ⚠ Check: the manual cites confirmation letters, but gives no section for confirmation.

### Examination includes testing of controls
- Definition: examination covers testing controls and audit evidence.
- Audit impact: an audit is more than review work.
- Test results are themselves audit evidence.
- Exam traps
  - A document review alone is not a full audit examination.

---

## Takeaway
- Competent evidence equals reliable plus relevant; sufficiency is quantity.
- Outside, objective, original and timely evidence is most reliable.
- Walk-throughs confirm understanding; re-performance best tests operating effectiveness.
- Interviews and observation are useful but weak alone; corroborate them.
- Collect enough evidence before escalating suspicions or reporting findings.
- Manual does not rank evidence; the hierarchy is a derived study aid.
