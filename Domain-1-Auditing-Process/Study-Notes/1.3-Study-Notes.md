## 1.3 Opening: Audit planning basics

### Audit planning
- Definition: deciding the overall audit strategy and the specific procedures before fieldwork.
- Use: turns audit objectives into a workable, ordered set of tasks.
- Audit impact: poor planning means missed risks and wasted audit effort.
- Planning happens at the beginning of the audit process.
- Example: before auditing payroll, the auditor lists procedures, people, and timing.

### Short-term and long-term planning
- Short-term planning: audit issues to be covered during the current year.
- Long-term planning: risk issues from changes in IT strategic direction.
- Example: short-term covers this year's access reviews; long-term covers a planned cloud migration.
- Audit impact: long-term planning keeps the audit function ahead of IT change.

### Review cycle and approval
- Analyze short- and long-term issues at least annually.
- Reason: new control issues, better techniques, and shifting risks and technology.
- Senior audit management reviews the analysis.
- The audit committee approves it, or the board if no committee exists.
- Results are communicated to relevant levels of management.
- Update the annual plan when key risk aspects change.
- Example triggers: acquisitions, new regulatory issues, market conditions.
- Audit impact: approval by the top gives the audit function independence and authority.
- ⚠ Check: manual uses the board only when no audit committee exists.

### Standards referenced
- Manual points to standards 1007 Assertions and 1008 Criteria, and guidelines 2007, 2008.
- ⚠ Check: outline omits these references; they are in the manual text.

## 1.3.1 Individual audit assignments

### Planning each engagement
- Definition: an individual audit assignment is one specific audit, such as one application.
- Use: each assignment needs its own plan, beyond the annual plan.
- Audit impact: an unplanned engagement may miss context-specific risks.

### Factors affecting the approach
- Results of periodic risk assessments.
- Changes in how technology is applied.
- Evolving privacy issues and regulatory requirements.
- System implementation or upgrade deadlines.
- Current and future technologies.
- Requirements from business process owners.
- IS resource limitations.
- Example: an imminent go-live may shift focus to pre-implementation review.

### Understanding the environment
- Understand business practices and functions related to the audit subject.
- Understand the information systems and technology supporting the activity.
- Know the regulatory environment in which the business operates.
- Audit impact: without context, the auditor cannot judge what matters or what is material.
- Standard 1201 and guideline 2201 cover risk assessment in planning.

### Audit universe 📓⭐
- Definition: the complete list of all processes that could be audited. 📓
- Use: the starting point for deciding what to audit each year. 📓
- Audit impact: anything missing from the universe never gets risk-rated or audited.
- It is the first step in annual IS audit planning. 📓
- It is the foundation of the annual audit plan. 📓
- It should include all processes that form the enterprise's business blueprint.
- Example: a retailer's universe lists sales, payments, inventory, payroll, and IT operations.
- Each process may get a qualitative or quantitative risk assessment.
- Qualitative means ratings like high, medium, low.
- Quantitative means numeric values or calculated scores.
- Risk factors influence how often a risk occurs or its business impact.
- Example: reputation is a critical risk factor for a retail business.
- Risk evaluation should use inputs from business process owners.
- Criteria should be objective, yet subjectivity cannot be fully removed.
- Reputation rating example: high means recovery takes over six months.
- Medium means recovery takes between three and six months.
- Low means recovery takes under three months.
- Time frame is the objective part; owners judging the duration is the subjective part.
- After rating each factor, define a rule to get each process's overall risk.
- The ideal annual plan includes every process rated high.
- Resources are usually too few to run the whole ideal plan.
- The gap shows management how much risk it accepts without more resources.
- Exam traps:
  - First step of the annual plan is the audit universe, not threats or sampling.
  - Threat and vulnerability analysis follows the universe, because you need scope first.
  - Control environment and sampling approach come later, during detailed planning.
  - A process already chosen for audit moves next to control objectives.
  - Do not confuse that later step with building the universe.

### Risk-based audit plan
- Definition: a plan that sends audit resources where risk is greatest.
- Use: choose and prioritize audits when time and staff are limited.
- Audit impact: low-risk areas may be skipped, which management must knowingly accept.
- It prioritizes high-risk areas. 📓
- It aligns with strategic organizational objectives. 📓
- It uses key business processes identified by senior management. 📓⭐
- When resources are limited, focus on high-risk areas. 📓
- It starts with a risk assessment. 📓⭐
- Example: with budget for four audits, pick the four highest-rated processes.
- Exam traps:
  - Best source for the plan is key processes from senior management.
  - Process owners' views on controls and vulnerabilities are less reliable, being self-interested.
  - Prior audit findings alone look backward, so they are weaker.
  - Relying on management's assurance of controls skips independent risk assessment.
  - Short on resources: focus on high-risk areas, not last year's findings.
  - Risk assessment's prime purpose: reasonable assurance of material-item coverage.
  - Allocating resources is a use of the assessment, not its prime purpose.

### Understanding the organization
- Understand external and internal factors affecting the organization.
- Understand how it selects and applies policies and procedures.
- Understand its objectives and strategies.
- Understand how it measures and reviews performance.
- Key components to understand:
  - Strategy management.
  - Business products and services.
  - Corporate governance process.
  - Transaction types, partners, and flows within information systems.

### Compliance versus substantive testing
- Definition, compliance test: checks whether controls operate as designed.
- Definition, substantive test: checks actual data or transactions for errors.
- Use: the risk assessment helps choose which type to perform.
- Audit impact: strong controls allow lighter substantive work; weak ones need more.
- The approach determines the nature and extent of testing.
- ⚠ Check: manual says inherent, control, detection risk "should not be of major concern".
- That phrasing is odd; read it as auditors also using controls and business knowledge.
- Auditors do not rely solely on risk assessment.
- The approach also relates control cost-benefit to the known risk.

### Business risk and risk models
- Definition, business risk: probable effect of an uncertain event on business objectives.
- Nature may be financial, regulatory, or operational.
- Risk may also come from specific technologies.
- Example: an airline faces safety regulation, so IT availability and reliability are critical.
- Risk also covers what the organization chooses to risk to advance goals.
- A risk model can be simple weights in an equation.
- Or an elaborate scheme with weights by business nature or risk significance.
- Understanding the business helps the auditor pick the right model.
- Standard 1204 Materiality is the related guidance.

### Audit planning steps
- Understand mission, objectives, purpose, and processes.
- Include availability, integrity, security, and confidentiality requirements.
- Understand the governance structure and practices.
- Understand changes in the auditee's business environment.
- Review prior work papers.
- Identify stated policies, standards, guidelines, procedures, and structure.
- Perform a risk analysis to design the audit plan.
- Set audit scope and objectives.
- Develop the audit approach or strategy.
- Assign personnel resources.
- Address engagement logistics.
- Identify chances for continuous audit or CAATs automation.
- Definition, CAATs: computer-assisted audit tools that automate testing.
- Use: test whole populations or repeat tests cheaply.
- Audit impact: improves coverage and lowers detection risk.
- Exam traps:
  - First planning step is a risk assessment, not designing the program.
  - Allocating resources and finding stakeholders come after risk analysis.

### Audit scope and objectives
- Definition, scope: the boundaries of what the audit covers.
- Definition, objective: what the audit intends to conclude about the area.
- Statutory and regulatory requirements are the prime consideration. 📓
- Audit objectives help identify key business risks. 📓
- Exam traps:
  - Organization policies, structure, or process complexity are secondary to legal duties.
  - Law outranks internal preference because noncompliance creates liability.

## 1.3.2 Effect of laws and regulations on IS audit planning

### Why law matters
- Every organization must meet governmental and external requirements, whatever its size.
- Statutory and regulatory requirements drive scope. 📓
- Legal obligations add credibility to the audit. 📓
- Industry regulations affect how data is processed, transmitted, and stored.
- Example: stock exchanges and central banks impose such rules.
- Closely regulated industries need special compliance attention.
- Several countries are adding legal rules on IS audit and assurance.
- Their content covers requirements, entity responsibilities, and financial, operational, and IS audit functions.
- Management at all levels should know relevant external requirements.

### SOX 📓
- Definition: US law on accurate financial reporting and internal controls.
- Use: forces public companies to certify and test financial controls.
- Audit impact: IT general controls over financial systems fall into audit scope.
- Notes add: section 302 certification and section 404 control assessment.
- ⚠ Check: manual 1.3.2 does not mention SOX or sections 302 or 404.
- ⚠ Check: "regulatory data quality is top priority" is unsupported in the manual. 📓
- Manual only says legal requirements are the prime scope consideration.
- Manual says candidates are not asked about specific laws.

### Two areas of concern
- Legal requirements applicable to the audit or IS audit itself.
- Legal requirements placed on the auditee's systems, data, and reporting.
- Both shape audit scope and audit objectives.
- Ergonomic regulations may also affect business operations.

### Steps to determine compliance with external requirements
- Identify requirements on electronic data, personal data, copyright, ecommerce, and e-signatures.
- Also identify requirements on IS practices, controls, and how data is stored.
- Document the applicable laws and regulations.
- Assess whether management considered them in plans, policies, standards, and procedures.
- Review internal IT documents addressing adherence to industry laws.
- Determine adherence to established procedures for external requirements.
- Check contracts with external IT providers reflect legal responsibilities.
- Example: a contract with a cloud host should assign breach notification duties.
- Exam traps:
  - Exams ask how to audit compliance, not what specific laws say.
  - Start by identifying and documenting requirements, not by testing controls.

## 1.3.3 Audit risk and materiality

### Audit risk components
- Definition, audit risk: chance a material error goes undetected by the audit.
- Use: the auditor aims to keep it acceptably low.
- Audit impact: high audit risk means a wrong opinion on the area.
- Also consider customer data, privacy, service availability, and public image.
- Definition, inherent risk: exposure of a process without considering any controls. 📓⭐
- Inherent risk exists independent of the audit.
- It arises from the nature of the business.
- Example: a payment gateway has high inherent fraud exposure before controls.
- Definition, control risk: material error not prevented or detected in time by controls.
- Example: manual log review has high control risk, since volume hides issues.
- Example: consistently applied computerized validation ordinarily has low control risk.
- Definition, detection risk: the auditor fails to find a material error.
- Proper statistical sampling or quality control reduces it.
- Overall audit risk: auditor may miss a material error in reports.
- Objective: limit overall audit risk to a sufficiently low level by completion.
- Audit impact: the auditor controls only detection risk; the others stem from the auditee.
- ⚠ Check: manual gives no formula multiplying inherent, control, and detection risk.
- That model is common outside this section, so treat it as ➕ here. ➕
- Exam traps:
  - Exposure without regard to controls is inherent risk, not control or residual risk.
  - Residual risk is what remains after controls; it comes from 2.5, not 1.3.3.
  - First analysis in a risk-focused audit is inherent risk, before controls.

### Materiality and assurance
- Definition, materiality: relative importance of a matter to the enterprise as a whole.
- Use: decides which items deserve audit effort.
- Audit impact: ignoring materiality wastes effort on trivia or misses big issues.
- Material items focus audit effort. 📓
- Material findings significantly affect objectives. 📓
- Reasonable assurance applies because sampling cannot examine everything. 📓⭐
- Inverse link: higher materiality means lower acceptable audit risk.
- Example: a large ledger balance is more material, so the auditor tolerates less risk.
- Small errors that look minor can combine into a material one.
- Standard 1204 Materiality gives related guidance.
- Exam traps:
  - IS audit gives reasonable assurance on material items, never absolute or all-item assurance.
  - "Definite" or "all items" distractors ignore sampling limits.
  - Audit risk is not statistical sampling risk, which is wrong assumptions about a population.
  - Potential material findings call for more evidence first, then discussion with auditee.

## 1.3.4 Risk assessment

### What it is
- Definition: finding, measuring, and ranking risks against acceptance criteria and objectives.
- Use: guides management action and priorities for controls.
- Audit impact: gives audit planning a defensible basis and covers material items. 📓
- Purpose: identify, evaluate, and mitigate risks through controls. 📓
- It identifies, quantifies, and prioritizes risk.
- It sets priorities for managing information security risk.
- It sets priorities for implementing selected controls.

### Who and when
- Management performs it periodically for changes in assets, threats, vulnerabilities, impacts.
- It is also repeated whenever significant change occurs.
- IT management is responsible for conducting risk assessments.
- The IS auditor may assist if expertise is missing.
- Management remains ultimately responsible for the process.
- The auditor may run a separate assessment to supplement audit planning.
- Audit impact: doing management's assessment threatens independence.
- Manual points to section 2.5 for more detail.
- ⚠ Check: notes say senior business management owns risk; manual says IT management conducts. 📓

## 1.3.5 IS audit risk assessment techniques

### Choosing techniques
- Auditors may face many audit subjects, each with different risk types.
- They evaluate candidates to find high-risk areas to audit.
- Methods range from high-medium-low judgment to complex numeric ratings.
- A combination of techniques is allowed.
- Methods may evolve; complexity should fit the organization.

### Scoring systems
- Definition: ranking audits by scoring risk factors, then comparing scores.
- Variables: technical complexity, level of control procedures, and financial loss.
- Variables may or may not be weighted.
- Audits are scheduled by comparing the resulting values.
- Example: an unweighted score adds three factor ratings for each system.

### Judgment-based assessment
- Definition: independent decision using business knowledge and experience.
- Inputs: executive directives, history, business goals, environmental factors.
- Audit impact: flexible, but more exposed to bias.
- Qualitative methods use categories; quantitative methods use numbers.

### Using management's assessments
- Leverage management risk assessments to supplement the auditor's own.
- Apply professional skepticism because of potential independence impairment.
- Benefits of using risk assessment:
  - Allocates limited audit resources effectively.
  - Gathers information from all management levels, including boards.
  - Directs audit work to high-risk, value-adding areas.
  - Establishes a basis for managing the audit department.
  - Links each audit subject to overall organization and business plans.
- Exam trap: relying on management's assessment without skepticism weakens independence.

## 1.3.6 Risk analysis

### What it is
- Definition: a subset of risk assessment used to find risks and vulnerabilities.
- Use: lets the auditor decide which controls are needed to mitigate risk.
- Audit impact: shapes planning, but is not itself audit evidence.
- Risk assessment procedures do not give sufficient evidence to support the audit opinion.
- Exam trap: evidence for the opinion comes from testing, not from the risk analysis.

### Risk formula and residual risk
- Outline formula: threat times vulnerability times impact. ➕
- ⚠ Check: not stated in manual 1.3.6; ISACA expresses it elsewhere in qualitative terms.
- Definition, residual risk: risk remaining after controls are applied. 📓
- ⚠ Check: residual risk is defined in manual section 2.5, not 1.3.6.
- Example: after adding MFA, leftover phishing risk is residual risk.
- Exam trap: residual risk is never the first step; inherent risk comes first.

### Risk and control
- Auditors must understand the relationship between risk and control.
- They should identify and tell apart risk types and mitigating controls.
- They should know common business risk areas and related technology risk.
- They should evaluate management's risk assessment and management processes.
- Controls address what should be achieved and what should be avoided.
- Risk also exists within the audit process itself.
- Audit impact: auditors must manage their own audit risk, not just the auditee's.

## Takeaway
- Audit universe first, then risk-rate each process, then plan.
- Law and regulation set scope; high-risk areas get scarce resources.
- Inherent risk ignores controls; control risk and detection risk follow.
- Auditors give reasonable assurance on material items, never absolute assurance.
- Management owns risk assessment; auditors assist, supplement, and stay skeptical.
- Risk assessment guides planning but does not serve as audit evidence.
