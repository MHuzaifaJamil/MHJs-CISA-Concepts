## 1.2.0 Basics: audit, assessment, review

### Audit
- Definition: Formal inspection and verification against standards, records, or efficiency targets.
- Use: Confirms standards are followed, records are accurate, and targets are met.
- Audit impact: Gives higher assurance than assessments or reviews.
- Example: Auditor tests whether payroll records match approved pay rates.

### Assessment and review
- Definition: Broader, less formal looks at quality, cost, perceptions, or policy.
- Use: Finds improvement chances, such as cutting costs of poor quality.
- Audit impact: Carries less negative stigma than a formal audit.
- Audit impact: Gives lower assurance than an audit.
- Either may be performed by internal or external groups.
- Exam traps:
  - Need strongest assurance for an opinion: choose a formal audit.
  - Assessment or review is wrong when an opinion is required.
  - Who performs the work (internal or external) does not change the type.

### Internal vs external audit
- Definition: Internal audit is by the organization's own staff; external is by outsiders.
- Use: Internal supports management; external often supports regulators, investors, or contracts.
- Audit impact: Independence differs, so reliance and scope decisions differ.
- ⚠ Check: Source only says audits may be internal or external.
- Integrated audits may also be run by either group, per the source.

## 1.2.0 Audit types

### Financial audit
- Definition: Checks whether financial reporting is accurate.
- Use: Supports reliable financial statements for owners and regulators.
- Audit impact: Often uses detailed substantive testing.
- Audit impact: IS auditors increasingly use a risk- and control-based approach.
- Relates to integrity and reliability of financial information.
- Example: Recounting inventory and confirming receivables to support balance-sheet figures.

### Operational audit
- Definition: Evaluates the internal control structure of one process or area.
- Use: Shows whether controls in that area are designed sensibly.
- Audit impact: IS audits of application controls count as operational audits.
- Audit impact: IS audits of logical security systems also count.
- Example: Reviewing access controls in the order-entry application.

### Compliance audit
- Definition: Tests controls against laws, standards, or industry practices.
- Use: Demonstrates adherence to specific regulations or standards.
- Audit impact: Often overlaps other audit types.
- Audit impact: May focus on particular systems or data.
- Example: Testing card-data controls against a payment-industry standard.
- Exam traps:
  - Compliance audit checks against a named rule set, not general efficiency.
  - Efficiency questions point to administrative or operational audits instead.

### Integrated audit
- Definition: Combines financial and operational audit steps in one audit.
- Use: Assesses objectives for financial data, asset safety, efficiency, and compliance.
- Audit impact: Includes compliance tests of controls and substantive steps.
- Audit impact: May or may not include an IS auditor.
- Audit impact: Internal or external auditors can perform it.
- Details of the method are in 1.2.2 below.
- ⚠ Check: Source text points readers to section 1.10 for related quality material.

### Administrative audit
- Definition: Assesses efficiency of operational productivity.
- Use: Finds waste or low productivity in routine operations.
- Audit impact: Focus is efficiency, not financial accuracy or legal compliance.

### IS (IT) audit
- Definition: Collects and evaluates evidence about information systems and related resources.
- Use: Judges whether systems are safeguarded and controls give reasonable assurance.
- Audit impact: Checks data and system integrity and availability.
- Audit impact: Checks information is relevant and reliable.
- Audit impact: Checks goals are met effectively and resources used efficiently.
- Audit impact: Controls should prevent, or detect and correct, undesired events in time.
- Exam traps:
  - Controls give reasonable assurance, never absolute assurance.
  - "Guarantees no fraud" style answers overstate what audit provides.

### Specialized audit
- Definition: Audit aimed at one narrow topic within a broader category.
- Use: Covers areas like fraud or services run by third parties.
- Audit impact: Needs specific skills and often specific tools.

### Third-party service audit
- Definition: Audit of outsourced processes run by a service provider.
- Use: Gives users assurance about a provider's controls, possibly across jurisdictions.
- Audit impact: Issues an opinion on the provider's description of controls.
- Audit impact: Delivered as a service auditor's report.
- Audit impact: The customer's IS auditor can use that report.
- Example: A payroll outsourcer gives clients an independent controls report.
- Exam traps:
  - Relying on the report does not remove the customer's own responsibility.
  - A service auditor's report cannot ensure the provider's controls always work.

### Third-party assurance reports (SOC 1, SOC 2, SOC 3) ➕
- Definition: Standard report types about a service organization's controls.
- Use: Customers read them instead of auditing the provider directly.
- SOC 1: Controls relevant to user entities' financial reporting.
- SOC 2: Controls for security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, or privacy.
- SOC 3: Like SOC 2 but without detailed control design and test results.
- Audit impact: Choose by purpose: financial reporting means SOC 1; security means SOC 2.
- Audit impact: SOC 3 lacks detail, so it gives weaker evidence for auditors.
- ⚠ Check: Outline marks this ➕ "not in manual", but the manual covers SOC reports.
- ⚠ Check: Coverage sits in section 2.9.2 Outsourcing Practices, not section 1.2.
- ⚠ Check: Manual ties these reports to SSAE 18, replacing SAS 70.
- ⚠ Check: Manual also names ISAE 3402 as the international equivalent.
- Keep ➕ until you confirm the exam blueprint places SOC under Domain 1.
- Exam traps:
  - Outsourced financial processing: pick SOC 1, not SOC 2.
  - Need detail on tests performed: SOC 3 is wrong, it omits them.
  - Auditor should also validate the third party was independent, objective, and competent.

### Fraud audit
- Definition: Specialized audit designed to discover fraudulent activity.
- Use: Hunts schemes and business irregularities.
- Audit impact: Uses specific tools and data analysis techniques.
- Example: Matching vendor bank accounts to employee bank accounts to spot fake vendors.

### Forensic audit
- Definition: Specialized audit to discover, disclose, and follow up on fraud and crime.
- Use: Builds evidence for law enforcement and judicial review.
- Audit impact: Evidence handling must withstand legal scrutiny.
- Exam traps:
  - Forensic audit aims at legal evidence; fraud audit aims at discovery.
  - Altering or mishandling evidence ruins its legal value.

### Computer forensic audit
- Definition: Investigation analyzing electronic devices to gather and preserve evidence.
- Use: Recovers digital evidence for investigations.
- Audit impact: A skilled IS auditor can assist a security manager or forensic specialist.
- Audit impact: IS auditor can verify evidence-collection procedures were followed.
- Example: Auditor confirms a seized laptop was imaged using the approved procedure.

### Functional audit
- Definition: Independent check that software works as its requirement specifications say.
- Use: Confirms configuration items' real function and performance match requirements.
- Audit impact: Done before software delivery or after implementation.
- Example: Comparing a delivered billing module's behavior with its signed requirements.

### Readiness assessment
- Definition: Review of current compliance with documented standards.
- Use: Produces actionable fixes before a formal audit.
- Audit impact: Focuses on control design, not operating effectiveness.
- Audit impact: Passing it does not prove controls operate effectively.
- Example: Pre-certification gap review before an external standards audit.
- Exam traps:
  - Readiness assessment tests design; operating effectiveness needs later audit testing.
  - It is preparation, not a substitute for the formal audit.

## 1.2.1 Control Self-Assessment (CSA)

### Basics
- Definition: Staff and management of a unit assess their own controls.
- Use: Assures stakeholders that internal control is reliable.
- Audit impact: Keeps employees aware of business risk and doing proactive control reviews.
- Methodology: review key objectives, assess risk, ensure controls manage that risk.
- Process is formal, documented, and collaborative.
- Example: Branch managers rate their own cash-handling controls each quarter.

### Implementation methods
- Questionnaires and surveys.
- Facilitated workshops.
- Informal peer reviews.
- Workshops suit small business units.
- Functional management and IS auditors deliberate the control structure together.
- Facilitator role: supports the decision-making process.
- Facilitator builds a supportive space for sharing experience, strengths, and weaknesses.
- Facilitator may add own expertise when appropriate.

### Who judges the controls
- Process owners evaluate controls against objectives and risk appetite.
- They know the process objectives best.
- Auditor guides; auditor does not perform detailed audit procedures here.

### Objectives
- Primary objective: leverage internal audit by shifting monitoring to functional areas.
- Line managers own and monitor controls in their environment.
- CSA enhances audit responsibilities; it never replaces them.
- Educate management on control design, monitoring, and high-risk areas.
- Define success measures for planning, implementation, and monitoring phases.
- Critical success factor: meeting business unit representatives early.
- That meeting identifies the primary objective and the reliability of controls.
- ⚠ Check: Source ties the CSF meeting to staff and management, not only representatives.

### Benefits
- Early detection of risk.
- More effective, improved internal controls.
- Cohesive teams through employee involvement.
- Sense of control ownership and less resistance to improvement.
- Greater awareness of objectives, risk, and controls.
- Better communication between operational and top management.
- Higher employee motivation.
- Improved audit rating process.
- Reduced control cost.
- Assurance to stakeholders, customers, and top management.
- ⚠ Check: Source splits awareness into objectives and risk/controls; outline merges them.
- ⚠ Check: Source assurance to top management concerns adequacy relative to laws and regulations.

### Disadvantages
- Could be mistaken for replacing the audit function.
- May feel like extra workload, such as one more report.
- Ignoring improvement suggestions damages employee morale.
- Lack of audit knowledge may limit detection of weak controls.

### IS auditor's role in CSA
- Auditor acts as facilitator and internal control professional.
- Management client is the participant, not the auditor.
- Auditor must understand the business process being assessed.
- Auditor guides auditees using risk-based control objectives.
- Value shows when management takes ownership, including active monitoring.
- Example: Managers want to drop a preventive approval step to speed work.
- Auditor explains the risk of removing it, instead of deciding for them.
- Exam traps:
  - In CSA the auditor facilitates; managers assess. Auditor-led testing is wrong.
  - CSA's main aim is shifting control monitoring to functional areas.
  - "Replace internal audit" or "cut audit staff" answers misread CSA's purpose.
  - Independence concern is weaker in CSA because it is not an audit opinion.
  - Distractor "manager wants fewer controls": auditor explains risk, not simply agrees.
- ⚠ Check: Last trap's independence point is my inference, not stated in the source.

## 1.2.2 Integrated Auditing

### Basics
- Definition: Combining audit disciplines to assess key controls with a risk focus.
- Use: Covers an operation, process, or entity in one audit.
- Audit impact: Business depends on IT, so all auditors need IT control knowledge.
- Audit impact: IS auditors also need to understand business control structures.
- Auditors work with business process specialists as one team.
- Approach demands focus on business risk and creative control solutions.

### Process steps
- Step 1: Identify risk for the area audited.
- Step 2: Identify relevant key controls.
- Step 3: Review and understand design of key controls.
- Step 4: Test IT system support for key controls.
- Step 5: Test operational effectiveness of management controls.
- Step 6: Issue a combined report or opinion on control risk, design, weaknesses.
- Order matters: design review comes before testing.

### Risk assessment roles
- IS auditor identifies risk in information management, IT infrastructure, IT governance, IT operations.
- Other specialists examine organizational environment, business risk, and business controls.
- Whole team discusses emerging risk, considering impact and likelihood.
- IT systems often form the first line of preventive and detective controls.
- Approach depends on a sound assessment of those IT controls' efficiency and effectiveness.

### Benefits
- One audit and one comprehensive report.
- Staff development and retention through variety.
- Process owners see the link between controls and audit procedures.
- Top management sees link between control effectiveness and IT resource allocation.
- Shareholders see link between governance and reliable financial statements.
- Example: One team audits order-to-cash covering finance steps and the ERP controls.
- Exam traps:
  - Integrated audit: pick single team, single report, risk focus.
  - Separate financial and IT reports are the opposite of integration.
  - Control design is understood before operating effectiveness is tested.

## Takeaway
- Audits give most assurance; assessments and reviews are softer and improvement-focused.
- Match audit type to purpose: financial, operational, compliance, forensic, functional.
- Readiness assessments test design only; operating effectiveness comes in the formal audit.
- CSA shifts monitoring to managers; the auditor facilitates and never replaces audit.
- Integrated audit: risk first, design review, then testing, one combined report.
- SOC 1 is financial reporting, SOC 2 is security-type criteria, SOC 3 is summary.
