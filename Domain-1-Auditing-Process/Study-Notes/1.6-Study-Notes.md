## 1.6 Overview: why sampling exists

- **Audit sampling**
  - Definition: testing part of a group to judge the whole group.
  - Use: reach valid conclusions when checking everything costs too much.
  - Audit impact: conclusions carry some risk of being wrong.
- Sampling supports valid conclusions about a population.
- Use statistical sampling when concluding about the entire population.
- Four sample steps: design, select, perform procedures, evaluate results.
- Goal of the steps: sufficient, reliable, relevant, useful evidence.
- Example: checking 60 of 5,000 change tickets instead of all.
- ⚠ Check: the manual says use statistical sampling to conclude on the whole population.
- Exam traps
  - Sampling gives reasonable assurance, never absolute assurance.
  - Judgment is needed in both statistical and non-statistical methods.

## 1.6.1 Compliance vs substantive testing ⭐

### Basics

- **Compliance test (test of controls)**
  - Definition: checks whether a control is actually working as designed.
  - Use: evaluates operating effectiveness at preventing, or detecting and correcting, weaknesses.
  - Audit impact: decides how much reliance the auditor places on controls.
- Question answered: are controls operating?
- Broad objective: reasonable assurance that a control works as seen in preliminary evaluation.
- Example: compare source and object versions of programs in the production library.
- Sampling examples for compliance tests: user access rights, change control, logs, license audits.
- ⚠ Check: the manual also lists documentation procedures and follow-up of exceptions.

- **Substantive test (test of details)**
  - Definition: checks whether balances and transactions are correct.
  - Use: detects material weaknesses at the assertion level.
  - Audit impact: gives direct evidence on data integrity.
- Question answered: are balances and transactions correct?
- It substantiates the integrity of actual processing.
- Examples: recompute interest on sampled accounts, vouch transactions to supporting documents.
- A substantive test can also check completeness and accuracy of report data.

### Link between the two

- Direct correlation: stronger controls allow less substantive testing.
- Weak controls raise doubts, so more substantive testing is needed.
- Include substantive tests when preliminary evaluation shows controls unreliable or absent.
- Substantive categories: analytical review procedures; test balances and transactions.
- Example: strong change control found, so reduce detail testing of program outputs.

### Exam traps

- Control test result drives the amount of substantive work, not the reverse.
- Compliance asks "is the control working"; substantive asks "is the number right".
- Do not skip substantive testing just because controls exist, unless tests supported them.
- Weak controls never justify less testing; they justify more detail testing.

## 1.6.2 Sampling 📓

### Basics

- **Population**
  - Definition: the whole group of items you want to examine.
  - Use: sets the boundary for selecting and concluding.
  - Audit impact: a wrongly defined population invalidates the conclusion.
- **Sample**
  - Definition: the subset of the population actually tested.
  - Use: infers population characteristics from sample characteristics.
  - Audit impact: sample quality sets conclusion reliability.
- Sampling is used when time and cost preclude full verification.
- Sample size, selection and evaluation all need judgment about the population.

### Core classifications

- **Statistical sampling** 📓
  - Definition: sampling using mathematical probability rules.
  - Use: calculate size, select items, evaluate results objectively.
  - Audit impact: results are quantifiable, including a confidence coefficient.
- Each item must have an equal chance of selection.
- Auditor sets precision (how close) and confidence (times in 100).
- Example: a random sample of 100 purchase orders with a stated 95 percent confidence.

- **Non-statistical (judgmental) sampling** 📓
  - Definition: sampling where the auditor's judgment sets method, size and items.
  - Use: focus on the most material and riskiest items.
  - Audit impact: error probability cannot be quantified.
- Example: picking the ten largest vendor payments for review.
- Purpose of sampling: representation of large populations 📓
- Exam traps
  - Statistical means probability-based, not just "bigger" or "computer-selected".
  - Judgmental sampling cannot give a mathematical confidence level.

### Attribute sampling ("how often") 📓

- **Attribute sampling**
  - Definition: counts presence or absence of a feature, giving a rate.
  - Use: generally applied in compliance testing.
  - Audit impact: tells how often a control fails.
- Conclusions are expressed as rates of incidence.
- The manual says it answers "How many?" while your notes say "how often".
- ⚠ Check: both phrasings are about rate of occurrence, not dollar amounts.
- Example: checking that access request forms carry an approval signature.
- Attribute-based (frequency estimating) sampling: measures rate of control failures 📓

- **Stop-or-go sampling** 📓
  - Definition: sampling that can stop at the earliest possible point.
  - Use: when few errors are expected in the population.
  - Audit impact: avoids over-sampling a well-controlled attribute.
- Example: stop after 40 clean items when the control is rarely failing.

- **Discovery sampling** 📓⭐
  - Definition: sampling designed to find at least one instance of an occurrence.
  - Use: seeking fraud, circumvention of regulations or other irregularities.
  - Audit impact: one error found taints the entire sample.
- Error-free sample means no fraud or irregularity is assumed.
- Manual glossary classes it as a form of attribute sampling.
- Example: checking for even one duplicate payment to a fake vendor.
- Exam traps
  - Fraud concern points to discovery sampling, never stop-or-go.
  - Stop-or-go is about efficiency when errors are few, not about fraud.
  - Discovery gives a probability of finding one example, not an error rate.

- **Precision sampling** 📓
  - Definition: sample size set by the desired accuracy of the estimate.
  - Use: calculating how large the sample must be.
  - Audit impact: tighter precision means a bigger sample.
- ⚠ Check: the manual does not list "precision sampling" as a named attribute model.
- ⚠ Check: the manual names three attribute types: attribute, stop-or-go, discovery.

### Variable sampling ("how much") 📓

- **Variable sampling**
  - Definition: estimates a value, such as dollars or weight, from a sample.
  - Use: generally applied in substantive testing.
  - Audit impact: concludes on deviations from the norm.
- Example: estimating total inventory value from sampled stock lines.
- Manual names three variable models: stratified, unstratified mean per unit, difference estimation.

- **Unstratified (mean-per-unit)** 📓
  - Definition: average the sample, then project it to the whole population.
  - Use: estimate a total when the population is fairly uniform.
  - Audit impact: large values can dominate the estimate.

- **Stratified mean-per-unit** 📓⭐
  - Definition: split population into groups, sample each group separately.
  - Use: populations with distinct subgroups, such as high-net-worth and normal customers.
  - Audit impact: gives smaller overall sample than unstratified, and balanced representation.
- Stratification prevents large values from dominating small ones.
- Exam traps
  - Two distinct customer types point to stratified, not simple random.
  - Systematic or cluster sampling do not guarantee each subgroup is represented.

- **Difference estimation** 📓
  - Definition: estimates total difference between audited and book values.
  - Use: when most book values are close to audited values.
  - Audit impact: sample differences are projected to the population.
- **Ratio estimation** 📓
  - Definition: projects the audited-to-book ratio to the population book value.
  - Use: when errors scale with item size.
  - Audit impact: gives total estimate from sampled error ratio.
- ⚠ Check: ratio estimation is not among the manual's three variable models.
- **Monetary Unit Sampling (MUS)** 📓
  - Definition: each dollar is a sampling unit, so large items are likelier selected.
  - Use: testing for overstatement in account balances.
  - Audit impact: focuses effort on high-value items automatically.
- ⚠ Check: MUS is absent from the manual text; treat as outside-source ➕.
- **Classical variable sampling** 📓
  - Definition: uses normal distribution to estimate population totals.
  - Use: estimating monetary totals from a sample.
  - Audit impact: relies on statistical assumptions about distribution.
- ⚠ Check: the manual's "classical sampling" appears only in exam questions, not text.

### Selection techniques 📓

- ⚠ Check: manual text omits selection techniques; treat as ➕ outside source.
- **Random sampling** 📓
  - Definition: every item has an equal chance of being picked.
  - Use: unbiased selection, often via random number generator.
  - Audit impact: supports a statistical conclusion.
- **Systematic (fixed-interval) sampling** 📓
  - Definition: pick every Nth item after a random start.
  - Use: simple selection from ordered lists.
  - Audit impact: periodic patterns in data can bias results.
- **Cluster sampling** 📓
  - Definition: select whole natural groups, such as branches, then test items within.
  - Use: when populations are geographically or organisationally spread.
  - Audit impact: cheaper, but clusters may not mirror the whole population.
- **Judgmental (subjective) sampling** 📓
  - Definition: auditor hand-picks items using experience.
  - Use: targets high-risk or material items.
  - Audit impact: cannot be projected statistically to the population.
- Example: systematic pick of every 10th customer file from a random start.

### Sampling parameters ➕

- ⚠ Check: the outline marks these ➕, but manual figure 1.17 does define them.
- **Sampling risk** is covered in 1.6.3 below.
- **Confidence level (confidence coefficient)**
  - Definition: percent chance the sample truly represents the population.
  - Use: 90, 95 or 99 percent are typical choices.
  - Audit impact: higher confidence means a larger sample.
- 95 percent is regarded as high assurance.
- Strong internal controls allow a lower confidence coefficient.
- **Level of risk**
  - Definition: one minus the confidence coefficient.
  - Use: states the accepted chance of being wrong.
  - Audit impact: lower risk demands larger samples.
- **Tolerable error rate**
  - Definition: maximum error allowed before the account is materially misstated.
  - Use: planned upper limit of the precision range for compliance testing.
  - Audit impact: higher tolerable rate allows a smaller sample.
- **Expected error rate**
  - Definition: auditor's estimate of the errors likely present.
  - Use: input to attribute sampling formulas only.
  - Audit impact: higher expected error means a larger sample.
- ⚠ Check: manual gives no sample-size direction for tolerable rate ➕.
- **Precision**
  - Definition: acceptable gap between sample result and true population.
  - Use: attribute: a percentage; variable: a monetary amount or number.
  - Audit impact: larger precision amount means smaller sample, higher undetected-error risk.
- Very low precision can create an unnecessarily large sample.
- In substantive testing, precision range and precision mean the same.
- **Sample mean**
  - Definition: sum of sample values divided by sample size.
  - Use: base for projecting a population total.
  - Audit impact: a biased sample gives a biased projection.
- **Sample standard deviation**
  - Definition: spread of sample values around the sample mean.
  - Use: measures variability inside the sample.
  - Audit impact: wide spread signals uncertainty.
- **Population standard deviation**
  - Definition: spread of the whole population around its mean.
  - Use: input to variable sampling formulas only.
  - Audit impact: greater deviation means larger sample.
- Exam traps
  - Expected error rate belongs to attribute sampling, not variable.
  - Population standard deviation belongs to variable sampling, not attribute.
  - Higher confidence or lower precision amount always enlarges the sample.

### Direction of sample size effects

| Factor | Change | Sample size |
|---|---|---|
| Confidence coefficient | Higher | Larger |
| Precision amount | Higher | Smaller |
| Expected error rate | Higher | Larger |
| Population standard deviation | Higher | Larger |

### Worked examples

**Example 1: level of risk**
- Formula: level of risk = 1 − confidence coefficient.
- Step 1: confidence coefficient is 95 percent, which is 0.95.
- Step 2: 1 − 0.95 = 0.05.
- Step 3: level of risk is 5 percent.
- Step 4: at 99 percent, 1 − 0.99 = 0.01, so 1 percent.
- Verification: 95 percent plus 5 percent equals 100 percent, and 99 plus 1 equals 100.

**Example 2: unstratified mean-per-unit projection**
- Formula: estimated total = sample mean × population size.
- Given: 50 items sampled, sample total $12,500, population 2,000 items.
- Step 1: sample mean = 12,500 ÷ 50 = $250.
- Step 2: estimated total = 250 × 2,000 = $500,000.
- Verification: scale-up factor 2,000 ÷ 50 = 40; 12,500 × 40 = $500,000, matching.

**Example 3: difference estimation**
- Formula: estimated total difference = average difference × population size.
- Given: 40 items, book $80,000, audited $79,200, population 1,000 items.
- Step 1: sample difference = 79,200 − 80,000 = −$800.
- Step 2: average difference = −800 ÷ 40 = −$20.
- Step 3: estimated total difference = −20 × 1,000 = −$20,000.
- Verification: scale-up factor 1,000 ÷ 40 = 25; −800 × 25 = −$20,000, matching.

**Example 4: systematic selection interval**
- Formula: interval = population size ÷ sample size.
- Given: population 2,000, sample 50, random start 17.
- Step 1: interval = 2,000 ÷ 50 = 40.
- Step 2: items picked are 17, 57, 97 and so on.
- Step 3: 50th item = 17 + (49 × 40) = 1,977.
- Verification: 1,977 is within 2,000, and 1,977 + 40 = 2,017 exceeds it, so exactly 50 items.

**Example 5: compare sample deviation rate with tolerable rate**
- Formula: sample deviation rate = deviations ÷ sample size.
- Given: 60 items tested, 3 deviations, tolerable error rate 4 percent.
- Step 1: 3 ÷ 60 = 0.05, which is 5 percent.
- Step 2: 5 percent exceeds the 4 percent tolerable rate.
- Step 3: the control cannot be relied upon; extend substantive testing.
- Verification: 4 percent of 60 is 2.4, and 3 deviations exceed 2.4, matching.
- ⚠ Check: formal evaluation uses the upper precision limit, above 5 percent.

**Example 6: monetary unit sampling interval ➕**
- Formula: interval = population book value ÷ sample size.
- Given: book value $2,000,000, sample size 50.
- Step 1: interval = 2,000,000 ÷ 50 = $40,000.
- Step 2: any single item of $40,000 or more is selected for certain.
- Verification: 50 × 40,000 = $2,000,000, matching the population value.

### Sample selection steps

- Step 1: determine the objective of the test.
- Step 2: define the population.
- Step 3: determine the method.
- Objective comes first because it drives population and method.
- Exam traps
  - Defining the objective precedes choosing sampling method or size.
  - Auditor does not pick items before the population is defined.

### Special situations

- Population too small for a valid sample: redesign the test, such as testing 100% 📓⭐
- Example: only a handful of access requests exist in a new application.
- Correct logic: choose a different test approach suited to the population.
- Do not conclude "deficient" merely because sampling is impossible.
- Do not fabricate simulated items to reach a sample size.
- ⚠ Check: manual text is silent on small populations ➕.

## 1.6.3 Sampling risk

- **Sampling risk**
  - Definition: chance the sample points to a wrong conclusion.
  - Use: lets the auditor plan sample size to limit wrong conclusions.
  - Audit impact: conclusion may differ from testing the whole population.
- Two types exist: incorrect acceptance and incorrect rejection.

- **Risk of incorrect acceptance**
  - Definition: weakness judged unlikely, but population is actually materially misstated.
  - Use: the more dangerous error, because problems stay hidden.
  - Audit impact: auditor gives a clean result wrongly.
- **Risk of incorrect rejection**
  - Definition: weakness judged likely, but population is not materially misstated.
  - Use: causes extra work and unneeded findings.
  - Audit impact: inefficiency and false alarms.
- Example: sample shows no exceptions, yet unsampled records hold major errors.
- ⚠ Check: "more dangerous" is common teaching, not in the manual ➕.

### Distinct from audit risk

- Audit risk has inherent, control and detection risk components.
- Detection risk includes sampling risk and non-sampling risk.
- Non-sampling risk is unrelated to sampling, such as human error or wrong procedures.
- Manual warns: do not confuse audit risk with statistical sampling risk.
- Exam traps
  - Sampling risk is about wrong assumptions drawn from a sample, not control weakness.
  - Human error is non-sampling risk; a larger sample won't fix it.
  - Sampling risk is part of detection risk, not inherent or control risk.

---

## Takeaway

- Compliance tests controls; substantive tests numbers; weak controls mean more substantive work.
- Attribute means how often, variable means how much.
- Fraud concern means discovery sampling; few expected errors means stop-or-go.
- Distinct subgroups mean stratified sampling.
- Higher confidence or expected error enlarges samples; larger precision amount shrinks them.
- Tiny population means redesign the test; sampling risk is not audit risk.
