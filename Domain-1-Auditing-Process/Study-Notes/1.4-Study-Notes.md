# 1.4 Types of Controls and Considerations: Study Notes

## 1.4.0 Opening idea: what an effective control does

- Control
  - Definition: a policy, procedure, practice or structure that reduces risk.
  - Use: keeps the organization on track toward its business objectives.
  - Audit impact: auditors judge controls by whether they reduce risk, not by existence.
- An effective control prevents, detects or contains an incident, and enables recovery.
- Controls combine policies, procedures, practices and organizational structures.
- Example: a locked server room plus a visitor log plus a written access policy.
- ⚠ Check: manual says "prevents, detects and/or contains"; recovery is an outcome, no class.

## 1.4.1 Internal controls

- Internal control
  - Definition: rules and structures that make sure management's directions are carried out.
  - Use: give reasonable assurance that business objectives will be met.
  - Audit impact: auditors test whether they exist, are designed well, and operate.
- Reasonable assurance
  - Definition: a high but not absolute level of confidence.
  - Use: acknowledges cost limits and human error in every control system.
  - Audit impact: never report that controls guarantee zero loss.
- Controls operate at all levels and in all functions of the organization.
- Everyone takes part, but the board and senior management own the culture.
- Board and senior management also monitor the system's effectiveness continuously.
- Example: a CEO that ignores policy breaks undermines every lower-level control.
- Control activities (manual's list)
  - Approvals and authorizations
    - Definition: formal sign-off before a transaction or change proceeds.
    - Audit impact: look for evidence of approval by the correct authority.
  - Verifications and reconciliations
    - Definition: comparing records to confirm they agree.
    - Example: matching the bank statement to the cash ledger monthly.
  - Reviewing operating performance
    - Definition: management comparing actual results with expectations.
  - Securing assets
    - Definition: restricting physical and logical access to valuable items.
  - Separation of duties (SoD)
    - Definition: no single person controls all steps of a sensitive process.
    - Use: prevents fraud or error without a second person noticing.
    - Audit impact: SoD gaps are a classic high-risk finding.
    - Example: the person who creates vendors cannot approve their payments.
- Controls may be manual, automated or both.
- Exam traps
  - Responsibility for the control culture sits with the board and senior management, not IT.
  - "Reasonable" assurance is the right wording; "absolute" or "complete" is wrong.
  - Automated controls still need manual oversight, so "fully automated" is not a safe answer.

## 1.4.2 Control objectives and control measures

- Control objective
  - Definition: the result or purpose a set of controls should achieve.
  - Use: links day-to-day control activities to company strategy.
  - Audit impact: auditors judge controls against their stated objective.
- Control objective is explicitly related to the company's overall strategy.
- Control measure
  - Definition: an activity that helps fulfil a control objective.
  - Use: breaks strategy into tasks assignable to roles or job descriptions.
  - Audit impact: auditors test the measure to conclude on the objective.
- Objective is the why; measure is what is done.
- Example: objective is accurate payroll; measure is dual review of the payroll register.
- Objectives may relate to
  - Effectiveness and efficiency of operations.
  - Reliability of financial reporting.
  - Compliance with laws and regulations.
  - Safeguarding information assets.
- Objectives apply to manual and automated controls alike, for example reviewing system logs.
- Objectives are the same in IS and manual settings; only implementation differs.
- ⚠ Check: manual gives these four as examples, not a closed set.
- Audit objective vs control objective
  - Control objective: the result a control should achieve.
  - Audit objective: what the audit itself must accomplish.
  - ⚠ Check: manual 1.4 never defines audit objective; it appears under 1.5.
- Exam traps
  - Audit objectives and control objectives differ; auditor sets one, management the other.
  - Objectives do not change in IS, so "new computer objectives" is rarely right.

### IS control objectives

- IS control objective
  - Definition: a high-level requirement for controlling an IT process area.
  - Use: gives management a checklist of what effective IT control should achieve.
  - Audit impact: forms criteria for audit scope and conclusions.
- Management's choices on objectives
  - Select the ones that apply.
  - Decide which ones to implement.
  - Choose how: frequency, span, automation.
  - Accept the risk of not implementing others.
- Audit impact: accepted risk must be a conscious, documented management decision.
- Specific IS control objectives
  - Safeguard assets: information current and protected from improper access.
  - SDLC processes are repeatable, reliable and aligned to business objectives.
  - Integrity of operating system environments, including network management.
  - Integrity of critical application environments and customer data.
  - Correct identification and authentication of users, including infrastructure support staff.
  - Efficiency and effectiveness of operations.
  - Compliance with user requirements, policies, laws and regulations.
  - Availability through BCPs and DRPs with backup and recovery.
  - Incident response plan to better protect data and systems.
  - Change management for system integrity and reliability.
  - Outsourced services with defined SLAs and protective contract terms.
- ⚠ Check: outline's four-item IS objectives list is not verbatim in manual.

#### Application integrity objectives

- Input authorization
  - Definition: each transaction approved and entered only once.
  - Audit impact: missing authorization allows fictitious or duplicate transactions.
- Input validation
  - Definition: each input is checked so it cannot harm processing.
- Processing accuracy and completeness
  - Definition: transactions recorded correctly, in full, in the proper period.
  - Audit impact: wrong-period posting distorts financial reporting.
- Reliability of overall processing: all system actions during processing are sound.
- Output accuracy, completeness and security
  - Definition: outputs can be relied on and are protected.
- Database confidentiality, integrity and availability depend on general IS security controls.
- Example: a payment file keyed twice is an input authorization failure.
- Exam traps
  - Input controls come first; output checks cannot repair weak input.
  - Duplicate or unapproved entries point to authorization, not validation, objectives.

#### General control methods

- General control methods apply to all areas of an organization.
- Governance and organizational controls; physical and logical access controls.
  - ⚠ Check: these two headings are outline grouping; manual's table has three categories.
- Managerial (administrative) controls
  - Definition: controls on oversight, reporting, procedures and process operation.
  - Use: direct how people and processes behave.
  - Audit impact: weak management controls make technical controls fail.
  - Policies and procedures.
  - Accounting controls such as balancing.
  - Employee training and development.
  - Compliance reporting.
  - Day-to-day operational controls are often grouped here too.
- Technical (logical) controls
  - Definition: controls delivered through technology, equipment or devices.
  - Use: enforce rules automatically, such as access or filtering.
  - Audit impact: they depend on managerial controls to operate correctly.
  - Firewall rulesets.
  - Network or host-based intrusion detection systems.
  - Passwords.
  - Antimalware solutions.
- Physical controls
  - Definition: controls that physically restrict access to facilities or hardware.
  - Use: protect equipment, people and media.
  - Audit impact: need maintenance, monitoring and ability to react to alerts.
  - Access badges and locks.
  - Closed-circuit TV (CCTV).
- Balance of control types
  - Example: a firewall needs trained staff, procedures, monitoring and regular testing.
  - Missing coinciding controls create a false sense of security.
  - Result: unidentified vulnerabilities, wasted resources and greater risk than intended.
- Examples of general controls
  - Systems development methodologies and change control.
  - Operations procedures.
  - Systems programming and technical support.
  - Quality assurance procedures.
  - Physical access controls.
  - BCP and DRP.
  - Networks and communications: LAN, WAN, wireless.
  - Database administration.
  - Protective and detective mechanisms against internal and external attacks.
- Exam traps
  - Technology alone is not a complete answer; check for the supporting managerial control.
  - Category (managerial, technical, physical) differs from function (preventive, detective).

#### IS-specific controls

- IS-specific control
  - Definition: a general control method translated into IT terms.
  - Use: builds safeguards into sensitive or critical system functions.
  - Audit impact: auditors map general methods to concrete IT procedures.
- Two families in the outline: application controls and infrastructure and operations controls.
- Example: "safeguard assets" becomes access controls over programs, data and equipment.
- Examples listed
  - Strategy and direction of the IT function.
  - General organization and management of the IT function.
  - Access to IT resources, including data and programs.
- ⚠ Check: manual 1.4 does not name "infrastructure and operations controls"; outline framing.

#### Business process applications and controls

- Business process control assurance
  - Definition: evaluating controls at process and activity level.
  - Use: gives assurance that an end-to-end process is controlled.
  - Audit impact: mixes management, programmed and manual controls.
- In integrated environments, controls are embedded in the business application.
- Auditor also evaluates process owner controls
  - Security and SoD.
  - Periodic access reviews.
  - Access approvals and application controls.
- Input, processing and output controls are covered in 3.4.
  - ⚠ Check: the manual excerpt does not give this cross-reference; confirm the chapter location.
- Auditor must first gain a clear understanding of the application under review.
- Computerized application audit challenges
  - Limited audit trails.
  - Instantaneous updating.
  - Information overload.
- Sample application risks and controls (manual's table)
  - Ecommerce
    - Risk: SQL injection from internet exposure.
    - Controls: secure coding training, code reviews, form input validity checks.
  - Electronic data interchange (EDI)
    - Risk: transmitted data intercepted and manipulated.
    - Control: encryption for confidentiality and integrity.
  - Email
    - Risk: social engineering of users.
    - Controls: spam filtering, hyperlink verification, phishing training.
  - Industrial control systems (SCADA, DCS, PLC)
    - Risk: compromise can directly affect human life.
    - Controls: network segmentation and multifactor authentication for access.
  - AI and expert systems
    - Risk: learned data and decision trees can be inherently biased.
    - Control: confirm proper expertise went into assumptions and formulas.
- Use authoritative guidance such as OWASP when reviewing ecommerce.
- Bring in external experts when the audit team lacks the skills.
- Exam traps
  - Match the control to the risk: encryption for interception, input checks for injection.
  - Missing skills means engaging experts, not skipping or guessing the review.
  - ICS risk is safety-related, so segmentation beats a purely cost-based answer.

## 1.4.3 Control classifications ⭐

- The manual classes control strength elements as preventive, detective or corrective.
- Figure 1.7 also lists deterrent and compensating categories.
- Preventive controls are generally stronger because they stop the threat event.
- Detective and preventive controls reduce likelihood of a threat event.
- Corrective controls mitigate consequences.
- Mix of types complements each other; choose by risk and cost-benefit.
- Threat-vulnerability-impact flow
  - Threat exploits vulnerability and produces impact.
  - Preventive and deterrent controls act before; detective during; corrective after.
  - Compensating controls cover a gap beside other controls.

### Preventive controls 📓

- Preventive control
  - Definition: stops a problem before it happens. 📓
  - Use: inhibit or impede attempts to violate security policy.
  - Audit impact: strongest, so gaps here carry the highest severity.
- Stops errors, fraud or omissions proactively. 📓
- Segregation of duties. 📓
- Using qualified resources. 📓
- Well-designed documentation. 📓
- Encryption, user authentication, vault-construction doors.
- Example: multifactor authentication blocks a login with a stolen password.

### Detective controls 📓

- Detective control
  - Definition: finds a problem after it has occurred. 📓
  - Use: warn of violations without stopping the action.
  - Audit impact: only useful if someone reviews alerts and responds.
- Identifies errors after they occur and gives visibility into issues. 📓
- Checkpoints in production jobs. 📓
- Echo messages in telecom protocols. 📓
- Audit trails, IDS, checksums.
- Example: an IDS raises an alert but does not block the packet.
- ⚠ Check: user notes call hidden cameras detective; manual lists security cameras as deterrent.

### Corrective controls 📓

- Corrective control
  - Definition: fixes or limits damage once a problem is found. 📓
  - Use: remediate errors, omissions, unauthorized use and intrusions.
  - Audit impact: assess speed and tested reliability of the fix.
- Corrects errors and lets processing continue. 📓
- A check routine that fixes errors automatically. 📓
- Data backups, error correction, automated failover.
- Example: after one machine is breached, isolate it to protect the network.

### Deterrent controls 📓

- Deterrent control
  - Definition: discourages people from trying something. 📓
  - Use: give warnings that dissuade intentional or accidental compromise.
  - Audit impact: weak alone; it never stops a determined attacker.
- Warning signs and visible barriers. 📓
- Visible cameras deter; hidden cameras only detect. 📓
- Warning banners on login screens.
- Acceptable use policies.
- Rewards for the arrest of hackers.
- ⚠ Check: the manual files CCTV under deterrent, with no visible versus hidden distinction.

### Compensating controls 📓

- Compensating control
  - Definition: an alternate control that offsets a missing or weak one.
  - Use: cover for baseline controls that cannot meet a requirement.
  - Audit impact: must achieve the same result as the control it replaces.
- Offsets a deficiency in the control structure. 📓
- Does not fix the vulnerability; it makes exploitation harder.
- Isolated network segment with strong perimeter security for unsecured systems.
- Third-party challenge-response for devices without individual logins.
- Example: logging and independent review of developer production access. 📓
- Developers may need emergency production access, so review replaces prohibition. 📓

### Recovery controls 📓➕

- Recovery control
  - Definition: restores systems or data to normal after a failure. ➕
  - Use: return operations after loss or damage.
- Example: restoring a server from backup media. 📓
- ⚠ Check: recovery is no separate manual class; backups appear under corrective.

### Directive controls ➕

- Directive control
  - Definition: policies and procedures that tell people what to do. ➕
  - Use: set expected behavior.
- ⚠ Check: absent from manual's classification; unlikely as an ISACA answer.

### Control purpose (Figure 1.8)

- Reduce likelihood
  - Infrastructure monitoring.
  - Capacity management.
  - Risk management.
  - Configuration management.
- Mitigate consequences
  - Backup and recovery.
  - BC or IT DR plan.
  - Service desk.
  - Special clauses in vendor contracts.
  - Spare processing site.
- Exam traps
  - Classify by when the control acts relative to the event, not by its technology.
  - If it stops the event, it is preventive; alerts only means detective.
  - Detective alone is the wrong answer when the question asks to stop the event.
  - Corrective follows detection; a fix with no detection step cannot work.
  - Compensating answers fit only when a stated constraint blocks the normal control.
  - Compensating control that weakens the objective is wrong, even if cheaper.
  - A mix of control types beats any single type.

## 1.4.4 Control relationship to risk

- Risk is addressed through control; control is justified by the risk it addresses.
- Auditor maps management's controls back to applicable risk.
- Management must document and implement controls per its risk assessment.
- Understanding risk sets audit procedures and the materiality of weaknesses.
- Vulnerability
  - Definition: a weakness that a threat can exploit. 📓
  - Use: the starting point for deciding where controls are needed.
  - Audit impact: lack of control measures indicates a vulnerability. 📓⭐
- Next step after finding vulnerabilities: assess threats and their likelihood. 📓
- Inherent risk
  - Definition: risk level before any management controls. 📓
- Residual risk
  - Definition: risk remaining after controls are applied. 📓
  - Audit impact: compare residual risk to the organization's tolerance.
- ⚠ Check: manual 1.4 does not define inherent or residual risk; notes do.
- If risk exceeds tolerance, add more controls.
- If proper controls are impossible, consider compensating controls.
- Compensating control must achieve the same result as the underperforming control.
- Non-IT compensating controls can exist.
- Risk responses
  - Avoidance: stop or move away from the risk. 📓⭐
    - Example: relocating a data center out of a flood zone.
  - Reduction or mitigation: lowers probability while the activity continues. 📓
  - Transfer: shift the financial impact, for example insurance or outsourcing.
  - Acceptance: knowingly keep the risk. 📓⭐
    - Audit impact: needs documented approval from senior management.
- ⚠ Check: manual 1.4.4 has no risk-response list; this comes from user notes.
- Senior business management owns risk levels. 📓
- Exam traps
  - After spotting a vulnerability, assess threats and likelihood before recommending controls.
  - Auditors verify acceptance is documented and approved; they do not accept risk themselves.
  - Moving the asset away is avoidance; adding safeguards while operating is reduction.
  - Insurance or outsourcing is transfer; accountability for the risk still stays with owners.

## 1.4.5 Prescriptive controls and frameworks

- Prescriptive control set
  - Definition: an authority's ready-made list of controls to implement and assess.
  - Use: gives a standard baseline to mitigate risk across the organization.
  - Audit impact: gives clear criteria for assessing compliance.
- CIS 18 Critical Security Controls
  - Definition: prioritized, simplified cybersecurity best practices.
  - Use: strengthen cybersecurity posture in a sensible order.
- OWASP SAMM
  - Definition: open framework for software security strategy.
  - Use: tailor secure software practices to the risks faced.
- SOC reports (AICPA)
  - Definition: framework for reporting on controls at service providers.
  - Use: customers gain assurance over outsourced service controls.
- PCI DSS
  - Definition: required controls for anyone touching credit card data.
  - Use: protect card data stored, processed or transmitted.
- CSA Cloud Controls Matrix (CCM)
  - Definition: cloud security control framework.
  - Use: guides cloud vendors and helps customers assess provider risk.
- Organizations must identify countermeasures that meet each control objective.
- Some prescribed controls may not apply to the business.
- Example: a merchant that stores no card data skips stored-data controls.
- Non-applicability needs formally documented reasons and validation.
- Also relevant: COBIT, ISO 27001, NIST (see 2.2 and 5.1). ➕
- ⚠ Check: COBIT, ISO 27001 and NIST are not in the manual's 1.4.5 list.
- ⚠ Check: the outline repeats the framework list twice; merged here.
- Exam traps
  - Undocumented non-applicability is a finding, even when the control truly does not apply.
  - A framework gives a baseline; it does not replace the organization's own risk assessment.

## 1.4.6 Evaluation of the control environment

- The control environment is reviewed under the risk-based audit plan.
- IS management should also evaluate control effectiveness itself.

### Management control monitoring

- Management control monitoring
  - Definition: management checking its own controls between audits.
  - Use: catch deviations before a less frequent audit would.
  - Audit impact: auditor may rely on results as evidence controls worked over time.
- Monitoring confirms requirements are met.
- Monitoring confirms standards are followed.
- Monitoring confirms employees comply with policies and procedures.
- Management uses results to continuously improve the security program.
- Auditor reviews the monitoring process
  - Exceptions are remediated and lessons learned applied.
  - Metrics rest on management's risk assessment.
  - Metrics give specific, quantifiable outputs.
  - Independence is considered for completeness and accuracy concerns.
  - Reporting sets effectiveness thresholds and tracks success over time.
- Example: monthly access recertification results reviewed by the security manager.
- Exam traps
  - Relying on management monitoring without checking its independence is weak audit logic.
  - Metrics must come from a risk assessment, not just whatever is easy to count.

### Independent evaluation of the control environment

- Independent evaluation
  - Definition: the auditor's own judgment of whether controls work.
  - Use: concludes on control effectiveness against audit-planning objectives.
  - Audit impact: requires judgment and experience, not a checklist alone.
- Understand applicable risk and controls first.
- Review evidence to decide if operations are well controlled and effective.
- Assess strengths and weaknesses against the control objectives.
- Exam traps
  - Conclusions rest on evidence reviewed, not on management's assertions alone.
  - Judge control effectiveness against objectives, not against the number of controls.

## Takeaway

- Control classes are preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent and compensating.
- Preventive is strongest; detective and preventive cut likelihood, corrective cuts impact.
- A compensating control must achieve the same result as the weak control.
- Technical controls fail without managerial and physical support around them.
- After finding a vulnerability, assess threats and likelihood before recommending anything.
- Risk acceptance needs documented senior approval; auditors verify, never decide.
