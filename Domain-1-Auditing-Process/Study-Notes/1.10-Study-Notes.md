## 1.10 Basics: why audit quality needs its own process

- Quality: how well an audit meets standards and gives dependable results.
- Definition: Quality assurance (QA) is planned, systematic activity giving confidence in quality.
- Use: QA keeps audits consistent, reliable, and improving over time.
- Audit impact: an unreviewed audit function may issue unreliable opinions.
- Manual premise: the audit process itself must improve continuously.
- Example: a firm audits others yearly but never reviews its own working papers.
- Example: that firm's audit conclusions cannot be trusted until it adds review checks.
- Four parts in manual: committee oversight, audit QA, training, monitoring.

## 1.10.1 Audit committee oversight 📓

- Definition: Audit committee is a governing body overseeing the audit function and its independence.
- Use: it oversees the IS audit function and its chief audit executive link.
- Audit impact: independent oversight protects audit fairness and thoroughness. 📓
- Definition: Chief audit executive (CAE) is the head of the audit function.
- Use: the CAE reports to the committee, not to the auditees.
- Audit impact: reporting to auditees would weaken independence.
- No committee exists: a designated group or individual assumes oversight duties.
- Oversight function plus continuous performance monitoring should be formally established.
- Monitoring is reviewed through periodic reporting.
- Example: a quarterly report to the committee shows plan completion and open findings.
- Example: a small firm names its board chair as the oversight owner.
- ⚠ Check: "fair and thorough" wording is from your notes, not the manual.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: the committee oversees the audit function for fair, thorough audits.
  - Distractor "approves audit policy" is a task, not the primary role of oversight.
  - Distractors about appointing officers or budgets are secondary committee duties.
  - Oversight is not the same as performing or supervising each audit.

## 1.10.2 Audit quality assurance (QA) 📓

- Definition: Audit QA program is a structured set of checks on audit work quality.
- Use: it provides a framework to improve audit effectiveness. 📓
- Audit impact: without it, errors and inconsistency go undetected.
- Ownership: audit leadership and assigned project leads own individual audit quality.
- Their duty: ensure documented audit procedures are followed.
- Definition: Documented audit procedures are written rules guiding how audits are done.
- Forms: audit manuals, wikis, sampling guidance, and similar tools.
- Requirement: procedures must be clearly identified and known to all applicable members.
- Audit impact: unknown procedures cannot be followed or enforced.
- Example: a wiki page defines sample sizes, and every auditor must cite it.
- Executive management commitment and support is the main success factor. 📓⭐
- ⚠ Check: manual 1.10.2 omits this; it comes from your notes ➕.
- Definition: Key performance indicator (KPI) is a measurable value tracking performance.
- Use: continuously improving KPIs demonstrate QA program success. 📓
- ⚠ Check: manual 1.10.2 lists no KPIs ➕; rely on your notes.
- Definition: Issue closure rate is the share of audit findings management has resolved.
- Use: a rising rate signals management responsiveness to audit recommendations. 📓
- Audit impact: it measures management action, not audit workload or finding quality.
- Example: closure rising from 60 percent to 85 percent shows prompter management action.
- ⚠ Check: the closure-rate point is not in the manual ➕.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: top-level backing drives QA success, not tools or strict policies.
  - Faster reports and lower costs do not prove improved quality.
  - Training helps quality but is not the success factor.
  - Higher closure rate means management responds, not that issues were few.
  - Closure rate does not prove recommendations were simple.

## 1.10.3 Audit team training and development 📓

- Definition: Continuing education is ongoing learning that keeps auditor skills current.
- Use: it equips auditors to address emerging risks. 📓
- Audit impact: without it, skills become outdated and audit quality falls. 📓
- Worst outcome of neglect: outdated skills and knowledge, not cost or staffing issues.
- Manual requires a formal development plan for all audit function members.
- Plan includes applicable training programs and certifications by role.
- Leadership must create a budget that supports training and development needs.
- Example: a cloud migration starts, so auditors get cloud assurance training first.
- Best training indicator: expanded audit coverage of previously unexplored areas. 📓
- Why: it shows new skills producing new audit work.
- ⚠ Check: coverage indicator is not in manual ➕; budget and hours are inputs.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: choose outcome measures over activity or input measures.
  - Training budget measures spending, not capability gained.
  - Training hours and exams passed show effort, not applied skill.
  - "Continuous education builds confidence" is a side effect, not the purpose.
  - Purpose is addressing emerging risks, not merely relying on new technology.

## 1.10.4 Monitoring

- Definition: Monitoring is regular checking that audit function requirements are being met.
- Use: it keeps the audit process continuing and compliant.
- Audit impact: gaps found early prevent weak or non-compliant audit work.
- Four manual examples: audit QA, independence, certification, and education monitoring.

### Audit QA monitoring

- QA results should be reviewed periodically and summarized.
- Purpose: spot trends and lessons learned.
- Actionable QA items should be remediated and tracked formally.
- Audit impact: untracked actions repeat the same audit weaknesses.

### Independence monitoring

- Definition: a process letting auditors self-report possible impairments of independence.
- Use: it can be built into the wider audit function.
- Leadership and auditors periodically check that independence is intact.
- Changes are reported through the established reporting process.
- Example: an auditor reports a new family tie to an auditee manager.
- Audit impact: unreported impairment can invalidate findings and opinions.

### Certification and accreditation

- Definition: Accreditation is formal recognition that the audit function meets recognized standards.
- Use: ownership is assigned to appropriate members of audit leadership.
- Owners maintain compliance with certification or accreditation bodies.
- Primary advantage: improved reliability of audit outcomes. 📓⭐
- Credibility, stakeholder confidence, and standards alignment are results of that reliability.
- ⚠ Check: manual assigns ownership only; "primary advantage" is from your notes ➕.

### Quality management system (QMS) 📓

- Definition: QMS is a formal framework of standardized, documented processes.
- Use: it standardizes processes for consistency and reliability.
- Audit impact: similar audits yield comparable results across teams.
- ⚠ Check: QMS is not mentioned in 1.10 of the manual ➕.
- It does not remove the need for performance monitoring.
- It does not mainly reduce cost.

### Continued professional education (CPE) monitoring

- Leadership must have a process monitoring auditor CPE compliance.
- Requirements may come from an internal plan or external certifying bodies.
- Audit impact: lapsed certification can undermine auditor credentials and function accreditation.

### Review of work papers and deliverables

- Audit leadership reviews work papers and final deliverables such as reports.
- A formal review process should exist for all audit types.
- Review forms named: detail review, engagement quality review, and QA.
- Depth depends on risk and guidance from authoritative sources.
- Example: a high-risk audit gets engagement quality review before report release.
- Exam traps
  - Correct logic: monitoring tracks compliance and remediation, not just collection of results.
  - Self-reported independence beats waiting for auditees to complain.
  - Accreditation proves reliable outcomes, not lower cost or faster audits.
  - Review level follows risk, not equal effort for every audit.

## Case study (Betatronics)

- Stem: mid-sized electronics manufacturer with US headquarters and Latin American factories.
- An internal IS auditor does preliminary work for a coming regulatory compliance review.
- The rules require management to run and test a controlled general IT environment.
- Areas: logical and physical security, change management, production, governance, end-user computing.
- Prior issues: shared admin accounts, weak password controls, poor segregation, undocumented changes.
- OS server updates were only partially effective.
- The audit charter names the audit committee as overseer.
- Company growth is double the industry average, with rising employee turnover.
- Manual questions ask first action, most concerning logical security finding, sample selection.
- The case also asks for general IT controls to test and year-one priority.

## Takeaway

- Committee or designated owner gives independent oversight of the audit function.
- Executive commitment drives QA success; improving KPIs prove it.
- Rising issue closure shows management responsiveness, not fewer issues.
- Training success is shown by wider audit coverage, not spending.
- Accreditation mainly improves reliability of audit outcomes.
- Monitoring covers QA, independence, accreditation, CPE, and risk-based review.
