## 1.9 Overview: why communication matters

### Basics
- Audit value depends on findings being understood and acted on.
- Findings need auditee buy-in, or corrective action stalls.
- Reporting skill is as important as testing skill.

### Communication skills
- **Facilitation**
  - Definition: guiding a group discussion toward an agreed outcome.
  - Use: running meetings, walk-throughs and exit interviews smoothly.
  - Audit impact: keeps discussions factual and reduces defensiveness.
- **Negotiation**
  - Definition: reaching a mutually acceptable position through give and take.
  - Use: agreeing realistic recommendations and implementation dates with auditee management.
  - Audit impact: auditor may adjust the fix, never the finding.
  - Example: management cannot afford a new tool, so the auditor accepts a compensating control.
- **Conflict resolution**
  - Definition: settling disagreements without damaging the working relationship.
  - Use: handling disputes over facts, severity or ownership of findings.
  - Audit impact: unresolved conflict delays reports and corrective action.

### Materiality
- **Materiality**
  - Definition: relative importance of a finding, based on business impact.
  - Use: deciding which findings go to which level of management.
  - Audit impact: over-reporting buries key issues; under-reporting hides real risk.
  - Example: a weak lock at one branch matters locally, not to headquarters.
- Materiality is judged by the potential effect if nothing is corrected.
- Materiality differs for each management level.

### Recipient perspective
- Consider each recipient's motivations, concerns and viewpoint before reporting.
- Tailor tone and detail to the reader, not the auditor's preference.

### Exam traps
- Best answer favors clear, tactful, business-impact communication.
- Distractor: dropping a finding to avoid conflict; this breaks integrity.
- Distractor: reporting every minor issue upward; this ignores materiality.

## 1.9.1 Communicating audit results

### Exit interview
- **Exit interview**
  - Definition: closing meeting held with auditee management at audit end.
  - Use: confirm facts, test recommendations, and agree implementation dates.
  - Audit impact: prevents surprises and factual errors in the final report.
- Auditor confirms report facts are correct and material.
- Auditor confirms recommendations are realistic and cost-effective.
- If recommendations are not realistic, seek alternatives through negotiation.
- Auditor recommends implementation dates for agreed recommendations.
- Example: management disputes a cost estimate; both sides agree a cheaper control.

### Communication order 📓⭐
- Discuss findings with auditee management first, before senior management. 📓⭐
- Purpose is to gain agreement on findings and corrective action.
- Do this before the report reaches senior management.
- On disagreement, explain the finding's significance and risk of not correcting.
- Auditor persuades with risk; the auditor does not drop the finding.
- After agreement, audit management briefs senior auditee management.
- ⚠ Check: "management first" is the manual's normal sequence, not an absolute rule.
- ⚠ Check: the manual excerpt here does not cover suspected fraud or illegal acts.
- Fraud involving management may need different escalation; verify in ITAF before relying on this.

### Reporting line and independence
- Auditor is ultimately responsible to senior management and the audit committee.
- Auditor may freely raise concerns with them.
- Manual wording: blocking access by levels below senior management limits independence.
- ⚠ Check: outline says "lower levels"; manual says "levels lower than senior management".
- **Audit committee**
  - Definition: board-level group, usually of non-employees, overseeing audit.
  - Use: receives periodic summaries of audit activity.
  - Audit impact: gives the auditor an independent route for sensitive findings.
- Summary of audit activities goes to the committee periodically, not per finding.

### Auditee requests for help
- Auditee may ask the auditor to help implement recommended controls.
- Auditor explains the difference between auditor and consultant roles.
- Assisting can damage independence.
- Example: auditor who designs the fix later audits their own design.

### Exam traps
- Correct logic: validate facts with auditee management, then escalate upward.
- Distractor: going straight to the audit committee skips agreement and fact checks.
- Distractor: implementing the fix yourself solves the problem but harms independence.
- Distractor: waiting for the auditee to agree before reporting; agreement is sought, not required.

## 1.9.2 Audit report objectives

### Six objectives
- Formally present results to auditee, and to client if different.
- Serve as formal closure of the engagement.
- Give assurance statements and, if needed, corrective areas and recommendations.
- Act as a reference for anyone later researching the auditee or topic.
- Form the basis for a follow-up audit if findings exist.
- Promote audit credibility through a well-developed, well-written report.

### Where objectives come from
- Report objectives derive from auditee and other users' requirements.
- They must also meet IS audit standards and audit organization protocols.
- Stakeholders are identified during audit planning.
- Auditor sets scope and objectives using risk, materiality and control appropriateness.
- Regulatory and IT governance requirements also shape scope.

### Report quality
- Every report gives unbiased, well-supported answers to the audit objectives.
- Example: objective is data center access control; report gives an adequacy opinion.
- If controls need strengthening, the report includes a recommendation.

### Standards
- **ISACA Standard 1401 Reporting**
  - Definition: standard governing how audit results are reported.
  - Use: sets expectations for report content and quality.
  - Audit impact: reports must follow it to count as compliant.
- **ISACA Standard 1402 Follow-up Activities**
  - Definition: standard requiring checks on agreed corrective actions.
  - Use: drives the follow-up program.
  - Audit impact: an audit without follow-up is incomplete.
- Manual names these as the standards a candidate should know.

### Exam traps
- Report must answer the stated objectives, not whatever the auditor found interesting.
- Distractor: a report that lists tests performed without a conclusion fails the objective.

## 1.9.3 Audit report structure and contents

### Introduction section
- States audit objectives and limitations.
- States scope and period of audit coverage.
- Summarizes nature and extent of procedures and processes examined.
- Includes a statement of methodology and guidelines used.

### Findings section
- Findings appear in separate sections.
- Often grouped by materiality, intended recipient, or both.
- Level of detail depends on the recipient and upper management guidance.
- Example: audit committee report omits issues only relevant to local management.
- Minor findings may go to management by memorandum.
- Report must include all significant findings.

### Conclusion and opinion
- **Overall conclusion and opinion**
  - Definition: auditor's judgment on adequacy of controls and procedures examined.
  - Use: answers the audit objective in a single clear statement.
  - Audit impact: must be supported by the findings and evidence.
- Opinion includes actual potential risk arising from detected deficiencies.
- Controls may be stated as adequate or inadequate.
- **Reservations or qualifications**
  - Definition: stated limits or exceptions affecting the audit opinion.
  - Use: warns readers where conclusions are less certain.
  - Audit impact: omitting them can mislead readers and expose the auditor.

### Writing each finding
- Describe the finding, its cause and its risk.
- Identify criteria applied: organizational, professional and governmental.
- Explain complex or confidential matters in a separate document.
- Reference that separate document from the main report.
- Example: detailed vulnerability data goes in a restricted annex.

### Timing and independence
- Issue the report promptly to encourage corrective action.
- Communicate significant findings early to proper persons when appropriate.
- Early communication must not change the report's intent or content.
- Auditor makes the final decision on content.
- Include positive comments on improved or effective controls for balance.
- Exercise independence throughout the reporting process.

### Management response
- Auditee management evaluates findings.
- Response states corrective actions and timing.
- Obtain a firm commitment on the implementation date.
- Also obtain how the fix will be performed.
- Reason: the corrective action itself may introduce risk.
- Constraints may delay action: staff limits, budgets, other projects.
- Auditor should not expect other changes to pause for audit fixes.
- Auditor may report progress on recommendations to senior management.
- Recommendations and dates are discussed while the report is being finalized.

### Exam traps
- Correct logic: auditor owns report content; management owns the response.
- Distractor: removing a finding because management objects violates independence.
- Distractor: delaying the report until all fixes finish defeats timeliness.
- Distractor: a negative-only report; balance requires noting effective controls.
- ⚠ Check: outline implies grouping is mandatory; manual says "often grouped".

## 1.9.4 Audit documentation

### Basics
- **Audit documentation (work papers)**
  - Definition: written record supporting the statements in the auditor's report.
  - Use: proves the work done and supports conclusions.
  - Audit impact: unsupported findings are indefensible and credibility suffers.
- Shows the engagement complied with standards.
- Supports the basis for conclusions and opinions.
- Work papers bridge audit objectives and the final report.
- Traceability should run from objectives to report and back.
- The report can be viewed as a set of particular work papers.

### Minimum contents
- Planning and preparation of scope and objectives.
- Description or walk-throughs of the audited area.
- The audit program.
- Audit steps performed and evidence gathered.
- Use of other auditors and experts.
- Findings, conclusions and recommendations.
- Document identification and dates.

### Recommended extras
- Copy of the issued report.
- Evidence of supervisory review.
- Content required by law, regulation, contract and professional standards.

### Quality of work papers
- Documentation must be clear, complete, retrievable and comprehensible.
- Work papers should be dated, initialed and page-numbered.
- They should be relevant, self-contained, properly labeled and filed.
- Format and media are optional; automated work papers are acceptable.
- Protect integrity of test evidence to preserve its proof value.
- Timing of evidence can be crucial to supporting findings.

### Ownership and access
- Documentation is generally the property of the auditee.
- Only authorized personnel may access it, under specific or general permission.
- External requests need prior senior management and legal counsel approval.
- Policies should cover custody, retention and release.
- ⚠ Check: ownership is "generally" the auditee's; the auditor still controls custody.

### Materiality in practice
- Materiality decides which findings reach the report.
- Judge what would be significant to each level of management.
- Example: remote-site physical access weakness matters locally, perhaps not at headquarters.
- Example: terminated user access caught by management review is documented, not escalated.
- Such a finding is still discussed with auditee management.

### Exam traps
- Correct logic: document everything, escalate only what is material.
- Distractor: discarding minor findings entirely; they are documented, perhaps by memorandum.
- Distractor: releasing work papers to outside parties on auditor's own authority.

## 1.9.5 Follow-up activities

### Basics
- **Follow-up**
  - Definition: checking whether agreed corrective actions were actually implemented.
  - Use: closes the loop between findings and real risk reduction.
  - Audit impact: without it, issued reports may change nothing.
- Auditing is an ongoing process, not a one-off report. 📓
- Assess whether the auditee's corrective action was appropriate. 📓
- Auditor should keep a follow-up program for agreed actions.

### Timing and depth
- Timing depends on finding criticality and auditor judgment.
- Depth ranges from simple inquiry about status to performing audit steps.
- Internal auditors may need to test that actions were implemented.
- Example: critical access finding gets retested; a minor policy wording gets an email check.

### Reporting and external auditors
- Communicate follow-up results to appropriate management levels.
- External firm auditors may not follow this process routinely.
- They may perform it if the auditee agrees.

### Exam traps
- Correct logic: verify the corrective action is appropriate, not merely reported done.
- Distractor: accepting management's statement alone, for high-criticality findings.
- Distractor: applying identical follow-up depth to every finding ignores criticality.

## 1.9.6 Types of IS audit reports

### What drives the report
- Driven by engagement type and standards reporting requirements.
- Content depends on scope, objectives and degree of IT examined.
- Format may be set by agreement between audit organization and auditee.
- The audit client may specify content or format.
- Client may belong to a different organization than the auditee.

### Single versus multiple reports
- Most audits produce one report.
- Sometimes a second, confidential security report is needed.
- It carries detailed technical information.
- Purpose: avoid disclosing security risk to unintended parties.
- Example: general report to the board; vulnerability details only to IT security.

### Engagement types
- Review, examination and agreed-upon procedures have similar reporting requirements.
- Each stipulates different reporting requirements and limitations.
- Differences stem from objectives, nature and extent of work, and assurance level.
- **Examination (audit)**
  - Definition: engagement with extensive testing to support a formal opinion.
  - Use: provides stronger evidence for formulating an opinion.
  - Audit impact: highest evidence demand.
- **Agreed-upon procedures**
  - Definition: engagement limited to procedures agreed beforehand with the client.
  - Use: targeted checks with narrower scope.
  - Audit impact: due to limitations, an audit opinion may not be expressed.
- **Review**
  - Definition: engagement using review work with less testing than an examination.
  - Use: lower-assurance evaluation.
  - Audit impact: ⚠ Check: manual gives no separate definition; this is inferred.
- All three include review work; testing is more prevalent in examinations.
- Agreed-upon procedures may include testing.
- Scope may match a review or examination, but is likely narrower for agreed-upon procedures.

### Exam traps
- Correct logic: assurance level follows the engagement type and evidence gathered.
- Distractor: issuing a full opinion after limited agreed-upon procedures.
- Distractor: placing technical vulnerability detail in the widely distributed report.

## Takeaway
- Agree facts with auditee management first, then escalate upward.
- Auditor controls report content; management controls the response and dates.
- Report only material findings upward; document the rest.
- Never trade independence for helpfulness during implementation.
- Follow-up verifies appropriateness of action, scaled to finding criticality.
- Confidential technical detail belongs in a separate restricted report.
